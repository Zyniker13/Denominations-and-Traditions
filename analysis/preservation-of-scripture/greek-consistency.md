# The Greek of the Preservation Claims, Old Testament and New

Part of [The Preservation of Scripture in Scripture and in the Confessions](README.md).

The question here is whether the Greek in which the Septuagint (LXX) states
the Old Testament's claims about God's word is the same Greek in which the
New Testament states its own: whether the two Testaments, read in Greek, say
the same thing in the same words, and where they do not.

The Old Testament Greek is Brenton's edition; the New Testament Greek is the
Textus Receptus, the 1904 Byzantine text and the SBL edition (see
[the editions](../../scripture/preservation/README.md#editions)). Every passage
named in **bold** is printed in full, in Hebrew and Greek, in
[passages.md](../../scripture/preservation/passages.md). Other passages are
cited only for their vocabulary. **[context]** marks a statement resting on
history outside the texts; **[inferred]** marks this study's own reading.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

But the LXX had used one verb for four different Hebrew expressions. |

| God's word *shall not return* / *be set aside* | שׁוּב (Isa 45:23; 55:11); שָׁנָה (Ps 89:35) | **ἀποστρέφω**; **ἀθετέω** (Ps 88:35; Isa 31:2) | ἀθετέω used of **men** rejecting the word (John 12:48; Mark 7:9); of God, ἀμετάθετος (Heb 6:17–18) | **Partly.** The verbs move from God's not retracting to men's setting aside. |
| Scripture *cannot be broken* | no Old Testament equivalent | — | **λυθῆναι** (John 10:35; cf. Matt 5:19); **ἀκυρόω** (Matt 15:6; Mark 7:13) | **New** to the New Testament. |
| *Add not, take not away* | הוֹסִיף / גָּרַע (Deut 4:2; 13:1; Jer 26:2) | **προστίθημι / ἀφαιρέω** (Deut 4:2; 13:1; Prov 24:22l; Eccl 3:14; Jer 33:2) | **ἐπιτίθημι / ἀφαιρέω** (Rev 22:18–19) | **Yes**, with a change of verb for "add". |

| The word is *fulfilled* | מִלֵּא (1 Kgs 8:24); הֵקִים "establish" (1 Kgs 8:20; Neh 9:8); בִּצַּע (Lam 2:17) | **πληρόω** (1 Kgs 8:24; 2 Chr 36:21); **συντελέω** (Lam 2:17); **πιστόω** (2 Kgdms 7:25; 1 Kgs 8:26) | **πληρόω** (Matt 26:54; Acts 1:16); **τελέω** (Luke 18:31; 22:37; Rev 10:7; 17:17); **δεῖ** "must" | **Yes**, and the New Testament adds necessity. |
| The teaching is *delivered* and *kept as a deposit* | — | **παραθήκη / παρακαταθήκη**, a deposit in law (Exod 22:7; Lev 5:21) | **παραδίδωμι** (Luke 1:2; 1 Cor 15:3), **παραθήκη** (1 Tim 6:20; 2 Tim 1:14), **κατέχω, κρατέω** (1 Cor 15:2; 2 Thess 2:15) | **New** as applied to God's word; the deposit term comes from the LXX's law. |
| *For ever* | לְעוֹלָם, לָעַד | **εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα** | **εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα** | **Yes.** |

**[inferred]** Read in Greek, the two Testaments speak of the word's
permanence in one vocabulary, and the New Testament plainly writes in the
Septuagint's idiom. The consistency is not complete: the New Testament adds
a claim the Old does not make in so many words ("the scripture cannot be
broken"), drops the Psalms' image of refined metal, and never once has God
as the subject of a verb of preserving with his word or a written text as
its object. Where the New Testament speaks of the word being kept, the
keepers are people: it is "delivered", held as a "deposit", "held fast".
The Greek of both Testaments has words for copying (ἀπογράφω, Prov 22:20;
τὸ δευτερονόμιον, Deut 17:18) and for storing a document so that it lasts
(Jer 39:14, ἵνα διαμείνῃ), but none is used in a promise that God will keep
the text of his word.

## 1. "Abides for ever": μένω and διαμένω

The Septuagint renders four different Hebrew expressions with one Greek verb:

| Passage | Hebrew | LXX |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Isa 40:8** | וּדְבַר־אֱלֹהֵינוּ **יָקוּם** לְעוֹלָם "shall stand (arise)" | τὸ δὲ ῥῆμα τοῦ θεοῦ ἡμῶν **μένει** εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα |
| **Ps 33:11** (LXX 32:11) | עֲצַת יְהוָה לְעוֹלָם **תַּעֲמֹד** "stands" | ἡ δὲ βουλὴ τοῦ κυρίου εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα **μένει** |
| **Ps 117:2** (LXX 116:2) | וֶאֱמֶת־יְהוָה לְעוֹלָם (no verb) | ἡ ἀλήθεια τοῦ κυρίου **μένει** εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα |
| **Ps 119:89** (LXX 118:89) | דְּבָרְךָ **נִצָּב** בַּשָּׁמָיִם "stands firm, is stationed" | ὁ λόγος σου **διαμένει** ἐν τῷ οὐρανῷ |
| **Ps 19:9** (MT 19:10; LXX 18:10) | **עוֹמֶדֶת** לָעַד "standing for ever" | **διαμένων** εἰς αἰῶνα αἰῶνος |

The Greek Psalter uses the same formula, μένει εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα, of God's
righteousness (Ps 110:3; 111:3, 9) and his praise (110:10).

The New Testament takes the formula over unchanged:

- **1 Pet 1:23, 25**: λόγου ζῶντος θεοῦ καὶ **μένοντος** (εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα in the
  Textus Receptus and Byzantine text) … τὸ δὲ ῥῆμα κυρίου **μένει εἰς τὸν
  αἰῶνα**, quoting Isaiah 40:8 in the LXX's wording.
- 2 Cor 9:9 quotes Ps 111:9 LXX, ἡ δικαιοσύνη αὐτοῦ **μένει εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα**.
- John 12:34: "we have heard out of the law that the Christ **abideth for
  ever** (μένει εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα)."
- 1 John 2:17: "he that doeth the will of God **abideth for ever**."
- διαμένω: Gal 2:5, "that the truth of the gospel might **continue** with
  you"; Heb 1:11, quoting Ps 101:27 LXX, "thou **remainest**".

**Assessment.** Consistent. The New Testament's language of the abiding word
is the Septuagint's. **[inferred]** Some of the apparent unity of the
"enduring word" theme across the Old Testament is a product of the Greek
translation: the Hebrew says the word "stands" or "arises" (Isaiah), the
counsel "stands" (Psalm 33), the word "is stationed" (Psalm 119), and the
truth simply "is for ever" (Psalm 117), and the Greek makes all of them
"abide". A reader who knows the Old Testament through the LXX, as 1 Peter's
readers did, hears one claim; a reader of the Hebrew hears several related
ones.

## 2. "Shall not pass away": παρέρχομαι

The New Testament's strongest permanence sayings use παρέρχομαι:

- **Matt 24:35; Mark 13:31; Luke 21:33**: ὁ οὐρανὸς καὶ ἡ γῆ
  **παρελεύσονται**, οἱ δὲ λόγοι μου **οὐ μὴ παρέλθωσι**.
- **Matt 5:18**: ἕως ἂν **παρέλθῃ** ὁ οὐρανὸς καὶ ἡ γῆ, ἰῶτα ἓν ἢ μία κεραία
  **οὐ μὴ παρέλθῃ** ἀπὸ τοῦ νόμου.
- **Luke 16:17**: εὐκοπώτερον … τὸν οὐρανὸν καὶ τὴν γῆν **παρελθεῖν**.

On the Greek of Matthew 5:18 itself (ἰῶτα, κεραία, and the "therefore" of
verse 19), see
[preservation-hebrew-and-greek.md §1.1](../preservation-hebrew-and-greek.md#11-matthew-518-read-in-the-greek):
what does not "pass away" there is the binding force of the least
commandment, not the accuracy of copies.

None of the Old Testament *word* passages in the LXX uses this verb. Its
closest Old Testament uses are two:

- **Ps 148:6** (LXX): of the heavens and their hosts, ἔστησεν αὐτὰ εἰς τὸν
  αἰῶνα … πρόσταγμα ἔθετο, καὶ **οὐ παρελεύσεται**, "he made an ordinance,
  and it shall not pass away" (Hebrew וְלֹא יַעֲבוֹר).
- **Dan 7:14** (Theodotion, Brenton's text): of the Son of Man, ἡ ἐξουσία
  αὐτοῦ ἐξουσία αἰώνιος, ἥτις **οὐ παρελεύσεται**, "his dominion is an
  everlasting dominion, which shall not pass away" (Aramaic דִּי־לָא יֶעְדֵּה).

**[inferred]** Both bear on the saying in the Olivet Discourse. In Psalm 148
it is the ordinance of the heavens that does not pass; Jesus says the heavens
themselves will pass and transfers the "not passing" to his own words.
Jeremiah makes the fixed order of heaven the measure of God's faithfulness to
Israel (Jer 31:35–36; LXX 38:35–37); Jesus makes his words outlast that
measure. In Matthew and Mark the saying comes a few verses after Jesus has
quoted Daniel 7:13, the Son of Man "coming in the clouds of heaven" (Matt
24:30; Mark 13:26); the Greek of Daniel 7:14 says of that Son of Man's
dominion exactly what Jesus says of his words.

James uses the same verb with the image of Isaiah 40: the rich man "as the
flower of the grass shall **pass away**" (ὡς ἄνθος χόρτου **παρελεύσεται**, Jas
1:10), and "the flower thereof **fell**" (τὸ ἄνθος αὐτοῦ **ἐξέπεσε**, 1:11),
the LXX's verb in Isaiah 40:7. 2 Pet 3:10 says the heavens "shall pass away"
(παρελεύσονται).

**Assessment.** Consistent in vocabulary, and deliberate in its use of it.
The New Testament's "shall not pass away" has no exact Old Testament
counterpart *about the word*; it takes a verb the Greek Old Testament used of
the stability of creation and of the Son of Man's kingdom and applies it to
Jesus's words, while denying it of creation.

## 3. "Not one word fell": πίπτω and its compounds

The historical books state God's faithfulness in the Hebrew idiom "not a word
fell" (לֹא־נָפַל דָּבָר). The LXX keeps the image in most places but not all:

| Passage | Hebrew | LXX |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Josh 21:45** | לֹא־נָפַל דָּבָר | οὐ **διέπεσεν** |
| **Josh 23:14** | לֹא־נָפַל דָּבָר אֶחָד (twice) | οὐκ **ἔπεσεν** εἷς λόγος … οὐ **διεφώνησεν** |
| **1 Sam 3:19** | וְלֹא־הִפִּיל "he let none fall" | οὐκ **ἔπεσεν** |
| **1 Kgs 8:56** | לֹא־נָפַל דָּבָר אֶחָד | οὐ **διεφώνησε** λόγος εἷς "not one word was discordant" |
| **2 Kgs 10:10** | לֹא יִפֹּל … אַרְצָה | οὐ **πεσεῖται** … εἰς τὴν γῆν |

The New Testament keeps the idiom:

- **Luke 16:17**: "easier for heaven and earth to pass, than one tittle of
  the law to **fall**" (μίαν κεραίαν **πεσεῖν**; KJV "fail").
- **Rom 9:6**: "not as though the word of God **hath fallen**" (ἐκπέπτωκεν;
  KJV "taken none effect").
- 1 Cor 13:8: "charity never **faileth**" (ἐκπίπτει in the Textus Receptus;
  πίπτει in the critical text).

**[inferred]** In the Greek, the word that does not "fall" is set against
the flower that does: Isa 40:7 LXX τὸ ἄνθος **ἐξέπεσε**, quoted in 1 Pet 1:24
and echoed in Jas 1:11. Paul's ἐκπέπτωκεν in Romans 9:6 uses the same verb.

**Assessment.** Consistent. The Hebrew idiom passes through the LXX into the
New Testament, though the LXX itself sometimes replaces it with a different
image (διαφωνέω, "to be out of tune, discordant").

## 4. "Shall not return", "will not set aside": ἀποστρέφω and ἀθετέω

| Passage | Hebrew | LXX |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Isa 45:23** | דָּבָר וְלֹא **יָשׁוּב** "a word, and it shall not return" | οἱ λόγοι μου οὐκ **ἀποστραφήσονται** |
| **Isa 55:11** | לֹא־**יָשׁוּב** אֵלַי רֵיקָם "shall not return to me empty" | οὐ μὴ **ἀποστραφῇ** |
| **Ps 89:34** (MT 89:35; LXX 88:35) | לֹא **אֲשַׁנֶּה** "I will not alter" | οὐ μὴ **ἀθετήσω** "I will not set aside" |
| **Isa 31:2** | לֹא **הֵסִיר** "has not removed" | οὐ μὴ **ἀθετηθῇ** "shall not be set aside" |

In the New Testament ἀθετέω is used of men setting God's word aside, not of
God declining to:

- **John 12:48**: "he that **rejecteth** me (ὁ ἀθετῶν ἐμέ) … the word that I
  have spoken, the same shall judge him."
- Mark 7:9: "full well ye **reject** (ἀθετεῖτε) the commandment of God, that
  ye may keep your own tradition."
- Gal 3:15: no one **disannuls** (ἀθετεῖ) even a man's confirmed covenant.

For God's not changing his word the New Testament uses other terms: "the
**immutability** (τὸ ἀμετάθετον) of his counsel" and "two **immutable**
things" (Heb 6:17–18); "the gifts and calling of God are **without
repentance**" (ἀμεταμέλητα, Rom 11:29).

**Assessment.** Partly consistent. The idea that God does not retract what he
has spoken is carried over, but not the Isaianic image of the word that
"returns", and ἀθετέω changes subject.

## 5. "Cannot be broken", "made of none effect": λύω and ἀκυρόω

These are New Testament formulations without an Old Testament equivalent:

- **John 10:35**: οὐ δύναται **λυθῆναι** ἡ γραφή, "the scripture cannot be
  broken (loosed, annulled)."
- **Matt 5:19**: whoever **breaks** (λύσῃ) one of the least commandments;
  5:17, "I came not to **destroy** (καταλῦσαι) but to fulfil."
- **Matt 15:6; Mark 7:13**: ye have **made of none effect** (ἠκυρώσατε,
  ἀκυροῦντες) the word (or commandment) of God by your tradition.
- Gal 3:17: the law, coming 430 years after, **cannot disannul** (οὐκ ἀκυροῖ)
  the covenant confirmed before.

**[inferred]** These verbs speak of annulling authority, not of destroying
a text. John 10:35 says a written sentence of Scripture cannot be annulled,
which is the New Testament's most direct statement about *Scripture* (γραφή)
rather than about God's word in general; Matthew 15:6 shows that the word can
nonetheless be annulled *in practice* by men.

## 6. "Add not, take not away": προστίθημι, ἐπιτίθημι, ἀφαιρέω

| Passage | Greek |
| --- | --- |
| **Deut 4:2** | οὐ **προσθήσετε** πρὸς τὸ ῥῆμα … καὶ οὐκ **ἀφελεῖτε** ἀπ' αὐτοῦ |
| **Deut 12:32** (MT, LXX 13:1) | οὐ **προσθήσεις** ἐπ' αὐτό, οὐδὲ **ἀφελεῖς** ἀπ' αὐτοῦ |
| **Prov 30:6** (Brenton 24:22l) | μὴ **προσθῇς** τοῖς λόγοις αὐτοῦ |
| Eccl 3:14 (of God's work, not his word) | ἐπ' αὐτῷ οὐκ ἔστι **προσθεῖναι**, καὶ ἀπ' αὐτοῦ οὐκ ἔστιν **ἀφελεῖν** |
| **Jer 26:2** (LXX 33:2) | μὴ **ἀφέλῃς** ῥῆμα |
| **Jer 36:32** (LXX 43:32) | καὶ ἔτι **προσετέθησαν** αὐτῷ λόγοι πλείονες "and many more words were added to it" |
| **Rev 22:18–19** | ἐάν τις **ἐπιθῇ** (TR ἐπιτιθῇ) … **ἐπιθήσει** ὁ θεὸς … ἐάν τις **ἀφέλῃ** (TR ἀφαιρῇ) … **ἀφελεῖ** (TR ἀφαιρήσει) ὁ θεός |

**Assessment.** Consistent. Revelation uses the Deuteronomic formula with
ἀφαιρέω unchanged and a different verb for "add" (ἐπιτίθημι, "lay upon"),
which makes the punishment fit the crime: whoever "lays on" words, God will
"lay on" plagues. **[inferred]** The LXX of Jeremiah 36 uses the same verb
of the words God himself had *added* to the restored scroll, which shows that
the formula forbids human, not divine, addition.

## 7. "Pure", "tried": πυρόω and ἁγνός, and what replaces them

The Psalms and Proverbs describe God's word as metal refined in the fire:

| Passage | Hebrew | LXX |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ps 12:6** (MT 12:7; LXX 11:7) | אִמֲרוֹת **טְהֹרוֹת** … כֶּסֶף **צָרוּף** | λόγια **ἁγνά**· ἀργύριον **πεπυρωμένον** |
| **Ps 18:30** (MT 18:31; LXX 17:31) | אִמְרַת יְהוָה **צְרוּפָה** | τὰ λόγια κυρίου **πεπυρωμένα** |
| **2 Sam 22:31** | the same | τὸ ῥῆμα κυρίου κραταιὸν **πεπυρωμένον** |
| **Ps 119:140** (LXX 118:140) | **צְרוּפָה** אִמְרָתְךָ | **πεπυρωμένον** τὸ λόγιόν σου |
| **Prov 30:5** (Brenton 24:22k) | כָּל־אִמְרַת אֱלוֹהַּ **צְרוּפָה** | πάντες λόγοι θεοῦ **πεπυρωμένοι** |
| **Ps 19:9** (MT 19:10; LXX 18:10) | יִרְאַת יְהוָה **טְהוֹרָה** | ὁ φόβος κυρίου **ἁγνός** |

The New Testament never applies πυρόω or ἁγνός to God's word. (It uses
πεπυρωμένος only literally, of gold and brass, Rev 1:15; 3:18.) It describes
the word's reliability in other terms:

- **John 17:17**: "thy word is **truth**" (ἀλήθεια).
- **Rev 22:6**: "these sayings are **faithful and true**" (πιστοὶ καὶ
  ἀληθινοί); the "faithful sayings" (πιστὸς ὁ λόγος) of the Pastoral
  Epistles (1 Tim 1:15 and elsewhere).
- **1 Pet 2:2**: "the **sincere** milk of the word" (ἄδολον, "unadulterated"),
  directly after Peter's quotation of Isaiah 40.

**Assessment.** Not consistent in vocabulary. The New Testament keeps the
claim (God's word is trustworthy and free of falsehood) but not the image
(metal refined seven times). **[inferred]** Its one word for freedom from
adulteration, ἄδολος, is the negative of the word for corrupting it (δολόω,
2 Cor 4:2), which shows the New Testament thinking of the word's purity in
terms of the honesty of those who handle it.

## 8. "Keep", "preserve": φυλάσσω and τηρέω

The passage most often cited for the preservation of the words, **Psalm
12:7** (MT 12:8; LXX 11:8), is in the Greek a prayer for the preservation of
people: σὺ κύριε **φυλάξεις ἡμᾶς** καὶ **διατηρήσεις ἡμᾶς**, "thou shalt keep
*us* and preserve *us*." The Hebrew has "them" and "him" (see
[passages.md](../../scripture/preservation/passages.md#ps-12-6)).

The New Testament's use of the same verbs follows the Greek pattern exactly:

- **God keeps people.** "Holy Father, **keep** them (τήρησον αὐτούς) … I
  **kept** them … I **guarded** them (ἐφύλαξα)" (John 17:11–12, 15); "him that
  is able to **keep** you (φυλάξαι ὑμᾶς) from falling" (Jude 24); "who are
  **kept** (φρουρουμένους) by the power of God" (1 Pet 1:5).
- **People keep the word.** "If a man **keep** my saying" (John 8:51; 14:23);
  "they have **kept** thy word" (John 17:6); "blessed are they that hear the
  word of God, and **keep** it" (Luke 11:28, φυλάσσοντες); "thou hast **kept**
  my word" (Rev 3:8); "blessed is he that **keepeth** the sayings of the
  prophecy of this book" (Rev 22:7).

Two further uses complete the picture:

- **God keeps a promise.** Solomon: "thou … hast kept (ἐφύλαξας) with thy
  servant David my father that thou promisedst him: thou spakest also with
  thy mouth, and hast fulfilled it with thine hand" (**1 Kgs 8:24**). God
  "keeps" what he said by doing it, as in "keeping covenant" (Deut 7:9,
  ὁ φυλάσσων διαθήκην). The object is a promise, and keeping it is its
  fulfilment.
- **God keeps a deposit.** "He is able to keep (φυλάξαι) that which I have
  committed unto him (τὴν παραθήκην μου)" (2 Tim 1:12). Two verses later
  Timothy is told to keep "that good thing which was committed unto thee"
  (τὴν καλὴν παραθήκην φύλαξον, 1:14). **[context]** 1:12 is usually read of
  Paul's life or salvation entrusted to God, 1:14 of the teaching entrusted
  to Timothy. See §13.

**Assessment.** Consistent with the LXX's reading of Psalm 12, and
inconsistent with the reading in which God keeps the words. **[inferred]** In
neither Testament's Greek is God the subject of a verb of keeping with his
words or a text as its object, except in the sense of keeping a promise by
performing it. Where the New Testament says the word is kept,
it is kept by believers, in obedience.

## 9. The nouns: ῥῆμα, λόγος, λόγια, γραφή

The LXX renders the Hebrew דָּבָר and אִמְרָה indifferently by ῥῆμα, λόγος and
λόγιον, and the New Testament is just as free:

- Isaiah 40:8 LXX has **ῥῆμα**; 1 Peter keeps ῥῆμα in the quotation (1:25)
  but uses **λόγος** for the same word two verses earlier (1:23).
- Psalm 119:89 LXX has **λόγος**; Jesus's "my **words** (λόγοι)" (Matt 24:35)
  and "the **word** (λόγος) that I have spoken" (John 12:48), but "my
  **words** (ῥήματα)" in John 6:63 and 12:48a.
- **λόγια**, "oracles", is the Greek Psalter's word for the pure words of Ps
  11:7 and 17:31 and the Psalm 118 refrain. Paul uses it for what was
  entrusted to the Jews (**Rom 3:2**, τὰ λόγια τοῦ θεοῦ), Stephen for what
  Moses received at Sinai (**Acts 7:38**, λόγια ζῶντα), and Hebrews and 1
  Peter for God's revelation (Heb 5:12; 1 Pet 4:11).
- **γραφή**, "scripture", is never the subject of a preservation claim in
  the LXX. In the New Testament it is the subject of John 10:35 ("cannot be
  broken") and 2 Tim 3:16 ("given by inspiration").

**Assessment.** Consistent: neither Testament distinguishes the word spoken
from the word written by its choice of noun. **[inferred]** The single claim
whose grammatical subject is "scripture" as such is John 10:35.

## 10. How the New Testament handles the Greek of the Old

When the New Testament quotes the Septuagint's own preservation passages, it
does not treat their Greek wording as fixed:

- **1 Pet 1:24–25** quotes **Isa 40:6–8** in the LXX's short form, with its
  "glory of man" and its "abides", but changes "the word **of our God**" (τοῦ
  θεοῦ ἡμῶν) to "the word **of the Lord**" (κυρίου), in all three editions,
  and adds "this is the word which by the gospel is preached unto you."
- **Matt 4:4** quotes **Deut 8:3** in the LXX's form, with "every **word**"
  (ῥήματι), a noun the Hebrew does not have.
- **Heb 10:37** reads **Hab 2:3** as the LXX does, "the coming one will come",
  of a person, and adds the article (ὁ ἐρχόμενος).
- **Rom 11:26–27** quotes **Isa 59:20–21** from the LXX ("this is my covenant
  with them"), but stops before the promise that the words "shall not depart
  out of thy mouth".

**[inferred]** The apostles quote the Greek Old Testament's statements of the
word's permanence while adapting their wording to the point being made. They
evidently located the permanence in what God said, not in the precise
wording of the translation they quoted.

## 11. The New Testament's text of its own claims

The New Testament's own preservation passages differ among the printed Greek
texts. All of the differences are recorded in
[passages.md](../../scripture/preservation/passages.md); the ones that touch
the claim are these:

| Passage | Textus Receptus | Byzantine (1904) | SBL | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1 Pet 1:23** | μένοντος **εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα** | the same | μένοντος (no "for ever") | The Textus Receptus has "for ever" twice; SBL only in 1:25 |
| **1 Pet 1:24** | δόξα **ἀνθρώπου** (= LXX) | the same | δόξα **αὐτῆς** (≈ Hebrew "its") | The editions differ on whether Peter's quotation agrees with the Greek or the Hebrew of Isaiah |
| **Mark 13:31; Luke 21:33** | οὐ μὴ **παρέλθωσι** | the same | οὐ μὴ **παρελεύσονται** | Subjunctive or future; same sense |
| **Matt 15:6** | τὴν **ἐντολὴν** τοῦ θεοῦ | the same | τὸν **λόγον** τοῦ θεοῦ | "Commandment" or "word" made void |
| **Rev 22:19** | ἀπὸ **βίβλου** τῆς ζωῆς | ἀπὸ τοῦ **ξύλου** τῆς ζωῆς | ἀπὸ τοῦ **ξύλου** τῆς ζωῆς | "Book of life" or "tree of life"; see below |

**[context]** The Revelation 22:19 difference is the most significant
because of where it stands. Erasmus had a single Greek manuscript of
Revelation for his first edition (1516), and it lacked the last six verses;
he translated them back into Greek from the Latin Vulgate, and the Latin text
he used read *libro vitae*, "book of life". The reading "book of life" entered the
Textus Receptus that way and has little or no support in Greek manuscripts.
The curse on whoever "shall take away from the words of the book of this
prophecy" is therefore transmitted, in the Textus Receptus, in a form that
itself departs from the Greek manuscript tradition.

Among the passages added in the second search, these differences touch the
claim:

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Rom 10:17** | ῥήματος **Θεοῦ** | the same | ῥήματος **Χριστοῦ** | "The word of God" or "of Christ" |
| **Rev 17:17** | τελεσθῇ **τὰ ῥήματα** τοῦ Θεοῦ | τελεσθῶσιν **οἱ λόγοι** | **τελεσθήσονται** οἱ λόγοι | Subjunctive or future; same sense |
| **Rev 10:7** | **τελεσθῇ** "should be finished" | **ἐτελέσθη** "is finished" | the same as Byzantine | Prospect or accomplished fact |
| **1 Cor 14:37** | τοῦ Κυρίου εἰσὶν **ἐντολαί** | the same | κυρίου **ἐστίν** | "Are the commandments of the Lord" or "is of the Lord" |
| **2 Pet 1:12** | **οὐκ ἀμελήσω** "I will not be negligent" | the same | **μελλήσω** "I will be ready" | The same purpose, to keep the readers in remembrance |

**[inferred]** The New Testament's preservation claims are themselves
transmitted the way the rest of the New Testament is: in substance without
loss, in wording with variation, none of which here changes what is claimed.

## 12. "Fulfilled", "must be": πληρόω, τελέω, πιστόω and δεῖ

The Old Testament's fulfilment idiom is mostly negative (the word did not
"fall", did not "return"; §3, §4). Its positive form is rarer, and the
Septuagint uses several verbs for it:

| Passage | Hebrew | LXX |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1 Kgs 8:24** | וּבְיָדְךָ **מִלֵּאתָ** "with thine hand hast fulfilled it" | ἐν χερσί σου **ἐπλήρωσας** |
| **1 Kgs 8:20; Neh 9:8** | וַיָּקֶם / וַתָּקֶם "hath performed (raised up)" | **ἀνέστησε** (1 Kgs 8:20); **ἔστησας** (2 Esd 19:8) |
| **2 Sam 7:25; 1 Chr 17:23; 1 Kgs 8:26** | הָקֵם "establish"; יֵאָמֵן "be confirmed" | **πίστωσον**, **πιστωθήτω** "be made sure" |
| **Lam 2:17** | בִּצַּע אֶמְרָתוֹ "he hath finished his word" | **συνετέλεσε** ῥῆμα αὐτοῦ |
| **Dan 9:2** | לְמַלֹּאות "to accomplish" (seventy years) | εἰς **συμπλήρωσιν** (Theodotion) |
| 2 Chr 36:21–22 | לְמַלֹּאות דְּבַר־יְהוָה "to fulfil the word of the LORD" | **πληρωθῆναι** λόγον Κυρίου |

The New Testament has the same two verbs, and adds necessity:

- **πληρόω**: "how then shall the scriptures be **fulfilled** (πληρωθῶσιν),
  that thus it **must be** (δεῖ γενέσθαι)?" (**Matt 26:54**); "that the
  scriptures of the prophets might be fulfilled" (26:56; Mark 14:49); "this
  scripture **must needs** (ἔδει) have been fulfilled" (**Acts 1:16**).
- **τελέω**: "all things that are written … shall be **accomplished**
  (τελεσθήσεται)" (**Luke 18:31**); "this that is written **must** yet be
  accomplished in me (δεῖ τελεσθῆναι)" (22:37); "the mystery of God should
  be **finished**" (**Rev 10:7**); "until the words of God shall be
  **fulfilled**" (17:17).
- The "yes" of 2 Cor 1:20 (ὅσαι γὰρ ἐπαγγελίαι θεοῦ, ἐν αὐτῷ τὸ ναί) is the
  New Testament's form of the Old Testament's "be confirmed" (יֵאָמֵן,
  "amen"): "and in him Amen".

**Assessment.** Consistent. The Septuagint's πληρόω and (συν)τελέω pass into
the New Testament with their meaning. **[inferred]** What the New Testament
adds is δεῖ, "it must": fulfilment is no longer only observed afterwards
("not one thing hath failed") but stated beforehand as necessary because it
is written.

## 13. "Delivered", "the deposit", "hold fast": the vocabulary of handing on

The New Testament describes the transmission of the apostles' teaching in a
vocabulary the Old Testament does not apply to God's word:

- **παραδίδωμι / παραλαμβάνω**, "deliver" and "receive": "even as they
  **delivered** them unto us, which from the beginning were eyewitnesses"
  (**Luke 1:2**); "I **delivered** unto you … that which I also **received**"
  (**1 Cor 15:3**); "keep the ordinances (παραδόσεις), as I delivered them to
  you" (1 Cor 11:2); "hold the traditions (παραδόσεις) which ye have been
  taught, whether by word, or our epistle" (**2 Thess 2:15**); "the faith
  which was once **delivered** (παραδοθείσῃ) unto the saints" (**Jude 3**).
- **παραθήκη / παρακαταθήκη**, "deposit": "keep that which is committed to
  thy trust" (**1 Tim 6:20**; 2 Tim 1:14); with the verb, "commit
  (παράθου) to faithful men" (**2 Tim 2:2**). In the Septuagint these are
  terms of the law of deposits: goods entrusted to a neighbour, which he must
  return or swear he has not touched (παρακαταθήκη, Exod 22:7, 10;
  παραθήκη, Lev 5:21, 23, in Brenton's numbering; KJV Exod 22:8, 11; Lev
  6:2, 4).
- **κατέχω, κρατέω, ἀντέχομαι**, "hold fast": "if ye **keep in memory**
  (κατέχετε)" (**1 Cor 15:2**); the good ground "**keep** it (κατέχουσιν)"
  (**Luke 8:15**); "**hold** (κρατεῖτε) the traditions" (2 Thess 2:15);
  "**holding fast** (ἀντεχόμενον) the faithful word" (**Titus 1:9**).
- **μένω ἐν**, "abide in": "if ye continue in my word" (**John 8:31**); "let
  that therefore abide in you, which ye have heard from the beginning" (**1
  John 2:24**); "the truth … which dwelleth (μένουσαν) in us, and shall be
  with us for ever" (2 John 2). This is the verb of Isaiah 40:8 (§1), now
  with believers as the place where the word abides.

**Assessment.** **[inferred]** This vocabulary is new as applied to God's
word, but it is not new Greek: the deposit terms are the Septuagint's legal
ones, and they bring with them the obligation to return what was entrusted
intact. The New Testament's picture of preservation through human keepers
is therefore stated in the Septuagint's language, and it places the duty of
faithful keeping on the church's teachers.

## 14. Writing, copying and storing

The Septuagint has the vocabulary for keeping texts, but uses it of human
acts:

- **Copying.** The king "shall write for himself this **deuteronomy**" (τὸ
  δευτερονόμιον, **Deut 17:18**), the Greek's rendering of מִשְׁנֵה, "a
  copy", which gave the book its name; Joshua writes "the deuteronomy" on
  the stones (**Josh 9:2c** LXX). In **Prov 22:20** the reader is told
  "**write them out** (ἀπόγραψαι) for thyself threefold".
- **Storing.** "Put them in an earthen vessel, **that they may continue**
  (ἵνα διαμείνῃ) many days" (**Jer 39:14** LXX = 32:14), of deeds of sale.
  The verb is the one used of God's word "abiding" in heaven (Ps 118:89–91).
- **God's own writing.** "The writing was the writing of God" (ἡ γραφὴ γραφὴ
  Θεοῦ, **Exod 32:16**); "I will write down for him a multitude" (**Hos
  8:12**, καταγράψω).

The New Testament speaks of writing in order to be remembered (**2 Pet
1:15**; 3:1, ὑπομνήσει), of letters being read and passed on (**Col 4:16**;
1 Thess 5:27, ἀναγνωσθῆναι), of the author's own hand as the mark of a
genuine letter (**2 Thess 3:17**), and of "books" and "parchments" (βιβλία,
μεμβράναι, **2 Tim 4:13**).

**Assessment.** **[inferred]** Neither Testament lacks the words for
copying and storing a text; both use them of what people do with writings.
The verb God uses of his word in Psalm 119:89 (διαμένει) is used in
Jeremiah of a document stored in a jar so that it lasts, which shows that
the Greek could have said that God keeps the text of his word in the same
words. It does not.

## Conclusions

1. **The Greek of the two Testaments is consistent on permanence.** μένω and
   διαμένω, εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα, παρέρχομαι, the "falling" idiom and the
   add-or-take-away formula all pass from the Septuagint into the New
   Testament with their meaning intact.
2. **The New Testament intensifies the claim.** "Shall not pass away" is
   applied to Jesus's words, and set beyond the life of heaven and earth,
   in language the Greek Old Testament had used of the ordinance of the
   heavens and of the Son of Man's kingdom. "Cannot be broken" is applied to
   Scripture as such.
3. **The New Testament does not take up the Psalms' purity image** and puts
   truthfulness and freedom from adulteration in its place.
4. **Neither Testament's Greek says that God keeps a text.** The Septuagint
   reads the one possible exception, Psalm 12:7, of people; the New Testament
   follows that pattern consistently. God "keeps" a promise by performing it
   (1 Kgs 8:24), and keeps what is entrusted to him (2 Tim 1:12), but the
   Greek of both Testaments, which has words for copying and storing texts
   (§14), never makes his word's text the object of his keeping.
5. **The New Testament describes the keeping of the word in the language of
   deposit and handing on** (§13): the word is delivered, entrusted, held
   fast and kept by believers and teachers. The deposit terms are the
   Septuagint's legal ones.
6. **The New Testament adds necessity to fulfilment** (§12): the Scriptures
   "must" be fulfilled, where the Old Testament observes afterwards that
   God's word did not fail.
7. **The consistency is partly the Septuagint's work.** It made several
   Hebrew expressions into one Greek one (μένει), and the New Testament read
   the Old Testament through it. To compare the Greek of the two Testaments is
   to compare the New Testament with the Greek translation it largely used,
   not with the Hebrew. The comparison with the Hebrew is in
   [hebrew-and-greek.md](hebrew-and-greek.md) and, at length,
   [preservation-hebrew-and-greek.md](../preservation-hebrew-and-greek.md).
