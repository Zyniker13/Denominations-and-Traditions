# Preserved in Hebrew or in Greek? Scripture's Preservation Claims Tested Against the History of Its Text

Scripture says that the word of God stands forever, that it will not pass
away, and that not one iota or stroke will fall from the Law. This study asks
how strong those claims look when they are rested on the actual history of
the Old Testament text, and in particular whether they are stronger if they
are grounded in the **Hebrew** (the Masoretic Text, "MT") or in the **Greek**
(the Septuagint, "LXX").

It weighs two features of the Hebrew that the question turns on:

1. **Hebrew's history as a language.** It was displaced as the vernacular of
   the Jews first by Aramaic and then, in much of the diaspora, by Greek, and
   ceased to be anyone's mother tongue from late antiquity until its revival
   in the late nineteenth century.
2. **Hebrew's script.** It was written as an *abjad*, consonants only, so
   that a written text could be read fully only with the help of an oral
   reading tradition, until the vowel signs were added in the early Middle
   Ages.

It then weighs the Septuagint on the same terms and compares the two.

The collection and categorization of the preservation passages themselves,
the full comparison of their Hebrew and Greek wording, and the full
cross-reference to the confessions belong to the companion study being
prepared in the companion thread "Preservation claims in Scripture,"
whose passage index is to be published under `scripture/`. This study quotes only the
passages its argument needs. Its cross-references to the confessions are
limited to the confessional claims about the Hebrew and Greek *text*.

Conventions: **[context]** marks a statement that rests on historical or
scholarly evidence rather than on a text in this repository; **[inference]**
marks a conclusion drawn from the evidence rather than stated by a source.
Translations of Hebrew and Greek are the author's own and deliberately
literal. Sources for every quotation and scholarly claim are listed under
[Sources](#sources).

## Summary

**The short answer.** Neither the Hebrew nor the Greek, taken alone, can
carry a claim that one identifiable text has been preserved letter for letter
from the beginning. Each carries a different *part* of the weight, and each
fails at a different point.

- **Grounded in the Hebrew**, the preservation claim is strongest at the level
  of the **consonantal text from about the end of the first century AD
  onward**. From the scrolls of Masada and the Bar Kokhba caves to the Aleppo
  and Leningrad codices, the proto-Masoretic consonants were transmitted with
  a fidelity that has few parallels in ancient literature. The claim is
  weakest in two places: **before** that point, when Hebrew texts of the same
  books circulated in more than one edition side by side; and in the
  **vowels**, which were written down only between about the seventh and
  tenth centuries AD and which rest, for the period before, on an oral
  reading tradition carried by the synagogue.
- **Grounded in the Greek**, the claim is strongest on **determinacy** (Greek
  writes its vowels), **continuity** (Greek never stopped being spoken or
  read), **antiquity of complete copies** (whole Greek Bibles survive from the
  fourth century, five to six hundred years before the oldest complete
  Hebrew Bible), and **apostolic use** (the New Testament quotes it far more
  often than it quotes the Hebrew directly). It is weakest on **originality**
  (it is a translation, and says so), and on **singularity**: there is no
  single "Septuagint," but an Old Greek translation overlaid by Jewish
  revisions, by Origen's Hexapla, and by later Christian recensions.
- **The two features of Hebrew this study was asked to weigh cut less deeply
  than they first appear, but they do cut.** Hebrew stopped being a mother
  tongue, but it never stopped being read, copied, chanted and studied; the
  community that read it aloud was continuous. The abjad leaves real
  ambiguity, but it is bounded: most consonantal strings have only one
  plausible reading in context. The cases that remain are few, but they
  include passages the New Testament itself argues from, and in several of
  them the apostles follow a reading of the consonants that the later
  Masoretic vowels reject (Gen 47:31 in Heb 11:21; Amos 9:12 in Acts 15:17).
- **The Greek does not escape the Hebrew problem; it relocates it.** The LXX
  is a translation of a Hebrew text. Where it differs substantially from the
  MT, the Dead Sea Scrolls have repeatedly shown that it often translated a
  *different Hebrew edition* rather than mistranslating the MT (Jeremiah,
  Samuel, Deut 32). To ground preservation in the Greek is therefore, in large
  part, to ground it in that other Hebrew edition, at one remove.
- **Scripture's own wording favours neither.** No preservation passage names
  a language, a script, a manuscript line, or a community of copyists. The
  enduring word is located "in the heavens" (Ps 119:89), "in your mouth and
  the mouth of your offspring" (Isa 59:21), in its accomplishment (Isa 55:11;
  Matt 5:18), and in "the word that was preached to you" (1 Pet 1:25). The New
  Testament quotes the Hebrew and the Greek as it suits, carries Jesus' own
  words almost entirely in translation, and transmits its own preservation
  sayings with minor variants. **[inference]** Read on their own terms, the
  passages promise the permanence and efficacy of what God has said, not the
  inerrant copying of one text-form.
- **For the confessions**: the Westminster Confession's "kept pure in all
  ages" (1.8) is defensible for the Hebrew consonantal text as a claim about
  the substance of the text, and not as a claim that every manuscript or
  edition is free of error. The Helvetic Consensus's claim that the vowel
  points are inspired (Canon II) is contradicted by the history of the points
  as to the signs themselves and survives only in its fallback form, "the
  power of the points," which is a claim for an oral tradition. Its refusal
  to let the LXX correct the Hebrew (Canon III) is in tension with the New
  Testament's own practice.

## 1. What the preservation claims actually say

The passages below are the core of what this study tests. The full catalogue
is the companion study's; these are given in the Hebrew of the Leningrad
Codex (as edited in the Westminster Leningrad Codex), the Greek of the LXX,
and the Greek of the New Testament where it quotes them.

Three things follow before any history is considered.

1. **No passage names a medium.** None says "in Hebrew," "in this script,"
   "in these copies," or "by these copyists." The places the passages do name
   are heaven, the mouth of God's people, the accomplishment of God's purpose,
   and the preached gospel. **[inference]**
2. **Only one passage is pitched at the level of letters**, Matt 5:18, and
   its horizon is fulfillment ("until all is accomplished"), not transmission.
   It is also the one passage whose wording presupposes a particular script
   (see [3.1](#31-two-scripts-not-one)). The Westminster Confession cites it,
   and only it, as the proof of "kept pure in all ages" (WCF 1.8, proof [a]).
3. **The most famous "preservation" text in popular debate, Ps 12:7, is about
   people in the Greek** and is ambiguous in the Hebrew. Its role in the
   argument depends on how one reads unpointed consonants
   ([3.3](#33-where-the-consonants-allow-more-than-one-reading)).

## 2. The Hebrew: a language displaced

### 2.1 Aramaic

Aramaic was the diplomatic language of the Near East by the late eighth
century BC. In 701 BC Judah's officials ask the Assyrian envoy to speak
Aramaic, which they understand, and not "Judean," which the people on the
wall understand (2 Kgs 18:26). After the exile the position is reversed.
Nehemiah finds Jewish children who speak "the language of Ashdod" and cannot
speak "Judean" (Neh 13:24).

The Bible's own text records the shift.

- **Parts of the Old Testament are written in Aramaic**: Ezra 4:8–6:18 and
  7:12–26, Dan 2:4b–7:28, Jer 10:11, and two words in Gen 31:47. Jerome
  noted it ([Preface to Daniel](../theologians/early-church/jerome/vulgate-prefaces.md#daniel):
  Daniel and Ezra "were written in Hebrew letters, but in the Chaldee
  language"). The "Old Testament in Hebrew" of WCF 1.8 is, strictly, the Old
  Testament in Hebrew and Aramaic.
- **Neh 8:8** says that when Ezra's Law was read, the Levites read "clearly"
  (*mephorash*) "and gave the sense." The Talmud takes *mephorash* to mean
  the Aramaic translation, and "gave the sense" to mean the division into
  verses and the cantillation, or, on another view, the traditions that fix
  the vocalization (b. Nedarim 37b). Whatever the verse originally meant, the
  rabbis read it as the origin of the practice they knew: Scripture read in
  Hebrew and immediately rendered into Aramaic.
- **The Targums** (Aramaic translations read in the synagogue) are attested
  at Qumran (11QtgJob, 4QtgLev) and fully developed in Onkelos and Jonathan.
  **[context]**

### 2.2 Greek

From Alexander onward Greek became the common language of the eastern
Mediterranean, and of Jewish communities across it. The LXX itself is the
first evidence: the Law was translated in Alexandria in the third century BC
because the Jews there needed it in Greek **[context]**. The grandson of Ben
Sira, translating his grandfather's book in Egypt about 132 BC, apologizes
for the result in his prologue:

> For what is said in Hebrew does not have the same force when it is
> translated into another language; and not only these, but the Law itself,
> and the Prophets, and the rest of the books differ not a little when they
> are read in their own language.

Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BC–AD 50), the most learned Jewish writer of the
diaspora, worked from the Greek and treated its translators as "prophets and
priests of the mysteries"
([*Life of Moses* II.vii](../theologians/jewish-antiquity/philo/life-of-moses.md#book-ii-chapter-vii)).
In Judea itself the evidence is trilingual: the letters from the Bar Kokhba
revolt (AD 132–135) are written in Hebrew, Aramaic and Greek. **[context]**

### 2.3 The end of spoken Hebrew, and what did not end

Hebrew continued as a spoken language in Judea into the second century AD;
the Mishnah (c. AD 200) is written in a living Hebrew, distinct from biblical
Hebrew. After the Bar Kokhba revolt the Jewish population of Judea was
destroyed or dispersed, and by the third or fourth century Hebrew had
ceased to be anyone's mother tongue. It was not again a language of the
nursery and the street until its revival in Palestine in the late nineteenth
and early twentieth centuries. **[context]** (Sáenz-Badillos; Schniedewind.)

The period of "death" is therefore roughly sixteen to seventeen centuries.
But "dead" needs qualifying for the purposes of this question. Throughout
those centuries Hebrew was:

- **read aloud** every Sabbath in every synagogue, by a cantillated reading
  tradition handed down from teacher to pupil;
- **copied** by professional scribes under detailed rules;
- **written** as a language of law, liturgy, poetry, correspondence and
  scholarship (the Mishnah, the liturgical poetry of the *piyyut*, the
  medieval grammarians, commentators and poets).

**[inference]** For textual preservation the relevant question is not
whether anyone spoke Hebrew at home, but whether there was an unbroken
community that could read the text aloud correctly and copy it accurately.
There was. Hebrew's history is more like Latin's in the medieval West than
like Hittite's: a language without native speakers, but never without
readers.

### 2.4 What the language's history did cost

Three real losses follow from Hebrew's long period without native speakers,
and the first two began **before** Christ.

1. **Vocabulary.** About 1,500 words occur only once in the Hebrew Bible,
   and for some hundreds of these there is no related form elsewhere in the
   Bible to anchor their meaning (Greenspahn). Their meanings had to be
   carried by tradition or, in modern times, recovered from Ugaritic,
   Akkadian, Arabic and other related languages. The LXX translators of the
   third and second centuries BC already did not know some of these words,
   and guessed, paraphrased or transliterated them. **[context]**
2. **Spelling.** The rabbis themselves admitted that some knowledge of the
   text had slipped. The Talmud says that the early scribes were called
   *soferim*, "counters," because they counted the letters of the Torah,
   and that the *vav* of *gaḥon* (Lev 11:42) was its middle letter. When a
   fourth-century sage asked which half that *vav* belonged to, and it was
   proposed to settle the matter by counting a scroll, the answer was that
   it could not be done: the early scribes were experts in which words were
   spelled with or without vowel letters, and "we are not" (b. Kiddushin 30a).
3. **The reading tradition was carried by people for whom Hebrew was not a
   first language.** The Masoretes who wrote down the vowels in the seventh to
   tenth centuries spoke Aramaic or Arabic. The most thorough modern study of
   the Tiberian tradition concludes that it preserves an ancient reading with
   remarkable continuity, but also that it was not static and shows
   developments of its own (Khan). **[context]**

## 3. The Hebrew: a script without vowels

### 3.1 Two scripts, not one

The Hebrew Bible was first written in the **Old Hebrew** (paleo-Hebrew)
script, a descendant of the Phoenician alphabet. After the exile the Jews
adopted the **square script** of Aramaic, which is the script of every
Hebrew Bible printed today. The Talmud records the change and assigns it to
Ezra:

> Initially the Torah was given to Israel in the Hebrew script and the holy
> tongue; it was given to them again in the days of Ezra in the "Assyrian"
> script and the Aramaic tongue. Israel chose the Assyrian script and the
> holy tongue, and left the Hebrew script and the Aramaic tongue to the
> commoners. (b. Sanhedrin 21b, paraphrased)

Both scripts appear at Qumran, where some biblical scrolls are written
wholly in paleo-Hebrew and some square-script scrolls write the divine name
in paleo-Hebrew letters. The Samaritans kept a form of the old script.
**[context]**

**This bears directly on Matt 5:18.** The *iota* of "one iota or one stroke"
stands for the Hebrew *yod*, and the saying works because *yod* is the
smallest letter. That is true of the square script, not of the old Hebrew
script, in which *yod* is a full-sized letter. **[inference]** The text
whose least letter Jesus says will not pass away is already a text that has
passed from one script into another. Whatever the saying promises, it cannot
be a promise that the original *letter-forms* were preserved.

The "stroke" (*keraia*, "horn" or "hook") is most naturally the small
projection that distinguishes one letter from another, as *dalet* (ד) from
*resh* (ר). These are precisely the letters that copyists confused.

- The same son of Javan is דֹדָנִים *Dodanim* in Gen 10:4 and רוֹדָנִים
  *Rodanim* in 1 Chr 1:7 (and Ῥόδιοι, "Rhodians," in the Greek of Gen 10:4).
- In Zech 12:10 the MT reads דָּקָרוּ "they pierced"; the LXX's κατωρχήσαντο
  "they danced insultingly" translates רקדו, the same three letters with
  *dalet* and *resh* reversed.
- In Ps 22:16 (MT 22:17) the MT's כָּאֲרִי "like a lion [at] my hands and my
  feet" differs from the LXX's ὤρυξαν "they dug [into] my hands and my feet"
  by the length of a single stroke: a *yod* in the MT where the Psalms scroll
  from Naḥal Ḥever (5/6ḤevPs) has a *vav*, כארו, a verb. **[context]**
  (Flint.)

### 3.2 What an abjad leaves unwritten

Hebrew was written with consonants only. Over time certain consonants
(א ה ו י) came to be used as **vowel letters** (*matres lectionis*), but
irregularly. The Qumran scrolls often spell much more fully than the MT,
which itself spells the same word differently in different places. Short
vowels, doubled consonants, and most distinctions of verbal stem and
pronominal suffix were not written at all. **[context]**

The full system of vowel signs and accents was devised by the **Masoretes**
between about the seventh and tenth centuries AD. There were several rival
systems (Babylonian, Palestinian and Tiberian) before the Tiberian system of
the Ben Asher family prevailed. The Aleppo Codex (c. 930) and the Leningrad
Codex (1008/1009) are its classic witnesses. The Talmud, completed before
the Masoretes, knows the reading tradition but never mentions written vowel
signs. **[context]** (Tov; Würthwein; Khan.)

Until then, the text as written underdetermined the text as read. The gap
was filled by an **oral tradition**, which the rabbis treated as part of
the revelation itself. The Talmud classes the "reading of the scribes," the
words read but not written, and the words written but not read as "law
given to Moses at Sinai" (b. Nedarim 37b–38a). The written record of that
gap is the Masorah:

- **Qere and ketiv**: places where the text is to be read differently from
  the way it is written. Their number ranges from several hundred to about
  fifteen hundred in different manuscripts and lists (Tov).
- **The divine name** is written יהוה and read *Adonai* throughout: a
  permanent difference between the written and the read text.
- **The eighteen "corrections of the scribes"** (*tiqqune soferim*), places
  where the tradition itself says the wording was altered out of reverence
  (for example Gen 18:22).
- **Extraordinary dots** over letters or words in fifteen places, and
  **inverted *nun*s** around Num 10:35–36, signs that the scribes doubted or
  bracketed the text they were copying.

**[inference]** The Masoretic system is a monument to the care with which
the Hebrew was guarded. It is also the clearest evidence that the guarding
was done by a tradition alongside the text and not by the written text
alone.

### 3.3 Where the consonants allow more than one reading

In most of the Hebrew Bible the consonants, read in context, admit only one
sensible reading, and the abjad makes no practical difference. The question
is what happens in the places where they do not. Four examples matter here,
because the New Testament or the preservation debate turns on them.

**Gen 47:31 and Heb 11:21.** The consonants מטה can be read *miṭṭah*, "bed,"
or *maṭṭeh*, "staff." The Masoretes pointed "bed": "Israel bowed himself
upon the head of the **bed**" (עַל־רֹאשׁ הַמִּטָּה). The LXX translators read
"staff": "upon the top of his **staff**" (ἐπὶ τὸ ἄκρον τῆς ῥάβδου αὐτοῦ).
Hebrews 11:21 quotes the LXX word for word. Calvin drew the conclusion in
his commentary on that verse: "This is one of those places from which we may
conclude that the points were not formerly used by the Hebrews; for the
Greek translators could not have made such a mistake as to put staff here
for a bed, if the mode of writing was then the same as now"
([Calvin on Heb 11:21](../theologians/reformed/continental/calvin/commentary-on-hebrews.md#hebrews-1121)).
His editor went further: "the Apostle adopted the Septuagint version, and
thus sanctioned it."

**Amos 9:12 and Acts 15:17.** The MT reads "that they may **possess**
(יִירְשׁוּ) the remnant of **Edom** (אֱדוֹם)." The LXX reads "that the remnant
of **men** (τῶν ἀνθρώπων, i.e. אָדָם *adam*) may **seek** (ἐκζητήσωσιν, i.e.
ידרשו)." *Edom* and *adam* are the same consonants (אדם) when *Edom* is
spelled without its vowel letter; "possess" and "seek" differ by one letter
(*yod* and *dalet*). At the Jerusalem council James quotes the LXX form
(Acts 15:16–17), and his argument, that the Gentiles are to seek the Lord,
depends on it. The Westminster Confession cites Acts 15:15 among its proofs
that the Church must finally appeal to the original-language Scriptures
(WCF 1.8, proof [b]). **[inference]** The verse it cites introduces a
quotation that follows the Greek against the Masoretic reading.

**Ps 12:7 (MT 12:8).** The MT reads תִּשְׁמְרֵם תִּצְּרֶנּוּ, "you will keep
**them**, you will guard **him**." The consonants of the second verb, תצרנו,
can be read either as "you will guard him" (with the doubled *nun* the
Masoretes pointed) or as "you will guard us." The LXX read "us" in both
verbs: φυλάξεις ἡμᾶς καὶ διατηρήσεις ἡμᾶς. In both the Hebrew and the Greek
the nearest antecedent is the afflicted poor of verse 5; the "words" of
verse 6 (אִמֲרוֹת, a feminine plural) do not match either masculine suffix.
**[inference]** Whatever one makes of the Masoretic pointing, the verse's
use as a promise that God will preserve his *words* rests on a reading the
Greek does not support and the Hebrew grammar strains against.

**Jonah 3:4.** "Yet **forty** days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown" (MT);
"yet **three** days" (LXX, ἔτι τρεῖς ἡμέραι). This is not a vowel question,
but it shows how far the two texts could stand apart in a single word.
Augustine, who held both texts inspired, judged that Jonah said "forty" and
that the Spirit said "three" through the translators
([*City of God* XVIII.44](../theologians/early-church/augustine/city-of-god.md#book-xviii-chapter-44)).

### 3.4 The controversy over the points

Whether the vowel points were as old as the consonants was argued for two
centuries, and the argument is represented in this repository.

| Date | Writer | Position |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1538 | Elias Levita, *Masoreth ha-Masoreth* | The points are post-Talmudic, the work of the Masoretes of Tiberias |
| 1549 | Calvin, *Commentary on Hebrews* | "The points were not formerly used by the Hebrews" ([Heb 11:21](../theologians/reformed/continental/calvin/commentary-on-hebrews.md#hebrews-1121)) |
| 1624, 1650 | Louis Cappel, *Arcanum punctationis revelatum*; *Critica sacra* | The points are late; the versions may be used to correct the Hebrew |
| 1648 | Johannes Buxtorf the younger, *Tractatus de punctorum… origine* | The points are ancient |
| 1659 | John Owen, *Of the Integrity and Purity of the Hebrew and Greek Text* | The points go back to Ezra and the great synagogue; "every letter and tittle" is preserved in the copies ([ch. II](../theologians/reformed/british/owen/integrity-and-purity.md#chapter-ii); [ch. V](../theologians/reformed/british/owen/integrity-and-purity.md#chapter-v)) |
| 1675 | Helvetic Consensus, Canon II | Inspired "not only in its consonants, but in its vowels — either the vowel points themselves, or at least the power of the points" |
| 1767 | John Gill, *Dissertation concerning… the Vowel-Points* | The points are "coeval with the letters" ([ch. IV](../theologians/reformed/british/gill/dissertation-vowel-points.md)) |

Owen saw clearly what was at stake. Summarizing the opposing view, he wrote
that if the points were a late invention, "the whole credit of our reading
and interpretation of the Scripture, as far as regulated by the present
punctuation, depends solely on the faithfulness and skill of those Jews whose
invention this work is asserted to be"
([ch. I](../theologians/reformed/british/owen/integrity-and-purity.md#chapter-i)).

**[context]** The historical question is now settled: the vowel and accent
*signs* date from about the seventh to the tenth centuries. What remains
open, and is argued in the scholarship, is how old the *reading* they record
is. The prevailing view is that the Tiberian vocalization records a reading
tradition that is much older than the signs and continuous with the reading
of the late Second Temple period, though not identical to it (Khan; Tov).
**[inference]** That is exactly the position the Helvetic Consensus fell
back on with "or at least the power of the points": a claim not for the
written signs but for the oral tradition they encode.

## 4. The Hebrew: the manuscript record

### 4.1 What survives, and how old it is

| Witness | Date | What it shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ketef Hinnom silver amulets | late 7th–early 6th c. BC | The priestly blessing (Num 6:24–26) in a shorter form than the MT; the oldest surviving biblical text (Barkay et al.) |
| Nash Papyrus | 2nd–1st c. BC | The Decalogue and the *Shema*, in a liturgical text mixing Exodus and Deuteronomy |
| Dead Sea Scrolls (Qumran and nearby) | c. 250 BC–AD 68 | More than two hundred biblical manuscripts, mostly fragmentary; every book but Esther |
| Masada, Wadi Murabbaʿat, Naḥal Ḥever | to AD 73 and 135 | Biblical scrolls almost identical to the later MT in their consonants |
| Cairo Genizah fragments | from c. 9th c. | The vocalization systems in competition |
| Aleppo Codex | c. 930 | The model Tiberian codex; most of the Torah and other parts lost after riots in Aleppo in 1947 |
| Leningrad Codex | 1008/1009 | The oldest complete Hebrew Bible; the base of modern critical editions |

**[context]** The gap between the latest Old Testament books and the oldest
complete Hebrew Bible is about thirteen centuries. Before the discovery of
the Dead Sea Scrolls (1947 onward) the oldest substantial Hebrew manuscripts
were medieval.

### 4.2 Before the Masoretes: several Hebrew editions

The Dead Sea Scrolls transformed the picture in two directions at once.

**They vindicated the care of the proto-Masoretic tradition.** The second
Isaiah scroll from Cave 1 (1QIsa^b), a thousand years older than the
Leningrad Codex, differs from it in its consonants only in small matters of
spelling and minor readings. The scrolls from Masada and the Bar Kokhba caves
are, for practical purposes, the MT. **[context]** (Tov; Würthwein.)

**They also showed that before the late first century AD the proto-Masoretic
text was one Hebrew text-form among several.** In Tov's classification the
proto-Masoretic group is the largest (around half of the scrolls long enough
to classify), but a large group is "non-aligned," and smaller groups stand
close to the Hebrew from which the LXX was translated or to the Samaritan
Pentateuch. Ulrich describes several books as circulating in "multiple
literary editions." **[context]** Among the cases that touch this study:

- **Jeremiah.** The LXX is shorter than the MT by about one-seventh and
  arranges the oracles against the nations differently. Two Hebrew
  fragments from Qumran (4QJer^b and 4QJer^d) share the short form. The
  difference is not a translator's abridgment but a second Hebrew edition.
  (Tov.) It is fitting that Jeremiah is also the book that narrates the loss
  and re-creation of a scroll of God's words: when Jehoiakim burned it,
  Jeremiah dictated it again, "and there were added besides to them many
  words like them" (Jer 36:32; LXX 43:32).
- **Deut 32:8.** The MT says God fixed the bounds of the peoples "according
  to the number of the **sons of Israel**" (בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל). The LXX reads
  "according to the number of the **angels of God**" (ἀγγέλων θεοῦ), and a
  Qumran manuscript (4QDeut^j) reads "**sons of God**" (בני אלוהים), the
  Hebrew the LXX presupposes.
- **Deut 32:43.** The LXX has a longer verse, including "let all the angels
  of God worship him," which Heb 1:6 quotes. A Qumran manuscript (4QDeut^q)
  has a longer Hebrew text here too, including a line calling on "all gods"
  to worship him. The MT has neither.
- **1 Sam 10:27–11:1.** The great Samuel scroll from Cave 4 (4QSam^a) contains
  a paragraph on Nahash the Ammonite's campaign of gouging out right eyes
  that is absent from the MT and the LXX but known to Josephus. Several
  modern translations now print it.
- **1 Sam 17:4.** Goliath is "six cubits and a span" in the MT, and "four
  cubits and a span" in the LXX and in 4QSam^a.
- **Isa 53:11.** "Out of the anguish of his soul he shall see" (MT) is "he
  shall see **light**" in the LXX (δεῖξαι αὐτῷ φῶς) and in three Isaiah
  scrolls from Qumran (1QIsa^a, 1QIsa^b, 4QIsa^d).
- **Isa 40:7–8.** The LXX lacks most of MT verse 7 ("for the breath of the
  LORD blows on it; surely the people is grass") and the repeated "the grass
  withers, the flower fades." In the great Isaiah scroll (1QIsa^a) the words
  of verse 7 are a later addition, written between the lines and in the
  margin. The passage at the centre of the preservation claims is itself one
  where the Hebrew circulated in a longer and a shorter form. **[context]**
  (Tov; Ulrich.)
- **Ps 145:13.** Psalm 145 is an alphabetic acrostic, one verse for each
  letter. In the MT the *nun* verse is missing. The LXX has it (144:13a,
  πιστὸς κύριος ἐν τοῖς λόγοις αὐτοῦ καὶ ὅσιος ἐν πᾶσι τοῖς ἔργοις αὐτοῦ, "the
  Lord is faithful in his words, and holy in all his works"), and so does the
  great Psalms scroll from Cave 11 (11QPs^a), in Hebrew: נאמן אלוהים בדבריו
  וחסיד בכול מעשיו. **[inference]** The one verse in the Psalter that the
  manuscript evidence shows the MT to have lost is a verse about the
  faithfulness of God's words.

The **Samaritan Pentateuch**, a third Hebrew witness to the Torah,
traditionally counts some six thousand differences from the MT, most of them
trivial, of which about nineteen hundred agree with the LXX. **[context]**
(Würthwein; Tov.)

### 4.3 After: consolidation and the Masorah

**[context]** By the end of the first century AD the proto-Masoretic text had
displaced the other Hebrew text-forms in Judaism. Why is debated: the
destruction of the Temple and of the sects that kept other texts, the
authority of a Temple master copy, and the dominance of the rabbinic movement
are all proposed. The early Jewish revisions of the Greek (below, 5.3) were
made to bring the Greek into line with this text, which shows that it had
become the standard by then.

From that point the Hebrew consonantal text was transmitted with unusual
precision, under rules for scribes, with counted letters, and eventually
under the full apparatus of the Masorah. Medieval Masoretic manuscripts
differ among themselves mainly in spelling, vocalization and accents, and
seldom in a way that changes the sense.

### 4.4 Assessment: the Hebrew as the ground of preservation

**Strengths.**

- It is the original language of almost all of the Old Testament.
- Its consonantal text has been transmitted with demonstrable, extraordinary
  stability for about nineteen centuries.
- Its custody was continuous, by the community Paul says was "entrusted with
  the oracles of God" (Rom 3:2), the text the Helvetic Consensus cites.
- It fits the one preservation saying pitched at the level of letters
  (Matt 5:18), which speaks of Hebrew letters in the square script.

**Weaknesses.**

- Before about AD 100 the Hebrew of several books existed in more than one
  edition, and the MT represents one of them. In places the MT is the
  shorter or damaged text (1 Sam 10–11; Ps 145:13).
- Its vowels, and therefore part of its meaning, were fixed in writing only
  in the early Middle Ages. For the centuries before, the reading rests on an
  oral tradition, carried after the first century by a community that did not
  speak Hebrew and did not accept the New Testament.
- Its oldest complete witness is a thousand years later than the oldest
  complete Greek Bibles.
- The scripts and spellings in which it was written changed over time, so
  that "the letters" of the text cannot mean the letter-forms that were first
  written.

## 5. The Greek: the Septuagint

### 5.1 Greek as a language

Greek has been spoken and written without interruption from antiquity to
the present day. The Koine in which the LXX and the New Testament were
written was the common language of the eastern Roman world, and the Greek
churches have read the LXX in their liturgy ever since. Greek is written in
a full alphabet, with vowels: a Greek text, once copied, fixes its own
reading in a way an unpointed Hebrew text does not. On both of the counts on
which this study was asked to test the Hebrew, Greek is stronger. **[context]**

### 5.2 A translation, and an inspired one?

The LXX began as a translation of the Torah in Alexandria in the third
century BC, and the rest of the books followed over the next two centuries
or so, by many hands and with very different methods: some books are
painfully literal, others free. **[context]** (Jobes and Silva; Fernández
Marcos.)

The legend grew with the telling.

- The ***Letter of Aristeas*** has seventy-two translators, six from each
  tribe, finishing the Law in seventy-two days
  ([text](../theologians/jewish-antiquity/aristeas/letter-of-aristeas.md)).
- **Philo** has them working apart and producing identical words "as if some
  unseen prompter had dictated all their language," so that they were "not
  translators but prophets"
  ([*Life of Moses* II.vii](../theologians/jewish-antiquity/philo/life-of-moses.md#book-ii-chapter-vii)).
- **Augustine** has them shut up separately and agreeing in every word,
  "because in very deed the one Spirit had been in them all"
  ([*City of God* XVIII.42](../theologians/early-church/augustine/city-of-god.md#book-xviii-chapter-42)),
  and concludes that where the Hebrew and Greek differ, the same Spirit said
  one thing through the prophets and another through the translators, "thus
  showing that both were prophets"
  ([XVIII.43](../theologians/early-church/augustine/city-of-god.md#book-xviii-chapter-43)).
- **Jerome** rejected the separate cells as a fable and denied that the
  translators were inspired
  ([Preface to Genesis](../theologians/early-church/jerome/vulgate-prefaces.md#preface-to-genesis)).

The translators' own community said otherwise. Ben Sira's grandson (above,
2.2) says plainly that a translation does not carry "the same force," and
that the Greek Law and Prophets "differ not a little" from the Hebrew.

**[inference]** A preservation claim grounded in the LXX must therefore
choose. Either it adopts Augustine's view that the translation is itself
inspired, a view no Protestant confession holds and which has no support in
the New Testament beyond its use of the Greek; or it treats the LXX as a
faithful translation, in which case the Greek can preserve only the *sense*
of the Hebrew and not its words, and the claim becomes a claim about sense.

### 5.3 Not one text but many

"The Septuagint" is a convenient name for a complicated tradition.

- **The Old Greek** is the original translation of each book, recoverable
  only by reconstruction.
- **Jewish revisions** began before the Christian era, correcting the Old
  Greek toward the proto-Masoretic Hebrew. The Greek Minor Prophets scroll
  from Naḥal Ḥever (8ḤevXIIgr, late first century BC or early first
  century AD) preserves one, the so-called *kaige* revision (Barthélemy).
  In the second century AD came the new versions of **Aquila**,
  **Symmachus** and **Theodotion**.
- **Origen's Hexapla** (c. 230–245) set the Hebrew, a Greek transliteration,
  and four Greek versions in parallel columns. Its LXX column added from
  the other versions what the Hebrew had and the Greek lacked (marked with
  asterisks) and marked what the Greek had and the Hebrew lacked (with
  obeli). Augustine describes the marks
  ([*City of God* XVIII.43](../theologians/early-church/augustine/city-of-god.md#book-xviii-chapter-43)).
  When the marks were dropped in copying, the hexaplaric additions entered
  the Greek tradition as if they were original. **[context]**
- **Christian recensions.** By about 400, Jerome reports, Alexandria and
  Egypt read the edition of Hesychius, Constantinople to Antioch that of the
  martyr Lucian, and Palestine Origen's, "and the whole world is at war with
  itself over this threefold variety" (*totusque orbis hac inter se trifaria
  varietate conpugnat*, Preface to Chronicles).
- **Daniel.** The churches did not read the Old Greek of Daniel at all. "The
  Septuagint version of Daniel the prophet is not read by the Churches of our
  Lord and Saviour. They use Theodotion's version" (Jerome,
  [Preface to Daniel](../theologians/early-church/jerome/vulgate-prefaces.md#daniel)).
- **Book-level differences.** Besides Jeremiah and Samuel, Greek Job is about
  a sixth shorter than the Hebrew (Origen noticed it,
  [Letter to Africanus](../theologians/early-church/origen/letter-to-africanus.md)),
  Proverbs is arranged differently (the sayings of Agur, Prov 30:1–14,
  stand after 24:22, so that "all the words of God are tried in the fire,"
  Prov 30:5, is found there in the Greek), the tabernacle chapters of Exodus
  35–40 differ, and Esther and Daniel carry long additions.
- **The canon.** The great Greek codices include books outside the Hebrew
  canon. A Protestant who grounds preservation in the LXX inherits the
  canon question that the companion studies,
  [canon-in-the-confessions.md](canon-in-the-confessions.md) and
  [canon-in-the-theologians.md](canon-in-the-theologians.md), treat at length.

### 5.4 The manuscript record

| Witness | Date | What it shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Papyrus Rylands 458 | 2nd c. BC | Fragments of Greek Deuteronomy: the Greek Torah before Christ |
| Papyrus Fouad 266 | 1st c. BC | Greek Deuteronomy, the divine name written in Hebrew letters |
| 4QLXXLev^a and other Qumran Greek fragments | 2nd–1st c. BC | Greek Leviticus and Numbers among the Dead Sea Scrolls |
| 8ḤevXIIgr | late 1st c. BC–early 1st c. AD | The Greek Minor Prophets, already revised toward the Hebrew |
| Codex Vaticanus, Codex Sinaiticus | 4th c. AD | Complete (or nearly complete) Greek Bibles, Old and New Testaments |
| Codex Alexandrinus | 5th c. AD | Likewise |

**[context]** From the second century AD onward the LXX was transmitted
almost entirely by Christians. Jews abandoned it for Aquila and the other
revisions, in part because Christians argued from it. The charge of
tampering ran both ways: Justin Martyr accused the Jews of removing "from the
tree" from Ps 96:10 ("the Lord reigned from the tree"), words found in only
a few Christian witnesses and absent from the main Greek manuscripts (the
Greek printed by Brenton reads simply ὁ κύριος ἐβασίλευσε, LXX Ps 95:10).

### 5.5 Assessment: the Greek as the ground of preservation

**Strengths.**

- Its language never died, and its script writes the vowels.
- Complete copies survive from the fourth century.
- It is the Old Testament the apostles usually quote, and the one through
  which the Gentile churches received the Scriptures (Augustine,
  [XVIII.42](../theologians/early-church/augustine/city-of-god.md#book-xviii-chapter-42):
  translated "that they might be made known to all the nations").
- In several places where it differs from the MT, the Dead Sea Scrolls show
  it to be a faithful witness to an older or better Hebrew reading.

**Weaknesses.**

- It is a translation, of uneven quality, and its own first readers said
  that translation loses force.
- There is no single LXX text, and the text that circulated in the churches
  was mixed with later revisions toward the Hebrew, so that in many places
  the "Greek" is itself a later echo of the Hebrew.
- Some of its differences from the MT are plain mistakes or guesses (Gen
  47:31 reads the wrong vowels; Zech 12:10 transposes two letters).
- Matt 5:18 and Rom 3:2 point to the Hebrew and its Jewish custodians, not to
  the Greek.
- It carries a different canon.

## 6. How the New Testament uses the two

The New Testament is the evidence any Christian theory of preservation has to
fit, because it shows how the apostles and Jesus himself handled the Old
Testament text.

**It usually quotes the Greek.** Most of its explicit quotations are closer
to the LXX than to the MT, and some arguments depend on readings found only
in the Greek. **[context]** (Swete; Jobes and Silva.)

| NT | OT | Follows | The point that depends on it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Heb 11:21 | Gen 47:31 | LXX "staff" against MT "bed" | Jacob's worship "leaning on his staff" |
| Acts 15:16–17 | Amos 9:11–12 | LXX "the rest of men may seek" against MT "possess the remnant of Edom" | The Gentiles seeking the Lord |
| Heb 1:6 | Deut 32:43 | LXX (supported by 4QDeut^q); absent from MT | "Let all God's angels worship him" |
| Heb 10:5 | Ps 40:6 (LXX 39:7) | LXX "a body you prepared for me" against MT "ears you have dug for me" | The incarnation as the true sacrifice |
| 1 Pet 1:24–25 | Isa 40:6–8 | LXX, without MT 40:7 | "The word of the Lord abides forever" |
| Rom 3:4 | Ps 51:4 (MT 51:6; LXX 50:6) | LXX "when you are judged" against MT "when you judge" | God's justification in his words |

**It sometimes follows the Hebrew against the Greek.**

| NT | OT | Follows | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Matt 2:15 | Hos 11:1 | MT "out of Egypt I called **my son**" against LXX "**his children**" | Jerome cites this pair to show the Hebrew contains what the apostles quoted ([Preface to Genesis](../theologians/early-church/jerome/vulgate-prefaces.md#preface-to-genesis)) |
| John 19:37 | Zech 12:10 | MT "**pierced**" against LXX "danced insultingly" | Likewise |

**It carries Jesus' words in translation.** Jesus taught chiefly in Aramaic;
the Gospels preserve a handful of his words in Aramaic and then translate
them (*Talitha koum*, Mark 5:41; *Eloi, Eloi, lema sabachthani*, Mark 15:34).
The saying "my words will not pass away" (Matt 24:35) has itself come down to
us in Greek. **[inference]** If the words of Jesus that "will not pass away"
are preserved, they are preserved in translation, and the New Testament
regards that as their preservation.

**It transmits its own preservation sayings with variants.** In Mark 13:31
the manuscripts differ over the form of the verb and whether to include the
emphatic negative. In 1 Pet 1:24 they differ over "all **its** glory" and
"all the glory **of man**," the latter matching the LXX. **[context]** (SBLGNT
apparatus.) None of these differences touches the sense. **[inference]** The
New Testament's own text illustrates the same kind of preservation that the
Hebrew tradition shows after AD 100: substantial and reliable, but not
letter-identical across copies.

**[inference]** Taken together, the New Testament does not adopt either
text as the exclusive vehicle of God's word. It argues from the Greek where
the Greek suits its purpose and from the Hebrew where the Hebrew does, it
treats a translation as "the word of the Lord" (1 Pet 1:25 quotes the
Greek and calls it that), and it shows no anxiety about the difference.
That practice is the strongest single argument against grounding the
preservation claims in either text-form to the exclusion of the other.

## 7. Head to head

This section argues the ratings in the [scorecard](#scorecard).

**1. Original language.** The Hebrew is the original for all but the Aramaic
sections. The Greek is a translation. *Hebrew strong, Greek weak.*

**2. A single identifiable text.** After about AD 100 the Hebrew has one
dominant consonantal text, and its manuscripts differ in minor ways. Before
that, several Hebrew editions circulated (section 4.2). The Greek never had
a single text in use: the Old Greek was revised in several directions, and
by the fourth century the churches read three competing recensions (5.3).
*Hebrew strong after AD 100 and weak before; Greek weak throughout.*

**3. Whether the writing fixes the reading.** An unpointed Hebrew text
depends on a reading tradition for its vowels; that tradition was not
written down until the seventh to tenth centuries, and the New Testament
itself follows readings of the consonants that the Masoretes did not adopt
(3.3). Greek writes its vowels. *Hebrew weak to moderate; Greek strong.*

**4. Continuity of the language.** Greek was never interrupted. Hebrew lost
its native speakers for some sixteen or seventeen centuries but never lost
its readers, scribes or scholars (2.3), though it lost some knowledge of
rare words and spellings, much of it before Christ (2.4). *Hebrew moderate;
Greek strong.*

**5. Antiquity of complete copies.** Complete Greek Bibles survive from the
fourth century; the oldest complete Hebrew Bible is from 1008/1009. The Dead
Sea Scrolls close much of the gap for the Hebrew, but they are mostly
fragmentary. *Hebrew weak; Greek strong.*

**6. Stability once fixed.** The proto-Masoretic consonantal text is the
best-guarded text of antiquity (4.3). The Greek was repeatedly revised,
partly toward the Hebrew, and its hexaplaric additions entered the main
tradition unmarked (5.3). *Hebrew very strong; Greek moderate.*

**7. Use by the New Testament.** The New Testament usually quotes the Greek,
sometimes in arguments that depend on a Greek reading, and occasionally
follows the Hebrew against the Greek (section 6). *Hebrew moderate; Greek
strong.*

**8. Fit to the letter-level saying (Matt 5:18).** The saying is about the
smallest letter of the Hebrew square script and the strokes of Hebrew
letters. It does not describe a Greek text. *Hebrew strong; Greek weak.*

**9. Fit to the gospel-level saying (1 Pet 1:25).** Peter quotes the Greek
form of Isa 40:6–8 and identifies "the word of the Lord" that "abides
forever" with the preached gospel. *Hebrew moderate; Greek strong.*

**10. Custody.** Rom 3:2 says the oracles of God were "entrusted" to the
Jews, and the Hebrew text was transmitted by the synagogue, which is the
ground on which the Helvetic Consensus receives it ("as handed down by the
Hebrew Church"). The LXX after the second century was transmitted by the
church. *Hebrew strong; Greek weak.* **[inference]** But two verses later
(Rom 3:4) Paul quotes the Psalms in the Greek wording, so the custody text
does not itself imply that the Greek is excluded.

**The decisive observation.** **[inference]** The Greek and the Hebrew are
not independent rivals. The LXX is a witness to a Hebrew text, and where it
departs most from the MT (Jeremiah, Samuel, Deut 32, Ps 145) the scrolls
show it translating a different *Hebrew* edition. The question "Hebrew or
Greek?" therefore resolves largely into "which Hebrew?": the
proto-Masoretic text, consolidated and guarded from about AD 100, or the
text-forms the LXX translated two or three centuries earlier, which the
apostles often used. Neither the MT alone nor the LXX alone holds all of
the evidence for the original wording; together, with the scrolls, the
Samaritan Pentateuch and the other ancient versions, they do much better.

## 8. What this means for the preservation claims

The answer depends on what the preservation claims are taken to claim. Three
readings are possible, and the evidence bears on each differently.

### 8.1 A strong verbal claim

*One identifiable text has been preserved, letter for letter (and, for the
Hebrew, vowel for vowel), from the first writing to the present.* This is the
Helvetic Consensus's claim (Canons I–II), and the claim many readers hear in
"kept pure in all ages."

- **Grounded in the Hebrew:** defensible for the **consonants after about
  AD 100**, allowing for minor differences among manuscripts. Not defensible
  for the **vowel signs**, which are medieval. Not defensible for the period
  **before AD 100**, when the MT was one Hebrew edition among several and in
  places the shorter or damaged one. Defensible for the vowels only in the
  fallback form, an inspired oral reading tradition, which grounds written
  Scripture's meaning in an unwritten tradition kept by the synagogue.
- **Grounded in the Greek:** not defensible at all. There is no single LXX
  text to point to, the LXX is a translation, and it was transmitted in
  competing recensions.

*Verdict:* the Hebrew does far better than the Greek, but neither sustains
the claim without exceptions. **[inference]**

### 8.2 A providential-sufficiency claim

*God has so ordered the transmission of Scripture that its original wording
is preserved in the body of surviving witnesses taken together, and can be
recovered from them.* This is close to Owen's position, who granted that the
autographs were "utterly perished" and that the copies contain "various
lections," and held that "the whole Scripture, entire as given out from God,
without any loss, is preserved in the copies of the originals yet remaining"
([ch. II](../theologians/reformed/british/owen/integrity-and-purity.md#chapter-ii)).

- **Grounded in the Hebrew:** strong, provided "the copies" includes the
  Dead Sea Scrolls and the ancient versions as witnesses to the Hebrew.
- **Grounded in the Greek:** cannot stand alone, since the Greek's value as
  a witness is measured by the Hebrew it translates; but it is an
  indispensable part of the evidence (4.2).

*Verdict:* strong, but only on a Hebrew-plus-versions basis, and on that
basis the LXX must be allowed to correct the MT in some places, which
Owen and the Helvetic Consensus (Canon III) refuse. **[inference]**

### 8.3 A message-and-efficacy claim

*What God has said, his promises, commands, and the gospel, will never be
lost or fail, and will accomplish what he sent it to do.* This is what the
passages most directly say (section 1), and it is how the New Testament uses
them.

- **Grounded in the Hebrew:** strong. The differences among Hebrew text-forms
  do not touch any doctrine.
- **Grounded in the Greek:** at least as strong. Greek is the medium in which
  the Old Testament actually reached the nations, in which the New Testament
  calls a translation "the word of the Lord," and in which Jesus' own words
  were preserved.

*Verdict:* both groundings satisfy this reading, and the Greek has the
better historical case for the *spread* of the word. **[inference]**

### 8.4 The two features weighed

**Hebrew's history as a language.** This weakens a Hebrew-grounded claim
less than one might expect, because the community that read and copied the
text never broke. Its real cost is the loss of certain knowledge of rare
words and of some spellings, and that loss began before the LXX was made.
It weakens claims about the *meaning* of a small number of words more than
claims about the *text*.

**Hebrew as an abjad.** This weakens a Hebrew-grounded claim more, and in a
specific way: it makes any claim that extends to vowels a claim about an
oral tradition, not about a written text, and it is the reason the New
Testament and the MT sometimes read the same consonants differently. A
consonantal text can be preserved perfectly and still be read in more than
one way.

**Overall.** **[inference]** If the preservation claims are verbal, the
Hebrew grounding is clearly the stronger of the two, but it holds only from
about AD 100 and only for the consonants. If they are claims about God's
message and its effect, the Greek grounding is at least as strong, and the
New Testament's own practice favours it. The passages themselves, which name
no text, language or script, read most naturally as the second kind of
claim, and nothing in the history of either text contradicts them on that
reading.

## 9. The confessions' text claims in this light

The companion study will cross-reference every confession against the
preservation passages. This section notes only how the confessions' claims
about the *Hebrew and Greek text* stand against the history traced above.
The confession texts and their analysis are in
[canon-in-the-confessions.md](canon-in-the-confessions.md).

| Confession | Claim | In light of this study |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Westminster Confession 1.8** (also **Savoy 1.8**, **Second London 1.8**) | The OT "in Hebrew (which was the native language of the people of God of old)" and the NT in Greek, "being immediately inspired by God, and by his singular care and providence kept pure in all ages, are therefore authentical"; proof, Matt 5:18; translation into "the vulgar language of every nation" commanded | Consonant with the history if "kept pure" means the substance of the text and the Hebrew is understood to include its Aramaic portions and its ancient witnesses. Exceeds the evidence if taken as a claim that one manuscript line has been kept free of all error, especially before AD 100. "Of old" concedes that Hebrew was no longer the people of God's language. The proof [b] Acts 15:15 introduces an LXX-form quotation (6). The command to translate presupposes that the word is the word in translation. |
| **Helvetic Consensus I–III** (1675) | The Hebrew "as handed down by the Hebrew Church" is inspired in consonants and vowels, "either the vowel points themselves, or at least the power of the points"; the LXX and other versions may not correct it | The vowel signs are medieval, so the first alternative is contradicted by the evidence; the second ("the power of the points") is a claim for an oral tradition (3.4). Canon III conflicts with the New Testament's use of LXX readings against the MT (6), and with the scrolls' confirmation of some of those readings (4.2). The strongest Hebrew-grounded claim in the confessions, and the one that fares worst. |
| **Belgic, French, Thirty-Nine Articles, Scots, Heidelberg, Dort** | No claim about the Hebrew or Greek text (see the [summary table](canon-in-the-confessions.md#summary)) | Compatible with any of the three readings in section 8. |
| **Irish Articles** (1615), Art. 4 | Scripture to be read "in the original tongues" and translated | Compatible; makes no claim about the transmission of the text. |
| **Book of Concord** | No claim about the Hebrew, the Greek or the LXX | Compatible with any of the three readings. The Lutheran dogmaticians later held the vowel points inspired (see [canon-in-the-theologians.md](canon-in-the-theologians.md)), but the Book of Concord binds no one to it. |
