# The Canon of Scripture in the Theologians

This study is the companion to
[The Canon of Scripture in the Reformed Confessions and the Book of Concord](canon-in-the-confessions.md).
That study asks what the confessions *bind*; this one asks what the major
teachers of the same traditions *taught*, on the same subjects, in the texts
collected under [`theologians/`](../theologians/README.md):

1. What makes a book canonical, and how is that known?
2. Which books belong to the Old and New Testaments?
3. What are the Apocrypha, and what use may be made of them?
4. What is the authority of the Hebrew text, and are its vowel points original?
5. What is the authority of the Greek: the New Testament original, and the
   Septuagint (LXX)?
6. What is the standing of the Latin Vulgate?

Every claim below is tied to a page or section of one of the texts in the
collection, and quotations are exact (spelling and punctuation as printed in
the edition used). Where a point rests on historical context rather than on
the text quoted, it is marked **[context]**. Where a point is the author of
this study's own reading of a text rather than something the text says in so
many words, it is marked **[inferred]**.

The collection reaches back to the early church and to Jewish antiquity
because every Protestant writer in it argues from those witnesses, and its
modern end is the last generation of confessional scholarship that is in the
public domain (Princeton and Westcott). It is limited to public-domain
English texts, so some major figures are present only through excerpts quoted
by others, and some are absent; see [Gaps](#gaps).

The short version:

- **Every Protestant teacher in the collection receives the Hebrew canon of
  the Old Testament and no more**, and every one of them appeals to the same
  line of patristic witnesses for it: Josephus, Melito, Origen, Athanasius,
  Cyril, Jerome and Rufinus. They answer the Augustinian line (Carthage,
  Augustine, Trent) rather than ignore it.
- **They differ on the use of the Apocrypha**, not on its exclusion: from
  Luther's "useful and good" and Chemnitz's "read in the
  churches… for the edification of the people" to Ames's denial of any
  "secundary Canon" and Gill's protest at their being bound with the Bible.
- **The Lutheran teachers keep a distinction within the New Testament** that
  the Reformed teachers drop: Luther's "chief books," Chemnitz's books
  without "sure, strong, and consentaneous" testimony, and the later
  "protocanonical" and "deuterocanonical" ranks, until Hollaz finds "no
  occasion any longer for that distinction." Whitaker states the Reformed
  position: the 27 books, "those all, and those only."
- **The vowel points divide the period, not the traditions.** Calvin says
  the points were not originally used. Lutheran orthodoxy (as summarized by
  Schmid), Owen and Gill hold them to be part of the inspired text. The
  nineteenth-century writers, Lutheran and Reformed alike, treat that view as
  an error now abandoned.
- **No Protestant teacher here treats the Septuagint as inspired or
  authentic**, but all of them must account for the apostles' use of it, and
  Calvin and Luther both say plainly that the apostles quoted it.
- **The Vulgate is answered as Trent's Vulgate.** The Protestant objection is
  to Trent's decree that it "be held authentic," not to the translation as a
  translation, which Owen calls "much the best" of the old versions.

## The inheritance: Jewish antiquity and the early church

The Protestant teachers argue from a fixed set of ancient witnesses. Those
witnesses fall into two lines, and most of what follows is an argument about
which line represents the church.

### The Hebrew canon and the twenty-two books

- **Josephus** gives the number and the limit: "only twenty-two books, which
  contain the records of all the past times; which are justly believed to be
  divine," and nothing after Artaxerxes has the same credit "because there
  hath not been an exact succession of prophets since that time"
  ([Against Apion I.8](../theologians/jewish-antiquity/josephus/against-apion-and-antiquities.md)).
  He adds that "no one has been so bold as either to add any thing to them, to
  take any thing from them, or to make any change in them." Eusebius copies
  the passage (*Church History* III.10).
- **Melito** (c. 170) is the first Christian list, sent so that his
  correspondent might have the books "accurately"; Esther is absent
  ([Eusebius IV.26](../theologians/early-church/eusebius/church-history.md)).
- **Origen** (as reported by Eusebius VI.25): "the canonical books, as the
  Hebrews have handed them down, are twenty-two," with the Maccabees named
  as "besides these."
- **Athanasius**, Festal Letter 39 (367): "twenty-two books in number,"
  Jeremiah counted "with Baruch, Lamentations, and the epistle." Wisdom,
  Sirach, Esther, Judith and Tobit are "not indeed included in the Canon, but
  appointed by the Fathers to be read by those who newly join us"
  ([§§4–7](../theologians/early-church/athanasius/festal-letter-39.md)). Of the
  canonical books he writes: "Let no man add to these, neither let him take
  ought from these."
- **Cyril of Jerusalem**: "Of these read the two and twenty books, but have
  nothing to do with the apocryphal writings"
  ([Lecture IV.35](../theologians/early-church/cyril-of-jerusalem/catechetical-lecture-4.md)).
- **Jerome**, in the preface to Samuel and Kings (the *Prologus Galeatus*,
  the "helmeted" or "armed" prologue): "what is not found in our list must be
  placed amongst the Apocryphal writings"; and "Wisdom, therefore, which
  generally bears the name of Solomon, and the book of Jesus, the Son of
  Sirach, and Judith, and Tobias, and the Shepherd are not in the canon"
  ([Vulgate prefaces](../theologians/early-church/jerome/vulgate-prefaces.md)).
  In the preface to the books of Solomon he lets the church "read these two
  volumes for the edification of the people, not to give authority to
  doctrines of the Church."
- **Rufinus** gives the three classes that the Reformation will inherit:
  the canonical books; books "which our fathers call not 'Canonical' but
  'Ecclesiastical'"; and the "Apocrypha," which "they would not have read in
  the Churches"
  ([Commentary on the Creed §§36–38](../theologians/early-church/rufinus/creed-and-apology.md)).
- **John of Damascus** keeps the 22 and says of Wisdom and Sirach that they
  "are virtuous and noble, but are not counted nor were they placed in the
  ark" ([Exact Exposition IV.17](../theologians/early-church/john-of-damascus/exact-exposition.md)).

### The wider canon

- **The council of Laodicea** forbids reading "uncanonical books" in church,
  and its list (canon 60) has "Jeremiah, and Baruch, the Lamentations, and the
  Epistle" but none of the other disputed books. **The council of Carthage**
  (419 code, canon 24) lists Tobit, Judith and two books of Maccabees among
  the books "received from our fathers to be read in church"
  ([canon lists](../theologians/early-church/councils/canon-lists.md)).
- **Augustine** judges the canon by "the greater number of catholic
  churches" and counts the Old Testament "within the limits of these
  forty-four books," "Tobias, and Esther, and Judith, and the two books of
  Maccabees" among them
  ([On Christian Doctrine II.8](../theologians/early-church/augustine/on-christian-doctrine.md)).
  Of the Maccabees: "These are held as canonical, not by the Jews, but by the
  Church" ([City of God XVIII.36](../theologians/early-church/augustine/city-of-god.md)).
  Of Wisdom and Ecclesiasticus, that they are not Solomon's, "yet of old the
  Church, especially the Western, received them into authority" (XVII.20).
- **Gregory the Great**, a pope, cites 1 Maccabees but marks it as from "the
  books, though not Canonical, yet brought out for the edifying of the
  Church" ([Moralia XIX.21](../theologians/early-church/gregory-the-great/moralia.md)).
  The Protestant writers will cite him for this (Cosin calls him to "give in
  his witness and suffrage for us";
  [Scholastical History, ch. IX](../theologians/reformed/british/cosin/scholastical-history-of-the-canon.md)).

### The Hebrew and the Seventy

- **The Septuagint legend.** The *Letter of Aristeas* has seventy-two
  translators finish the Law in seventy-two days, and an imprecation laid
  "upon any who should revise the text by adding to, or in any way
  transposing, or omitting ought from, what had been written"
  ([§§301–311](../theologians/jewish-antiquity/aristeas/letter-of-aristeas.md)).
  **Philo** makes the translators "like men inspired," every one using "the
  self-same nouns and verbs, as if some unseen prompter" spoke to each
  ([Life of Moses II](../theologians/jewish-antiquity/philo/life-of-moses.md)).
  **Josephus** and Aristeas both speak of the Law only
  ([Antiquities, Preface §3](../theologians/jewish-antiquity/josephus/against-apion-and-antiquities.md)).
- **Augustine** takes the inspiration of the Seventy to its height: "the same
  Spirit who was in the prophets when they spoke these things was also in the
  seventy men when they translated them"
  ([City of God XVIII.43](../theologians/early-church/augustine/city-of-god.md)).
  He urges Jerome to translate from the LXX rather than the Hebrew, because
  the LXX "has no mean authority, seeing that it has obtained so wide
  circulation, and was the one which the apostles used"
  ([Letter 71](../theologians/early-church/augustine/letters-with-jerome.md)),
  and reports the riot at Oea over Jerome's new rendering of Jonah's "gourd."
- **Jerome** answers by appeal to the "Hebrew verity," and to the old
  Christian scholar who first compared the texts: Origen marked "with an
  asterisk those passages in our copies which are not found in the Hebrew"
  ([Letter to Africanus](../theologians/early-church/origen/letter-to-africanus.md)).
  **Rufinus** accuses Jerome of "translation under the influence of the
  Jews" and of undermining the version the church had used
  ([Apology II.32–37](../theologians/early-church/rufinus/creed-and-apology.md)).
- **No ancient source in the collection discusses the vowel points.** The
  Masoretic pointing is later than all of them **[context]**, and when the
  seventeenth century argues about it, the ancient material is quarried for
  indirect evidence (Jerome's readings, the transliterations in Origen's
  Hexapla, the LXX's mistakes).

## Lutheran

### Luther

Luther's prefaces to the German Bible are the source of the Lutheran
distinction within the canon, and they are judgments about books, not lists
([prefaces](../theologians/lutheran/martin-luther/prefaces-to-the-bible.md);
Jacobs's translation, *Works of Martin Luther* vol. VI).

- **New Testament.** The 1522 preface names John's Gospel, Paul's epistles
  (especially Romans) and 1 Peter "the true kernel and marrow of all the
  books," and calls James "really an epistle of straw, compared to them"
  (p. 444). The passage was dropped from later editions **[context]**.
- **The four books.** "Hitherto we have had the right certain chief books
  of the New Testament. The four following had, in ancient times, a different
  reputation" (Hebrews, p. 476). Of James: "I cannot put him among the chief
  books" (p. 477); of Jude, that the Fathers threw it "out of the main body of
  the Scriptures" (p. 479); of Revelation in 1522, that he holds it "neither
  apostolic nor prophetic" (p. 488).
- **The test.** "All the genuine sacred books agree in this, that all of
  them preach Christ… What does not teach Christ is not apostolic, even though
  St. Peter or Paul taught it" (James, p. 478).
- **The Apocrypha.** The edition used omits Luther's prefaces to the
  Apocrypha (p. 366). His 1534 heading is given in English in the Lutheran
  Cyclopedia: "books which are not regarded of equal authority with the Holy
  Scriptures, but whose reading is nevertheless useful and good"
  ([Jacobs, "Apocrypha"](../theologians/lutheran/henry-eyster-jacobs/lutheran-cyclopedia-apocrypha.md)).
  The Table Talk is blunter: "The third book of Esdras I throw into the Elbe,"
  and "I am so great an enemy to the second book of the Maccabees, and to
  Esther, that I wish they had not come to us at all, for they have too many
  heathen unnaturalities" ([Table Talk XXIV](../theologians/lutheran/martin-luther/table-talk.md)).
  Esther is a canonical book; Luther's dislike of it is a judgment of value,
  not a canon list **[inferred]**.
- **The languages.** "The languages are the scabbard in which the Word of God
  is sheathed," and "the Hebrew language is called sacred"
  ([To the Councilmen, pp. 185–186](../theologians/lutheran/martin-luther/to-the-councilmen-on-the-languages.md)).
  He warns that the Jews' "glosses and interpretations (which I have tested)
  are not to be trusted" and that "the old interpreters, even Jerome, made
  mistakes"
  ([Old Testament preface, p. 380](../theologians/lutheran/martin-luther/prefaces-to-the-bible.md)).
- **The Septuagint.** "St Paul observed the translation of the Seventy
  Interpreters, for he condemned them not; and whereas he was preacher to the
  Greeks, therefore he was constrained to preach as they understood"
  ([Table Talk CCCCXXV](../theologians/lutheran/martin-luther/table-talk.md)).

### The dogmaticians: Chemnitz, Gerhard, Quenstedt, Calov, Hollaz

Schmid's *Doctrinal Theology* is a compendium of the seventeenth-century
Lutheran theologians in their own words, and is the only public-domain English
source for most of them
([§§6–12](../theologians/lutheran/heinrich-schmid/doctrinal-theology-holy-scriptures.md)).

- **What makes a book canonical.** Chemnitz: the Scriptures "derive
  canonical authority principally from the Holy Spirit, by whose impulse and
  inspiration they were written; secondarily, from the writers themselves…;
  finally, from the primitive Church, as a witness" (§12 note 8, p. 85). The
  later church may not decide "anything concerning the sacred books of which
  she cannot adduce clear documentary proof from the testimony of the
  primitive Church." Calov makes two requisites: "inspiration" and "the
  divine sanction (canonicatio divina)" (note 5).
- **How it is known.** Schmid's own observation is that, whatever they say of
  the testimony of the Spirit, "in all the investigations by the
  Dogmaticians, in regard to the canonicity of a single book, there is never
  any allusion to the testimony of the Holy Spirit… but they are all
  conducted upon the basis of historical evidence" (note 9, p. 87).
- **The Old Testament list** follows the Hebrew canon, and Gerhard's list of
  the Apocrypha is "Tobias, Judith, Baruch, I, II, and III Maccabees, III and
  IV Ezra, Wisdom of Solomon, Ecclesiasticus or Syracides," with the other
  additions as "appendices" (§12, p. 81). Quenstedt's tests for an Old
  Testament book include that it was "written in the original Hebrew tongue"
  and "approved, cited, and commended by Christ and the Apostles" (note 10).
- **The use of the Apocrypha.** Chemnitz: "Are then these books to be
  absolutely condemned and rejected ? This we by no means demand" (note 5).
  They were read "merely however for the edification of the people, not as
  proof in matters of doctrine," and "No dogma is, therefore, to be deduced
  from these books which has not clear and indubitable support and evidence in
  the canonical books."
- **The New Testament antilegomena.** Schmid's text distinguishes the books
  "authenticated by all the testimonies" from Hebrews, 2 Peter, 2 and 3 John,
  James, Jude and Revelation, "in regard to which doubts are entertained by
  some" (p. 82). Chemnitz is "the most strenuous": without "sure, strong, and
  consentaneous evidence" of the earliest church, "the Church in after times…
  can no more create a certainty out of an uncertainty than it can make truth
  out of falsehood" (note 12). Gerhard and Quenstedt then make two ranks of
  canonical books, "protocanonical" and "deuterocanonical," the doubt
  touching the human author and not the divine authority; and Hollaz finds
  "no occasion any longer for that distinction" (pp. 89–91).
- **Hebrew and Greek.** The authentic text is "the authentic Hebrew text of
  the Old and Greek of the New Testament" (Hollaz, §12 note 9). A good
  translation, "Such is Luther's translation of the Bible," is "as
  efficacious in causing assent to the faith" as the originals, "although the
  words of that translation were not immediately inspired by God"; canonical
  authority requires that "not only the sense, but also the words, shall have
  been derived immediately from God" (§8 note 18).
- **The vowel points.** Schmid reports that "The inspiration of the Hebrew
  vowel-points was included in this theory" of verbal inspiration, citing
  Gerhard's argument that otherwise "the Scriptures were not communicated by
  God through the prophets, so far as the single words are concerned, since
  without the vowel-points the words cannot possibly exist" (§6, p. 48).
- **The Septuagint** does not appear in the sections Schmid collects.

### Later Lutheran: Jacobs

Henry Eyster Jacobs (1905) keeps the Hebrew canon and the rejection of the
Apocrypha, and gives them a Christological ground: "It is according to this
standard that the Protestant Churches reject the Apocrypha; for these books
bear no testimony concerning Christ"
([Summary, p. 273](../theologians/lutheran/henry-eyster-jacobs/summary-of-the-christian-faith-holy-scripture.md)).
The canon was gathered "not by the decree of any councils, whether of Laodicea
or any other" (p. 270). He quotes Luther's test from the preface to James and
then sets aside Luther's verdict: his "opinions concerning the Epistle of James
… are not indeed to be followed" (pp. 280–281). He criticizes the older
dogmatics for raising the human to the divine, "as when it is claimed that the
Hebrew vowel points are inspired" (p. 282).

## Reformed: continental

### Calvin

- **How the canon is known.** Rome claims that on the church "depend both
  the reverence which is due to Scripture, and the books which are to be
  admitted into the canon"; Calvin answers that Scripture is known by "the
  secret testimony of the Spirit," which "is superior to reason"
  ([Institutes I.vii](../theologians/reformed/continental/calvin/institutes-book-1-chapters-6-9.md)).
- **The Apocrypha.** Against Trent's Fourth Session, Calvin invokes Jerome
  and Rufinus, who counted Ecclesiasticus, Wisdom, Tobit, Judith and the
  Maccabees "not canonical but ecclesiastical books, which might indeed be
  read to the people, but were not entitled to establish doctrine," and he
  turns the epilogue of 2 Maccabees, which asks pardon for its faults, against
  it: "How very alien this acknowledgment from the majesty of the Holy
  Spirit!" ([Antidote, pp. 70–71](../theologians/reformed/continental/calvin/antidote-to-the-council-of-trent-session-4.md)).
- **The disputed New Testament books.** Calvin receives them all, while
  saying what was doubted. Of Hebrews: "this passage indicates that this
  epistle was not written by Paul" (on 2:3), and "either Luke or Clement was
  the author" (on 13:23)
  ([Hebrews](../theologians/reformed/continental/calvin/commentary-on-hebrews.md)).
  Of James: it "was not formerly received by many Churches without opposition
  … I, however, am inclined to receive it without controversy." Of 2 Peter:
  "If it be received as canonical, we must allow Peter to be the author"
  ([Catholic Epistles](../theologians/reformed/continental/calvin/commentary-on-james-and-2-peter.md)).
- **Hebrew, Greek and the Vulgate.** "The sacred oracles of God were
  delivered by Moses and the Prophets in Hebrew, and by the Apostles in Greek"
  and "nothing is better than to consult the original." Calvin attacks Trent
  for "determining that the Vulgate translation only is to be held authentic"
  and lists its errors, such as "Lay hold of discipline!" for "Kiss the Son"
  in Psalm 2 ([Antidote, pp. 68–73](../theologians/reformed/continental/calvin/antidote-to-the-council-of-trent-session-4.md)).
- **The vowel points.** On Hebrews 11:21 ("worshipped on the top of his
  staff"): "This is one of those places from which we may conclude that the
  points were not formerly used by the Hebrews; for the Greek translators
  could not have made such a mistake as to put staff here for a bed, if the
  mode of writing was then the same as now" (p. 290). The translator's note
  goes further: "The points are of no authority; and the Apostle adopted the
  Septuagint version, and thus sanctioned it" (p. 291). That translator is
  John Owen of Thrussington (1853), not the Puritan John Owen **[context]**.
- **The Septuagint in the New Testament.** "The Apostle followed the Greek
  translators when he said, 'A body hast thou prepared;' for in quoting these
  words the Apostles were not so scrupulous, provided they perverted not
  Scripture to their own purpose" (on Hebrews 10:5, p. 227). In the
  *Institutes* the LXX is a providence: "the Greek translation appeared to
  disseminate them over the whole world" (I.viii.10).

### Bullinger, Ursinus, Ames and Wollebius

- **Bullinger** lists the canon with no Apocrypha and counts "fourteen
  epistles" of Paul and seven "which are called both canonical and catholic";
  the books of Moses and the prophets "came sound and uncorrupted even until
  the time of Christ"
  ([Decades I.1–2, pp. 54–55](../theologians/reformed/continental/bullinger/decades-decade-1-sermons-1-2.md)).
- **Ursinus**: "We are not to believe God on account of the testimony of the
  church, but the church upon the testimony of God" (p. 118); and "No doctrine
  can be established by the authority of an apocryphal book" (p. 547)
  ([Commentary on the Heidelberg Catechism](../theologians/reformed/continental/ursinus/commentary-on-the-heidelberg-catechism-selections.md)).
- **Ames** (*Marrow*, 1638/39): the Apocrypha "doe not pertaine to the
  divine Canon, neither were they rightly enough joyned by men of old to the
  canonicall bookes, as a certaine secundary Canon," one reason being that
  they were "not written in *Hebrew*, nor delivered to the *Iewish* Church."
  On the versions: "neither, those seventy, who turned it into *Greeke*, nor
  *Hierome*, nor any such like did performe the office of a Prophet," and
  God's providence preserved "the Fountaines" so that they "should not be
  maimed by the losse of any booke, or deformed by any grievous fault"
  ([Book I, ch. 34](../theologians/reformed/continental/ames/marrow-of-sacred-divinity-book-1-chapter-34.md),
  theses 27–36).
- **Wollebius** (Ross's translation): the Apocrypha, "Although they may be
  read with profit, yet they come short of that authority, which is in the
  Canonical Books, for proving Articles of Faith"; and "no translation is
  authentical, But that which agreeth with the Original fountains of the
  Hebrew and Greek"
  ([Praecognita, Rules XI and XVI](../theologians/reformed/continental/wollebius/abridgment-of-christian-divinity-praecognita.md)).

## Reformed: British

### Whitaker

Whitaker's *Disputation* (1588), written against Bellarmine and Stapleton, is
the fullest Reformed treatment of all six questions
([Question I](../theologians/reformed/british/whitaker/disputation-question-1-canon.md),
[Question II](../theologians/reformed/british/whitaker/disputation-question-2-authentic-edition.md),
[Question III](../theologians/reformed/british/whitaker/disputation-question-3-authority.md)).

- **The Old Testament.** The argument is from the prophetic office: "All
  canonical books of the old Testament were written by prophets: none of these
  books was written by any prophet: therefore none of these books is
  canonical" (I.v, pp. 49–50). Hence "there are no more canonical books of
  the old Testament than the twenty-two which our churches receive" (I.vi,
  p. 64). Chapters VII–XV take the disputed books one at a time.
- **The New Testament.** "Those books of the new Testament which the council
  of Trent hath enumerated, those all, and those only, our church receives"
  (I.xvi, p. 105). Of Luther's doubts about James and Jude: "they must answer
  for themselves: their opinions are no concern of ours," though Luther "hath
  erred no more in this respect than several catholic churches and some holy
  fathers formerly."
- **How the canon is known.** "This (I mean the internal testimony of the
  Holy Spirit) is the only argument which can persuade us" (III.iii, p. 295);
  "the canon of scripture was settled in the time of the apostles, and
  consigned in a certain number of books" (III.v).
- **The authentic edition.** "The Hebrew of the old Testament, and the Greek
  of the new, is the sincere and authentic scripture of God" (II.i, p. 111);
  "we make no edition authentic, save the Hebrew in the old, and the Greek in
  the new, Testament" (II.vii). He holds that the Hebrew copies "could not
  have been corrupted by the Jews."
- **The vowel points.** Whitaker does not argue the antiquity of the points.
  In defending the Hebrew of Isaiah 9:6 he writes: "The points indeed require
  the latter reading, but the letters will bear either" (II.ix). That treats
  the points as a reading of the letters rather than as part of the text
  **[inferred]**.
- **The Septuagint.** It was "the noblest and most famous" of the Greek
  versions (II.iii, p. 117), but "nor is it the same thing to be an
  interpreter and to be a prophet," and Jerome rightly called the seventy
  "not prophets." He reports, as the better opinion, that "the true
  Septuagint is wholly lost, and that the Greek text, as we have it, is a
  mixed and miserably corrupted document." Even Augustine "thought that it
  should be corrected by the originals."

### Owen

Owen's two treatises of 1659 were written against the *Prolegomena* of
Walton's London Polyglot, which adopted Cappellus's account of the points and
the various readings **[context]**
([Integrity and Purity](../theologians/reformed/british/owen/integrity-and-purity.md),
[Divine Original](../theologians/reformed/british/owen/divine-original.md)).

- **The canon.** The Old Testament canon was delivered to the Jewish church
  "in the days of Ezra and his companions," and the Scriptures are received
  "without the contribution of help or assistance from tradition, church, or
  any thing else without themselves" (*Divine Original* I–II, pp. 297–307).
- **The text.** "The whole word of God, in every tittle of it, preserved
  entire in the copies of the original extant" (*Integrity* II, synopsis,
  p. 353). He grants various readings, but holds them of no consequence:
  "we grant that there are and have been various lections in the Old Testament
  and the New."
- **The vowel points.** Owen holds that the points "were completed by אנשי
  כנסת הגדולה, the men of the great synagogue, Ezra and his companions"
  (*Integrity* V, p. 371), and answers the arguments for their novelty one by
  one in chapter VI. What is at stake for him: "If these hundreds of words were
  the critical conjectures and amendments of the Jews, what security have we
  of the mind of God" (VII, p. 405).
- **The versions.** No translation may correct the original: "Better all
  old translations should be consumed out of the earth than that such a figment
  should be admitted" (VIII, p. 409). Of the LXX: "The SEPTUAGINT is that
  which must bear the weight of the whole" (p. 416), and Owen treats the
  Aristeas story as fable. Of the Vulgate: "I esteem it much the best in the
  whole collection exhibited unto us… but not to be compared to sundry modern
  translations" (p. 416).

### Cosin

Cosin's *Scholastical History of the Canon* (1657) is the Anglican answer to
Trent: a catena of witnesses, century by century, for the canon of Article VI
([complete](../theologians/reformed/british/cosin/scholastical-history-of-the-canon.md)).

- Jerome's *Prologus Galeatus* stands at its centre: Jerome set it before his
  Bible "as a helmet of defence against the introduction of any other books
  that should pretend to be of equal authority with it" (ch. VI, p. 111).
- Augustine is reconciled with Jerome rather than opposed to him, and the
  Septuagint is narrowed: "S. Augustine acknowledged no more books than what
  were then translated out of the Hebrew copies sent from Jerusalem, where
  neither Tobit nor Judith, nor any of that class, were to be found" (ch. VII,
  p. 128).
- The conclusion: "That it is not in the power of the Roman Church, nor any
  other, either to make new articles of Faith, or to make any books sacred and
  canonical Scriptures" (p. 285).

### Gill

- **The canon and the Apocrypha.** The Apocrypha are "sometimes bound up
  with the Bible, to the great scandal and disgrace of it"
  ([Body of Divinity I.2, p. 16](../theologians/reformed/british/gill/body-of-doctrinal-divinity.md)).
- **The vowel points.** Gill's *Dissertation* (1767) is the most extended
  defence of the points in the collection. His own view: "I am inclined to
  believe that the vowels were coeval with the letters, and that the penmen of
  the sacred scriptures, severally annexed, the vowel-points to letters in
  their writings" (p. 257). Without them Scripture "becomes flexible, and may
  be turned as a nose of wax" (p. 269), and if they are a human addition, "a
  pointed Bible, if possible, should not be in the world" (p. 272). He argues
  that Origen's Hexapla transliteration of Genesis 1 "exactly agrees with our
  modern punctuation" (p. 189)
  ([Dissertation, ch. IV](../theologians/reformed/british/gill/dissertation-vowel-points.md)).

### Westcott

Westcott writes as a historian of the canon rather than as a controversialist,
and his Anglicanism appears in his verdict: "the Church of England has
followed the golden mean in adhering to the judgment of Jerome"
([Bible in the Church, p. 291](../theologians/reformed/british/westcott/bible-in-the-church.md)).

- "There is not one direct quotation in the whole of the New Testament from
  any of the books included in the Apocrypha" (p. 44), though the Septuagint
  "sufficed (to speak generally) for all" in the apostolic age (p. 23).
- On Trent: "This fatal decree, in which the Council, harassed by the fear of
  lay critics and 'grammarians,' gave a new aspect to the whole question of the
  Canon"; and "no anathema should be added to the second part of the decree
  which affirmed the authority of the Latin Vulgate" (pp. 257–258).
- On the New Testament: "it is impossible to point to any period as marking
  the date at which our present Canon was determined"
  ([General Survey, p. 501](../theologians/reformed/british/westcott/general-survey-canon.md)).
- "Canonicity, and not inspiration, was the point to which all the chief
  authoritative decisions on the Bible pointed" (*Bible in the Church*,
  p. 295).

## Reformed: Princeton

- **Archibald Alexander** (1826; 1851 edition) gives the standard Protestant
  arguments against the Apocrypha in order: "they are not found in the Hebrew
  Bible"; "They are never quoted by Christ and his apostles"; and "these books
  were not received as canonical by the Christian Fathers," followed by a
  survey of witnesses through Cajetan
  ([The Canon, pp. 41–65](../theologians/reformed/princeton/archibald-alexander/the-canon-of-the-old-and-new-testaments.md)).
  "The Hebrew Bible of the Jew is the Bible of the Christian" (p. 33).
- **Charles Hodge** states the rule: "those books, and those only, which
  Christ and his Apostles recognized as the written Word of God, are entitled
  to be regarded as canonical," and the Apocrypha "were not written in Hebrew
  and were not included in the canon of the Jews. They were, therefore, not
  recognized by Christ as the Word of God"
  ([Systematic Theology I, pp. 152–153](../theologians/reformed/princeton/hodge/systematic-theology.md)).
- **W. H. Green** on the Apocrypha: "It is not the canonicity of these books
  which led to their insertion in the Septuagint and other versions, but their
  incorporation with these versions which led in certain quarters to their
  admission to the canon" (p. 182); and he contrasts the Anglican and
  Westminster positions: "The view of the Westminster Confession would
  logically banish them from the volume of Holy Scripture altogether"
  (p. 192). He rejects the theory that "the Alexandrian Jews had a more comprehensive
  canon than the Jews of Palestine" (pp. 124–130)
  ([The Canon](../theologians/reformed/princeton/green/the-canon.md)).
- **Green on the points.** He narrates the Buxtorf–Cappellus controversy, the
  Helvetic Consensus and Owen, and then: "It is now universally conceded,
  however, that the vowel-points were not coeval with the letters of the
  text" (p. 65). Of Owen: "He evidently argues under the impression that to
  deny the inspiration of the points is to destroy all certainty in the
  interpretation of the Old Testament." Green's own conclusion on the text is
  that "no other work of antiquity has been so accurately transmitted"
  (p. 181) ([The Text](../theologians/reformed/princeton/green/the-text.md)).
- **B. B. Warfield** locates canonicity in apostolic authority rather than
  authorship: "the principle of canonicity was not apostolic authorship, but
  imposition by the apostles as 'law'" (p. 455)
  ([Formation of the Canon](../theologians/reformed/princeton/warfield/formation-of-the-canon.md)).

## Comparative observations

### 1. Jerome's canon, Augustine's church

The Protestant writers do not invent a canon; they choose Jerome's. Whitaker,
Cosin, Alexander and Green each build their case on the same sequence
(Josephus, Melito, Origen, Athanasius, Cyril, Jerome, Rufinus) and each must
deal with Carthage and Augustine. Calvin concedes that Trent's view "was held
in the Council of Carthage" and by Augustine, but since Augustine "testifies
that all of his age did not take the same view, let us assume that the point
was then undecided" ([Antidote, p. 70](../theologians/reformed/continental/calvin/antidote-to-the-council-of-trent-session-4.md)).
Whitaker calls Carthage "merely provincial" and then asks "in what sense they
called them canonical," answering that they did not mean "of equal authority
with those which are canonical" in the strict sense
([Question I, ch. IV, pp. 40–44](../theologians/reformed/british/whitaker/disputation-question-1-canon.md)).
Cosin reads Augustine's "canonical" "only in a modal or qualified sense"
([Scholastical History, ch. VII, p. 135](../theologians/reformed/british/cosin/scholastical-history-of-the-canon.md)),
and Green speaks of the canon "understood in a lax and improper sense"
([The Canon, p. 182](../theologians/reformed/princeton/green/the-canon.md)), so that the Augustinian line
becomes a looser use of the word rather than a rival canon. Gregory the Great's "though not
Canonical" is the favourite evidence that the Latin church itself kept
Jerome's distinction until Trent.

### 2. One canon, two uses of the Apocrypha

Exclusion is unanimous; use is not. The range runs:

- **Read with profit, never for doctrine.** Luther's 1534 heading; Chemnitz
  ("by no means" to be condemned); Gerhard's "means of moral improvement";
  Calvin's and Jerome's "ecclesiastical books"; Wollebius ("read with
  profit"); Cosin and Westcott for the Church of England.
- **No standing at all.** Ames (no "secundary Canon"); Gill ("scandal and
  disgrace" to bind them with the Bible); and Green's reading of Westminster,
  which would "banish them from the volume of Holy Scripture altogether."

This is the same scale the confessions show, from the Thirty-Nine Articles to
the Westminster Confession
([confessions analysis, Summary](canon-in-the-confessions.md#summary)).

### 3. The New Testament: ranks or a closed list

On the Old Testament the Lutheran and Reformed teachers agree. On the New they
do not, at least at first. Luther ranked the books; Chemnitz refused to let a
later church "create a certainty out of an uncertainty"; Gerhard and Quenstedt
kept two ranks of canonical books; and only Hollaz dropped the distinction.
The Reformed teachers acknowledge the same ancient doubts (Calvin on Hebrews,
James and 2 Peter) but settle them at once: Whitaker receives "those all, and
those only" that Trent named, and declines to defend Luther. By 1905 Jacobs,
a Lutheran, also says Luther's opinion of James is "not indeed to be
followed." This matches what the confessions show: the Reformed confessions
list the New Testament books, and the Book of Concord lists none.

### 4. How a book is known to be canonical

Both traditions name the testimony of the Holy Spirit (Calvin, Whitaker,
Ursinus, Wollebius; Gerhard and Hollaz among the Lutherans). But the
Lutherans, by Schmid's own account, decide individual books on historical
evidence alone, following Chemnitz's rule of the "primitive Church" as
witness, and the Princeton writers frame it the same way: "those books… which
Christ and his Apostles recognized" (Hodge), "imposition by the apostles"
(Warfield), and Alexander's rule of those "nearest to the time." Whitaker
alone in the collection calls the Spirit's testimony "the only argument which
can persuade us" of the canon itself.

### 5. The vowel points

The question was not a Lutheran-Reformed one. The order is chronological:

1. **Before the controversy.** Calvin (1549) holds that the points were not
   originally used. Whitaker (1588) argues from "the letters" against "the
   points" without taking up their date **[inferred]**.
2. **The seventeenth-century orthodoxy.** After Elias Levita and Cappellus
   denied their antiquity, the elder and younger Buxtorf defended it, and
   their view was, at the time, "very generally adopted by the orthodox party
   in the Church, both in England and on the continent" (Green, p. 64).
   Lutheran verbal inspiration "included" the points (Schmid on Gerhard);
   Owen traces them to Ezra and the great synagogue; the Helvetic Consensus
   (1675) makes them confessional for the Swiss
   ([confessions analysis](canon-in-the-confessions.md)).
3. **The last defence.** Gill (1767) makes them "coeval with the letters."
4. **The nineteenth century.** Green: "now universally conceded" that they
   are not; Jacobs counts the claim among the dogmaticians' errors; Westcott
   cites the Helvetic Consensus as a historical curiosity **[inferred]**.

What unites the defenders is Owen's worry, which Green names: that without
inspired points there is no "certainty in the interpretation of the Old
Testament." What unites the others is Calvin's evidence: the LXX's mistakes
show translators reading an unpointed text.

### 6. The Septuagint

The ancient inspiration of the Seventy (Philo, Irenaeus via Eusebius, Cyril,
Augustine) has no defender among the Protestant teachers. Ames: the seventy
did not "performe the office of a Prophet"; Whitaker: "nor is it the same thing to
be an interpreter and to be a prophet"; Owen will not let it "bear the weight"
of correcting the Hebrew. Yet none denies that the apostles quote it: Luther
says Paul "observed the translation of the Seventy," and Calvin that "the
Apostle followed the Greek translators." Their resolution is that the
apostles used the LXX where it gave the sense, which sanctions those
renderings, not the version **[inferred]**. Green and Cosin add a historical
argument the earlier writers lack: the Apocrypha came into the Greek Bible by
association, not because the Alexandrian Jews held a wider canon.

### 7. The Vulgate

The objection is to Trent's second decree, not to Jerome. Calvin lists the
Vulgate's errors; Whitaker spends most of Question II on them; Owen ranks it
"much the best" of the versions; Westcott notes that Trent itself put "no
anathema" on that clause. The Lutheran parallel is Hollaz on Luther's own
translation: efficacious for faith, but without canonical authority, because
canonical authority belongs to the words of the originals alone.

Included only through others, or not at all, because no public-domain English

- **Chemnitz** (*Examination of the Council of Trent*) and **Gerhard**
  (*Loci Theologici*): present only as quoted by Schmid. The English
  translations (Kramer, 1971; Concordia, 2006 onward) are under copyright. A
  1582 English partial translation of Chemnitz is not in the public
  transcriptions that could be reached.

  translation was found. His heading to the Apocrypha appears only as quoted
  in the Lutheran Cyclopedia.
- **Turretin** (*Institutes of Elenctic Theology*, Topic II), the **Leiden
  Synopsis**, **Polanus**, and the Latin vowel-point literature (Cappellus,
  the Buxtorfs, Heidegger): no public-domain English translation.

  Leigh**: not yet attempted.
- **Jerome's prefaces**: the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers series prints
  only summaries of several (Tobit and Judith, Chronicles, Ezra, Esther, the
  Prophets), so those are given as summaries.
- Partial texts: Owen's Epistle Dedicatory, the Preface and first three
  chapters of Gill's *Dissertation*, the opening chapters of Green's two
  volumes, and the later chapters of Alexander, Westcott and Whitaker
  (Questions IV–VI) are not included.
