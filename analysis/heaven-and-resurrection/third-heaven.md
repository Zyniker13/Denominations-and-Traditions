# The “Third Heaven”

Part of [Heaven, the New Earth and the Resurrection of the Body](README.md).

What did the Reformed teach about the “third heaven” of 2 Corinthians 12:2,
and about the heavens generally? How does that teaching stand against the
Greek of the verse, the Hebrew and Greek of the Old Testament, the
Lutheran dogmaticians, and the Jewish writers who also counted heavens?

The passages are printed in
[passages.md](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#the-heavens-and-the-third-heaven)
in Hebrew, the Septuagint and three Greek editions of the New Testament.
**[context]** marks a statement resting on history outside the texts;
**[inferred]** marks this study's own reading.

## Summary

1. **The Reformed writers agree that there are three heavens, and that the
   third is the highest: the heaven of the blessed, where Christ is in his
   human nature.** The first is the air, where the birds fly; the second is
   the heaven of the sun, moon and stars; the third is invisible. So Perkins
   (1595), Ussher (1645), Poole's *Annotations* (1685), Watson, Boston,
   Dickson and Edwards. The confessions do not number the heavens: they
   say “the highest heavens” (Westminster Confession 32.1; Larger
   Catechism 53, 86).
2. **They identify “paradise” in 2 Corinthians 12:4 with the third heaven of
   12:2**, and with the paradise promised to the thief (Luke 23:43): Poole,
   Edwards, Hodge. Calvin reads paradise as “the presence of God”.
3. **The scheme is drawn from Scripture's use of the word “heaven”, not
   stated by it.** The Greek of 2 Corinthians 12:2, ἕως τρίτου οὐρανοῦ, is an
   ordinal without an article and without a total: “as far as a third
   heaven”. Nothing in the verse says that the third is the last. The
   Reformed count rests on usage elsewhere (“the fowls of heaven”, “the
   stars of heaven”, “the heaven of heavens”).
4. **Calvin is reticent.** He will not “satisfy their curiosity” about the
   place, and he turns the passage against “that trifler Dionysius” and the
   scholastic speculation on the heavenly hierarchies.
5. **The Lutheran dogmaticians make the same threefold distinction**
   (Quenstedt: not “the aerial or sidereal heaven of nature” but “the
   residence, and home of the blessed”), and agree that heaven is “that
   certain place” (Gerhard). **They differ on what the place does to
   Christ's body**: for the Reformed (Ames, Wollebius, the Heidelberg
   Catechism) the ascension was a “real and local translation” to a heaven
   that is “not an *ubiquitary* Heaven”; for the Lutherans the heaven of
   the ascension “is not a circumscribed and physical locality”.
6. **The Jewish writers counted differently.** The Talmud gives two heavens
   (R. Judah, from Deuteronomy 10:14) or seven (Resh Lakish), and puts the
   souls of the righteous in the seventh, ʿaravot (Hagigah 12b). The
   Testament of Levi and 2 Enoch know seven heavens with paradise in the
   third, as the Greek *Apocalypse of Moses* has Adam taken “into paradise,
   even to the third heaven”. R. H. Charles (1896) concluded from 2 Enoch
   that Paul held seven heavens.
7. **[inferred]** The Jewish evidence supports the Reformed identification
   of the third heaven with paradise, and weakens the claim that the third
   heaven is the last. Neither count is stated by the text; the three-heaven
   count is drawn from Scripture's own vocabulary, the seven-heaven count
   from books outside it.

## The text

### 2 Corinthians 12:2–4

[Printed in full.](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#2cor-12-2-4)

> οἶδα ἄνθρωπον ἐν Χριστῷ … ἁρπαγέντα τὸν τοιοῦτον ἕως τρίτου οὐρανοῦ …
> ὅτι ἡρπάγη εἰς τὸν παράδεισον καὶ ἤκουσεν ἄρρητα ῥήματα (SBL)

- **ἕως τρίτου οὐρανοῦ.** ἕως gives the limit of the motion: “up to, as far
  as”. τρίτου is an ordinal; it implies a series counted from below and
  says how far up the series the man was taken. It does not give the
  number of the series. There is no article: “a third heaven”, as far as
  the Greek goes, or “the third” if the series is taken as known. The three
  editions agree here; their one difference in these verses, ἐκτὸς or
  χωρὶς τοῦ σώματος in verse 3, does not touch the heavens.
- **ἁρπάζω** is to seize and carry off, the verb of 1 Thessalonians 4:17
  (ἁρπαγησόμεθα, “shall be caught up”).
- **εἰς τὸν παράδεισον.** παράδεισος is the Septuagint's word for the garden
  of Eden (Gen. 2:8, παράδεισον ἐν Ἐδέμ, for גַּן־בְּעֵדֶן). In the New
  Testament it is used only here, of the place promised to the thief
  (Luke 23:43, [passage](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#luke-23-43)),
  and of the place of the tree of life (Rev. 2:7,
  [passage](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#rev-2-7)).
- **Verses 3–4 repeat verse 2** with “paradise” in place of “a third
  heaven”. Whether this is one rapture told twice, or a second rapture
  beyond the first, the grammar does not settle. Both readings had
  defenders among the Reformed, as Poole reports.

### The heavens elsewhere

- **“The heaven of heavens.”** Hebrew שְׁמֵי הַשָּׁמַיִם (Deut. 10:14; 1 Kings
  8:27; Neh. 9:6; Ps. 148:4) is a construct chain, the Hebrew superlative:
  the highest heavens. The Septuagint renders it in the singular in
  Deuteronomy, 1 Kings (3 Kingdoms) and 2 Esdras (ὁ οὐρανὸς τοῦ οὐρανοῦ)
  and in the plural in Psalm 148 (οἱ οὐρανοὶ τῶν οὐρανῶν). No text numbers
  the heavens. Two of them say that the heaven of heavens was made (Neh.
  9:6) and that it cannot contain God (1 Kings 8:27: Hebrew “cannot
  contain”, Greek “will not suffice”).
  ([Deut. 10:14](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#deut-10-14),
  [1 Kings 8:27](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#1kgs-8-27),
  [Neh. 9:6](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#neh-9-6),
  [Ps. 148:4](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#ps-148-4).)
- **“Above all the heavens.”** Christ ascended ὑπεράνω πάντων τῶν οὐρανῶν
  (Eph. 4:10), passed through the heavens, διεληλυθότα τοὺς οὐρανούς (Heb.
  4:14), and was made ὑψηλότερος τῶν οὐρανῶν (Heb. 7:26). The heavens are
  plural, are passed through, and are surpassed; how many they are is not
  said. ([Eph. 4:10](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#eph-4-10),
  [Heb. 4:14; 7:26](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#heb-4-14).)

## The Reformed teaching

### Three heavens, from Scripture's usage

The fullest statement is Perkins's, on “maker of heaven” in the Creed
([*Exposition of the Creed*](../../theologians/reformed/british/perkins/exposition-of-the-creed-last-things.md),
1595, p. 68). “Heaven” in Scripture “signifieth all that is aboue the
earth”, and “according to this acception of the worde, there are three
heauens, as Paul saith, *He was taken up into the third heauen.*” The first
is the air (“the foules of the heaven”, Gen. 7:11), the second holds the
sun, moon and stars (Gen. 1:14), and

> Besides these two heauens, there is a thirde which is inuisible: and yet
> it is the worke of Gods handes: and it is that glorious place where Christ
> in his manhoode sitteth at the right hande of the father: and whether the
> soules of the faithfull departed are carried, and placed: and in which at
> the end of the world shall all the elect both in bodie and soule, haue
> perfect ioy and blisse in the glorious sight and presence of God for euer.

Perkins adds that this third heaven is a creature, against those who
thought it “was neuer created, but was eternall with God himselfe”, and he
identifies it with the city Abraham looked for (Heb. 11:10): “the heauenly
Ierusalem, this thirde heauen”.

The others give the same three:

- **Ussher**, *A Body of Divinitie* (1645), p. 97, in catechism form: “How
  many heavens are mentioned in the Scriptures?” “Three”: the air, “the sky,
  wherein the Sun, the Moon, and the Starres are placed”, and “The third,
  wherein the Angles, and the soules of the Saints from hence departed are
  now in” (2 Cor. 12:2).
  ([text](../../theologians/reformed/british/ussher/body-of-divinity-heavens-and-last-things.md))
- **Poole's *Annotations*** (1685), on 2 Cor. 12:2: Paul means “the highest
  Heavens, where God most manifesteth his Glory”. Scripture “calleth all
  *Heaven* that is not Earth or Water”: an “*Aerial* Heaven”, a “*starry
  Heaven*”, and “then the *highest Heaven*; which is meant in the Lords
  Prayer … and is called the *Heaven of Heavens.* This is the Heaven here
  spoken of.”
  ([text](../../theologians/reformed/british/poole/annotations-last-things.md))
- **Watson**, *Body of Practical Divinity*, p. 477: “The *Caelum Empyraeum*
  the Empyraean Heaven, which *Paul* calls the third Heaven: *Vbi* Christ
  is, there is the Kingdom of Glory scituated.”
  ([text](../../theologians/reformed/british/watson/body-of-practical-divinity-resurrection-and-heaven.md))
- **Boston**, *Fourfold State* (1720), p. 303: “When *Paul* was caught up to
  the third heaven, the seat of the blessed, he *heard* there *unspeakable
  words*.”
  ([text](../../theologians/reformed/british/boston/fourfold-state-resurrection-and-heaven.md))
- **Dickson**, *Truths Victory over Error*, p. 329, on the Westminster
  Confession's “highest Heavens”: “*Paul* also calls it the *third Heaven;*
  2 *cor.* 12. 2. 4.”
  ([text](../../theologians/reformed/british/dickson/truths-victory-over-error-chapters-32-33.md))
- **Edwards**, *True Saints, when absent from the Body, are present with the
  Lord* (1747), p. 7: the highest heaven is “a Place beyond all the visible
  Heavens … This is the same which the Apostle calls *The third Heaven,* 2
  Cor. xii.2. reckoning the Aerial Heaven as the first, the starry Heaven as
  the second, and the highest Heaven as the third.”
  ([text](../../theologians/reformed/american/edwards/true-saints-present-with-the-lord.md))

The confessions name the place without counting: the souls of the righteous
“are received into the highest heavens” (Westminster Confession 32.1; the
Savoy Declaration 31.1 the same), and Christ “visibly went up into the
highest heavens” (Larger Catechism 53). The Second London Confession (1689)
changes the phrase of 32.1 to “received into paradise, where they are with
Christ”. See [confessions.md](confessions.md).

### Paradise is the third heaven

- **Poole**, on 2 Cor. 12:4: “Some by *Paradise* understand a place distinct
  from the *third Heaven* before mentioned; and think the Apostle here
  speaks of more Visions than one. But they speak much more probably, who
  interpret it of the third Heaven before mentioned: called *Paradise,* in
  regard of the delight and pleasures of it.” He joins Luke 23:43 and Rev.
  2:7.
- **Edwards**, *True Saints*, p. 9: “Paradise is the same with the third
  Heaven; as appears by 2 Cor. xii.2, 3, 4.”
- **Hodge**, *Systematic Theology* III (1872), p. 727: “In 2 Corinthians xii.
  4, Paul says he was caught up into paradise, which he explains by saying
  that he was caught up into the third heaven.”
  ([text](../../theologians/reformed/princeton/hodge/systematic-theology-vol-3-last-things.md))
- **Calvin**, on 2 Cor. 12:4 (p. 369), takes “paradise” for “the heavenly
  glory”, and the promise to the thief as “Thou shalt enjoy the presence of
  God, in the condition and life of the blessed.”
  ([text](../../theologians/reformed/continental/calvin/commentaries-on-the-last-things.md))

### Calvin's restraint

Calvin, alone among these writers, declines to describe the place. On the
rapture: “Some enquire, also, as to the place, but it does not belong to us
to satisfy their curiosity” (p. 367). He takes the ascent as a degree of
revelation: “For our faith scales heaven and enters it, and those that are
superior to others in knowledge get higher in degree and elevation, but to
reach the *third heavens* has been granted to very few” (p. 368). And he
turns the “unspeakable words” against speculation: from curiosity and
ambition “has sprung up a great part of scholastic theology, and every
thing, which that trifler Dionysius has been so daring as to contrive in
reference to the Heavenly Hierarchies” (p. 370). On the place of departed
souls in the *Institutes* (III.25.6, p. 618): “As to the place of abode, the
question is not less futile and inept.” On Philippians 3:20 he steers
between two errors: “as it were rash and foolish to mount up beyond the
heavens, and assign to Christ a station, or seat, or place of walking, in
this or that region, so it is a foolish and destructive madness to draw him
down from heaven by any carnal consideration” (p. 110).

**[inferred]** Calvin holds that Christ's body is in heaven and not on
earth, as the Reformed after him did, but he does not build the heavens
into a cosmology. Perkins, Ussher and Poole are bolder in classifying the
heavens; none of them goes beyond the three that Scripture's vocabulary
gives.

### A place, and not everywhere

The Reformed insist that the third heaven is a place, because Christ's
body is in it.

- **Ames**, *Marrow* (1642), I.23 §22, p. 108: “The place into which he
  ascended, was the Heaven of the blessed, and which is not an
  *ubiquitary* Heaven, as some doe imagine, so as that ascension should
  only be a change of condition, and not of place, but it is the highest
  above all the other Heavens, *Eph.* 4. 10.”
  ([text](../../theologians/reformed/continental/ames/marrow-of-sacred-divinity-exaltation-glorification-end-of-world.md))
- **Wollebius**, *Abridgment* (1626; English 1660), I.XIX, p. 161: “The form
  consisteth in Christs real and local translation from this World to the
  highest Heaven.”
  ([text](../../theologians/reformed/continental/wollebius/abridgment-of-christian-divinity-last-things.md))
- **The Heidelberg Catechism** 47: “with respect to his human nature, he is
  no more on earth” … “with respect to his Godhead, majesty, grace and
  spirit, he is at no time absent from us”. Question 49: “we have our flesh
  in heaven as a sure pledge”.

## The Lutheran dogmaticians

Schmid's compendium
([text](../../theologians/lutheran/heinrich-schmid/doctrinal-theology-last-things.md))
shows the Lutherans making the same distinction of heavens, and drawing a
different conclusion from it. Quenstedt (p. 402) says the heaven into which
Christ ascended

> is not the aerial or sidereal heaven of nature, for to think of this here
> is irreverent; nor the heaven of grace … but the residence, and home of
> the blessed, where He presents Himself to the blessed for them to look upon
> Him face to face … The goal, properly speaking, is ὑπεράνω πάντων οὐρανῶν
> (above all heavens), Eph. 4: 10, viz., at the very right hand of God.

Gerhard defines heaven as “that certain place (ποῦ) … in which the blessed
will see God” (p. 662). But Gerhard denies that Christ passed through the
spheres “until in the progress of time He came to His Father in the
empyreal heaven, in which, residing in a local and bodily manner, He is held
restrained from being present upon earth in an invisible and illocal manner
before the day of judgment” (p. 403). And Schmid concludes: “We have above
proved that Christ ascended to the where of the blessed. But since this is
not a circumscribed and physical locality, the ascension itself is not a
local and physical passing over to it” (p. 402). The Formula of Concord
says the right hand of God “is no fixed place in heaven” (Solid Declaration
VIII.28).

**[inferred]** The two traditions divide the heavens alike. They divide on
whether the highest heaven contains and limits the body of Christ, which is
the question of the Lord's Supper carried into cosmology. The Lutheran
sources gathered here do not discuss the number of heavens in 2 Corinthians
12. See [lutheran-and-reformed.md](lutheran-and-reformed.md).

## Jewish reckonings: two heavens, three, or seven

**The Talmud** (Hagigah 12b, Streane's translation, p. 63;
[text](../../theologians/rabbinic/mishnah-and-talmud-on-the-world-to-come.md)):
“R. Jehudah said, There are two firmaments”, citing Deuteronomy 10:14,
“Behold, unto the Lord thy God belong the heaven and the heaven of heavens.”
“Resh Laḳish said, There are seven” … “Vilon, Raḳia‘, Sh’chaḳim, Z’bul,
Ma‘on, Makhon, Araboth.” The seventh, Araboth, holds “the souls of the
righteous … and the dew with which the Holy One, blessed be He, is about to
quicken mortals” (p. 65). Hagigah 14b tells that “Four men went up into
Paradise” (p. 83), the *pardes*, of whom only R. Akiba came out unharmed.

**The Second Temple writings**
([text](../../theologians/jewish-antiquity/second-temple-on-heaven-and-resurrection.md)):

- The **Testament of Levi** 2–3: Levi passes from the first heaven into the
  second, and sees “a third heaven far brighter than those two”; the angel
  tells him he will see “four other heavens brighter than these” (p. 13).
- The Greek **Apocalypse of Moses** (“Revelation of Moses”): God commands
  Michael, “Raise him into paradise, even to the third heaven, and let him be
  there until that great and dreadful day”, and later, “Go into paradise,
  into the third heaven” (pp. 569–570).
- **2 Enoch** VIII: “And these men took me from thence, and brought me to the
  third heaven, and placed me in the midst of a garden” (p. 7), the garden of
  the tree of life. In the same heaven, to the north, is the place of
  torment. The Lord sits on his throne in the seventh heaven (Charles, p. xxxviii).
- **R. H. Charles**, introduction to 2 Enoch (1896): “the location of
  Paradise in this heaven agrees with the Pauline account 2 Cor. xii. 2, 3”
  (p. xxxvii); “Heretofore exegetes have been divided as to whether St.
  Paul believed in the existence of three heavens or of seven. Owing to the
  fresh evidence on the subject furnished by the Slavonic Enoch there is no
  longer room for reasonable doubt on the question” (p. xl). He reckons
  that “according to later Judaism” paradise belonged to the fourth heaven,
  and calls the Talmud's description of the seven heavens “puerile in the
  extreme” (p. xxxix).

## Assessment

**What the text establishes.** That Paul was taken “as far as a third
heaven” and “into paradise”, and that he could not say whether in the body
or out of it. The parallel of verses 2 and 4 favours, without proving,
that the two names describe one place. Elsewhere the Old Testament knows a
“heaven of heavens” which God made and which cannot contain him, and the New
Testament a plurality of heavens above which Christ ascended.

**The Reformed count.** **[inferred]** The three heavens of Perkins and
Poole are drawn from the uses of the one word *heaven* (שָׁמַיִם, οὐρανός): the
birds of heaven, the stars of heaven, the heaven of heavens. That is an
argument from Scripture's own vocabulary, and it gives a ready sense to “a
third”. But it is an inference, and the verse does not say that the third
heaven is the last. Perkins's “there are three heauens, as Paul saith” puts
more into Paul's mouth than the Greek holds.

**The seven-heaven reading.** **[inferred]** Charles's case rests on 2 Enoch
and the Testament of Levi, books outside the text. It shows that a Jew of
Paul's time could speak of paradise in a third heaven with more heavens
above, and that is real evidence for how the phrase could be heard. It is
not evidence from the text, and nothing in the New Testament counts seven
heavens; Revelation, which counts nearly everything, does not count them.
On the rule that the Greek is read as the text itself, the number of the
heavens is left open.

**What the Jewish evidence does settle.** **[inferred]** The Apocalypse of
Moses and 2 Enoch both put paradise in the third heaven. In the literature
nearest to Paul, then, “third heaven” and “paradise” name the same place,
which supports the Reformed identification of 12:2 with 12:4 against those
Poole mentions who distinguished them. The same literature tells against
the Reformed assumption that the third heaven is the highest. The Talmud
agrees with the Westminster Confession on one point: the souls of the
righteous are in the uppermost heaven, ʿaravot, as the Confession puts them
in “the highest heavens”.

**Between the Reformed and the Lutherans.** **[inferred]** Both reject a
heaven of the blessed that is merely a state, and both deny that God is
contained by any heaven (1 Kings 8:27). The difference is Christological:
whether Christ's human body is held in the highest heaven as in a place.
The texts that speak of his place (Acts 3:21, “whom the heaven must
receive”; Heb. 4:14; Eph. 4:10) are cited by both sides and decide less than
either side claims.
