# The Reformed Position in Light of the Jewish Sources

Part of [Heaven, the New Earth and the Resurrection of the Body](README.md).

How does the Reformed teaching on the heavens, the final home of the
Christian, the new heavens and earth, and the resurrection of the body look
when it is set beside the Jewish reading of the same Hebrew Bible? The Jewish
witnesses gathered here are the Second Temple writers and Josephus, the
Mishnah and the Talmud, the Midrash and the synagogue prayers, Maimonides and
his critic the Raavad, and the heirs of the Haskalah in Reform Judaism. Where
do they and the Reformed agree, where do they part, and which keeps closer to
the Hebrew? The last section before the assessment asks the same of the
Lutheran dogmaticians.

(with [the Midrash and prayer book](../../theologians/rabbinic/midrash-and-prayer-book-on-the-resurrection.md)),
[theologians/jewish-antiquity/](../../theologians/jewish-antiquity/second-temple-on-heaven-and-resurrection.md),

## Summary

1. **On the resurrection of the body the Reformed stand with rabbinic
   Judaism, and against every party that gave it up.** The Mishnah denies a
   share in the world to come to “He who says that there is no allusion in
   the Torah concerning resurrection”; the daily *Amidah* blesses God “who
   quickenest the dead”; the thirteenth of the principles drawn from
   Maimonides confesses “a resurrection of the dead”. On the other side
   stand the Sadducees, Maimonides' bodiless world to come, and the Reform
   rabbis of 1869 and 1885.
2. **Both find the doctrine in the Torah, and by the same kind of
   argument.** R. Simai's proof from Exodus 6:4 (“It does not read ‘to you’
   … but ‘to them’”) and Rabban Gamaliel's from Deuteronomy 11 rest on God's
   promise to the patriarchs, who are dead. So does Jesus' proof from Exodus
   3:6 in Matthew 22:32, which Perkins and Hodge take up, and Paul's appeal
   before Agrippa to the hope of the promise made to the fathers (Acts
   26:6–8).
3. **They describe the raised body alike: the same body, changed.** The grain
   of wheat that is buried naked and “comes out dressed”, the dead raised
   “with the blemishes they had in their life, and thereafter … cured”, and
   the body raised from what remains of it, all keep the body and change its
   state. That is the Westminster Confession's “the self-same bodies, and
   none other, although with different qualities”. Paul's γυμνὸν κόκκον
   (1 Cor. 15:37) is the rabbis' grain.
4. **They count the heavens by the same method and reach different numbers.**
   Resh Lakish's seven heavens, like Perkins's three, are drawn from the
   Hebrew Bible's words for heaven, each with its proof text. R. Judah's two
   come from “the heaven and the heaven of heavens” (Deut. 10:14), the verse
   behind the Reformed “highest heaven”. The Talmud puts the souls of the
   righteous in the highest heaven, as the Westminster Confession does.
5. **The rabbinic hope is of the land and of a renewed world.** “All Israel
   has a share in the world to come” is proved from “for ever shall they
   possess the land” (Isa. 60:21); “The heavens and the earth are destined to
   pass away and to be renewed” (Pirke de R. Eliezer 51). This is nearer to
   the later Reformed (Ussher, Poole, Hodge) than to the older (Boston,
   Edwards), and nearer to both than to Maimonides, who makes the world
   permanent and the world to come bodiless.
6. **The rabbinic order of the last things has a stage that the Reformed
   deny: the days of the Messiah.** Glazer, summarizing the commentators on
   Maimonides, counts “four distinct eras”: this world, the Messianic era,
   the resurrection, the world to come. Calvin rejects the Christian form of
   that stage as the Chiliasts' “fiction”, and the Augsburg Confession
   condemns it as “certain Jewish opinions”. Goodwin's thousand-year reign of
   the raised saints is the one Reformed parallel in this collection.
7. **The Haskalah and Reform gave up the body and the land together.**
   Mendelssohn's *Phædon* (1767) argued for the soul's immortality from
   reason alone. The Philadelphia Conference (1869) declared that “The
   belief in the bodily resurrection has no religious foundation”;
   Pittsburgh (1885) rejected it and expected “neither a return to
   Palestine” nor the restored Jewish state; Kohler (1918) called the
   resurrection a national hope, “obsolete for the modern Jew”. The Reformed
   had answered the Christian forms of the same position: Calvin against
   those who “hold that they will be clothed with new bodies, and thus deny
   the resurrection of the flesh”, Hodge against those who make the only
   resurrection that of the soul at death.
8. **On two points the Lutheran dogmaticians are further from the Jewish
   sources than the Reformed are**: the annihilation of the world, which no
   Jewish source here teaches, and the “Illocality” of the glorified body.
   On chiliasm the Augsburg Confession and the Reformed agree, and both
   describe the Jewish order correctly.

## The order of the last things

Simon Glazer, in a note to his translation of the *Mishneh Torah* (1927,
p. 434, n. 7), gives the order that the commentators on Maimonides agreed
on: “The dead will be resurrected. Life will then go on for a time. Then
life as we know it will cease completely, when the era of the World to Come
will be inaugurated. In all there are four distinct eras: (1) this world, as
at present; (2) Messianic era; (3) era of Resurrection; (4) era of the World
to Come.” The Reformed confessions have three: this life, the soul with
Christ until his coming, and the resurrection, judgment and everlasting
life.

| Stage | The rabbis and the commentators on Maimonides | The Reformed confessions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| At death | The soul “returns to the place whence it was given, from heaven” (Pirke de R. Eliezer 34, p. 257); the souls of the righteous are in ʿaravot, the highest heaven, “bound in the bundle of life” (Hagigah 12b, p. 65) | The souls, “which neither die nor sleep”, “immediately return to God who gave them”; the righteous “are received into the highest heavens” (Westminster 32.1) |
| The days of the Messiah | An age on this earth before the resurrection. Samuel holds “that there will be no difference between this time and the time of Messiah, except that Israel will no longer be under the dominion of foreigners” (Sanhedrin 91b, p. 273); Maimonides follows him (Kings XII.1–2) | None. Calvin: the Chiliasts' “fiction is too puerile to need or to deserve refutation” (*Institutes* III.25.5, p. 615). Goodwin's thousand years (below) are the exception |
| The resurrection | Of Israel, of the righteous, or of all: the rabbis differ (*Jewish Encyclopedia*); in the land of Israel (Kohler) | Of “all the dead”, just and unjust, at Christ's coming (Westminster 32.2–3; Belgic 37) |
| The world to come | “neither eating nor drinking; no procreation of children” (Rav, Berakhot 17a); bodiless for Maimonides, embodied for the Raavad | Body and soul together, “in the immediate vision and fruition of God” (Larger Catechism 90) |

**The middle stage.** **[inferred]** The difference between the tables is
the Messiah. For the rabbis he is to come, and his days are a stage of this
world's history before the end. For the New Testament he has come and has
risen, ἀπαρχὴ τῶν κεκοιμημένων, and the order of the
resurrection is ἀπαρχὴ Χριστός, ἔπειτα οἱ Χριστοῦ ἐν τῇ παρουσίᾳ αὐτοῦ
(1 Cor. 15:20, 23): Christ, then his people at his coming, with no third
term between. The Reformed confessions keep that order. The Augsburg
Confession (XVII) condemns “certain Jewish opinions, that before the
resurrection of the dead the godly shall take possession of the kingdom of
the world, the ungodly being everywhere suppressed.” As a description of the
Jewish order it is exact: the Messianic age comes before the resurrection in
Glazer's list. Maimonides' second class of thinkers held that in the days
of the Messiah “all men will be kings for ever. Their bodily frames will be
mighty and they will inhabit the whole earth unto eternity” (Abelson,
p. 29).

**Goodwin.** Thomas Goodwin's *The World to Come* (1655) reads the phrase
of Hebrews 2:5, τὴν οἰκουμένην τὴν μέλλουσαν, as the Messiah's world: the
Apostle cites Psalm 8 “to convince these *Hebrews,* that that Psalm was
meant of the Messiah whom they expected” (p. 11). In it the raised saints
reign on earth for a thousand years, and Israel is restored: “*Rom.* 11. 26.
*All Israel shall be saved.* There he tels us of *a new world* of the Jewes”
(p. 29). **[inferred]** This is the nearest any Reformed writer gathered here
comes to the rabbinic order, with the difference that Goodwin's kingdom
follows a first resurrection (Rev. 20:5) where the rabbis' Messianic age
precedes the resurrection. Kohler's “double resurrection” (below) is closer
still.

**“The world to come.”** The rabbinic phrase הָעוֹלָם הַבָּא is ambiguous in
the Jewish sources themselves. Maimonides takes it for the soul's life after
death, which exists already: “it is not because it is not now in existence,
so that it might be inferred that this world will be destroyed and thereafter
will come that world” (Repentance VIII.8, p. 437). The Raavad and the
commentators take it for the age after the resurrection; Glazer: “Evidently
the RABD erred in placing the World to Come instead of resurrection”
(p. 431, n. 3). **[inferred]** The New Testament's coming age (ἐν τῷ μέλλοντι,
Matt. 12:32 and Eph. 1:21; μέλλοντος αἰῶνος, Heb. 6:5) is tied to the
resurrection: Luke 20:35 speaks of those counted worthy τοῦ αἰῶνος ἐκείνου
τυχεῖν καὶ τῆς ἀναστάσεως τῆς ἐκ νεκρῶν, *to obtain that age and the
resurrection from the dead*. On this the Reformed stand with the Raavad, not with Maimonides.

## The Hebrew Bible that both read

### The heavens

The Hebrew word, שָׁמַיִם, is plural in form, and Deuteronomy 10:14 sets
הַשָּׁמַיִם beside שְׁמֵי הַשָּׁמָיִם, “the heaven of heavens”. R. Judah reads
two heavens from the verse: “There are two firmaments, as it is said,
… ‘Behold, unto the Lord thy God belong the heaven and the heaven of
heavens.’” (Hagigah 12b, Streane, p. 63). The Reformed read the construct
chain as a superlative, the highest heaven above the visible heavens (see
[third-heaven.md](third-heaven.md)).

Resh Lakish counts seven, each a Hebrew word: וילון רקיע שחקים זבול מעון
מכון ערבות, “Vilon, Raḳia‘, Sh’chaḳim, Z’bul, Ma‘on, Makhon, Araboth”. Of
the upper heavens the Gemara asks, “And whence do we get it that it is
called heaven?” (p. 64), and answers from a verse that sets the word beside
שָׁמַיִם: Z’bul from Isaiah 63:15, Ma‘on from Deuteronomy 26:15, Makhon from 1
Kings 8:39. For Araboth it joins the one who “rideth” upon it in Psalm 68:4
to the one who “rideth upon the heaven” in Deuteronomy 33:26.

**[inferred]** This is the Reformed method. Perkins, Ussher and Poole count
three heavens from the uses of the one word *heaven* (the birds of heaven,
the stars of heaven, the heaven of heavens); Resh Lakish counts seven from
the different words for it. Both are counts drawn from Scripture's
vocabulary, and the Hebrew states neither number. The Talmud's seventh
heaven holds “the souls of the righteous”, as the Westminster Confession's
“highest heavens” do; it also holds “the dew with which the Holy One,
blessed be He, is about to quicken mortals” (p. 65), the dew of Isaiah
26:19.

Of the four who “went up into Paradise” (Hagigah 14b, p. 83), the Tosaphoth,
as Streane reports them, say “and they did not go up literally, but it
appeared to them as if they went up” (p. 83, n. 2). Paul leaves the same
question open of himself: εἴτε ἐν σώματι οὐκ οἶδα· εἴτε ἐκτὸς τοῦ σώματος
οὐκ οἶδα (2 Cor. 12:2). The Second Temple books that put paradise in the third
heaven (the Apocalypse of Moses, 2 Enoch) and Charles's argument from them
are treated in [third-heaven.md](third-heaven.md).

### The resurrection

The Hebrew Bible has no noun for resurrection; the rabbinic תְּחִיַּת הַמֵּתִים,
*the reviving of the dead*, is built from the verb of Isaiah 26:19, יִחְיוּ
מֵתֶיךָ, “Thy dead men shall live”. Kaufmann Kohler (1918) finds “no trace of
such a belief in the entire Bible save for two late post-exilic passages”
(p. 392), Isaiah 26:19 and Daniel 12:2. The Mishnah takes the opposite view
and makes it an article: the Hebrew is האומר אין תחיית המתים מן התורה,
*he who says, There is no reviving of the dead from the Torah*, which
Rodkinson renders “He who says that there is no allusion in the Torah
concerning resurrection” (p. 265). The Gemara gives the reason: “He denies
resurrection, therefore he will not have a share in it, as punishment
corresponds to the deed” (p. 265).

The Gemara then asks, “Where is resurrection hinted at in the Torah?”
(p. 266), and gathers answers. It also tests them:

- From the tense. Exodus 15:1, אָז יָשִׁיר, “It does not read ‘sang,’ but will
  sing (yoshir). This is a hint of resurrection in the Torah” (R. Meir,
  pp. 273–274). The Gemara objects that the same form is found in 1 Kings 11:7,
  “and nevertheless it does not mean in the future, but in the past”
  (p. 274).
- From the division of the words. Deuteronomy 31:16, “Thou shalt sleep with
  thy parents ‘ve-qom,’”—“and arise.” The Minim answer: “Perhaps this word
  ve-qom is connected with its succeeding words” (p. 267). So the Hebrew
  verse runs, and so the Aramaic text of the objection quotes it: וְקָם
  הָעָם הַזֶּה וְזָנָה, *and this people will rise up and go astray*.
- From the promise. R. Simai on Exodus 6:4, לָתֵת לָהֶם אֶת־אֶרֶץ כְּנָעַן: “It
  does not read ‘to you’ (as it should, the patriarchs of that time being
  already dead), but ‘to them’—hence this is a hint that they would be
  restored” (pp. 266–267). Rabban Gamaliel's proof from the same words in
  Deuteronomy 11 (Rodkinson prints xi. 9; the Hebrew text of the Talmud
  cites 11:21, and both verses read לָתֵת לָהֶם) was the one the Minim
  “accepted” (p. 268).
- From the identity of the subject. Deuteronomy 32:39, “I make one die and I
  make one alive … I wound and I heal”: “As wounding and curing apply to one
  person only, the same is the case with death and life—they apply to one
  person” (p. 273).

**[inferred]** The proofs that survive the Gemara's own testing are those
from the promise and from the one subject. These are the two that the New
Testament and the Reformed use. Jesus' answer to the Sadducees (Matt.
22:31–32) is τὸ ῥηθὲν ὑμῖν ὑπὸ τοῦ Θεοῦ, *what was spoken to you by God*,
in Exodus 3:6: Ἐγώ εἰμι
ὁ Θεὸς Ἀβραάμ … Οὐκ ἔστιν ὁ Θεὸς Θεὸς νεκρῶν, ἀλλὰ ζώντων (see
[divine-quotations](../../scripture/divine-quotations/quotations.md#matthew-2232--exodus-36)).
The Hebrew of Exodus 3:6 has no verb (אָנֹכִי אֱלֹהֵי אָבִיךָ אֱלֹהֵי
אַבְרָהָם); the Septuagint and Matthew supply εἰμι. The argument as Matthew
gives it does not rest on a tense but on the relation: God is still the God
of the patriarchs, and he is not the God of the dead. Paul argues from the
same promise before Agrippa: ἐπ᾿ ἐλπίδι τῆς πρὸς τοὺς πατέρας ἐπαγγελίας,
and then, Τί ἄπιστον κρίνεται παρ᾿ ὑμῖν, εἰ ὁ Θεὸς νεκροὺς ἐγείρει; (Acts
26:6, 8). Hebrews 11:16 closes on the same words: οὐκ ἐπαισχύνεται αὐτοὺς ὁ
Θεός, Θεὸς ἐπικαλεῖσθαι αὐτῶν. R. Simai's “to them” and Jesus' “I am the
God of Abraham” are one argument: God's covenant word to the dead
patriarchs cannot fail them.

The Reformed take it so. Perkins (p. 521): “This argument Christ useth
against the Sadduces, which denied the resurrection”; God is the God of
Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, “which are dead, and therfore they must rise
againe.” Hodge is more careful (p. 771): “All that this passage directly
proves is that the dead continue alive after the dissolution of the body.”
**[inferred]** The rabbinic form of the argument, from the promise of the
land, reaches the body more directly than Hodge's reading of Jesus' form
does: land is given to bodies.

**Ezekiel 37.** All three traditions read the valley of dry bones as a figure
of Israel's return, as verse 11 does: הָעֲצָמוֹת הָאֵלֶּה כָּל־בֵּית יִשְׂרָאֵל
הֵמָּה, *these bones are the whole house of Israel*. They differ on what the
figure presupposes.

- The Talmud, in the school of Elijah, holds with those who say “that in
  reality Ezekiel did not restore any dead at all, and the whole prophecy
  was only a parable for the Jewish nation that it would be restored again”
  (Sanhedrin 92b, p. 278). Others in the same passage held that the dead
  were raised.
- Calvin (*Institutes* III.25.4, p. 614): “Though under that figure he
  encourages the people to hope for return, yet the ground of hope is taken
  from the resurrection, as it is the special type of all the deliverances
  which believers experience in this world.”
- Kohler (pp. 392–393): the vision “gave more definite shape to the picture,
  although in the form of allegory. As the prophet himself says, he aimed to
  describe the resurrection of Judah and Israel from their grave of exile.”
  For Kohler the national figure is the origin of the doctrine; for Calvin
  the doctrine is the ground of the figure.

### “Inherit the land”

The Mishnah's proof that “All Israel has a share in the world to come” is
Isaiah 60:21, לְעוֹלָם יִירְשׁוּ אָרֶץ, “for ever shall they possess the land”
(p. 265). The *Jewish Encyclopedia* (1905): “The very term used to express
the idea of sharing in the future life is ‘to inherit the land’”, citing
Matthew 5:5 after Psalm 37:11. The Psalm's וַעֲנָוִים יִירְשׁוּ־אָרֶץ becomes
in the Greek κληρονομήσουσι γῆν and in Matthew κληρονομήσουσι τὴν γῆν, and
Paul widens the promise to Abraham to τὸ κληρονόμον αὐτὸν εἶναι τοῦ κόσμου,
*that he should be heir of the world* (Rom. 4:13). Kohler: “The Jewish
belief in resurrection is intimately bound up with the hope for the
restoration of the Israelitish nation on its own soil, and consequently
rather national; indeed, originally purely local and territorial” (p. 392).

**[inferred]** Kohler is right that in the rabbinic sources the resurrection
and the land belong together; R. Simai's proof joins them. The New
Testament keeps the joining and widens the land to the earth and the world.
The Reformed who make the renewed earth the final home (Ussher, Poole,
Hodge) keep it too; those who make the highest heaven the final home
(Boston, Edwards) keep the resurrection of the body and lose the land.

## The soul between death and the resurrection

| | The Jewish sources | The Reformed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where the soul goes | “The soul goes forth and returns to the place whence it was given, from heaven, as it is said, ‘And the soul returns unto God who gave it’” (Eccl. 12:7; Pirke de R. Eliezer 34, p. 257) | “immediately return to God who gave them” (Westminster 32.1, citing Eccl. 12:7) |
| Whether it sleeps | “Just as the Holy One, blessed be He, has no sleep in His presence, so the soul does not sleep” (p. 259) | “(which neither die nor sleep)” (Westminster 32.1); the elect departed are at rest, “not that they will sleep and come to any certain oblivion” (Scots 17) |
| The place of the righteous | ʿAravot, the highest heaven (Hagigah 12b) | “the highest heavens” (Westminster 32.1); “paradise” (Second London 31.1) |
| The body meanwhile | “Blessed art thou, O Lord, who restorest souls unto dead bodies” (*Elohai neshamah*, Singer, p. 5); the *Amidah*: “keepest thy faith to them that sleep in the dust” (p. 45) | The bodies, “being still united to Christ, do rest in their graves till the resurrection” (Shorter Catechism 37) |

The Second Temple sources are not one. The *Jewish Encyclopedia* says of
the apocalypses: “All these believed that the soul would sleep in Sheol till
the judgment, but several Alexandrian writers … held, like Ps. xlix. and
lxxiii., that the spirits of the righteous entered on a blessed immortality
immediately at death.” Of the Psalms it says that their view “made a
resurrection unnecessary”. Josephus has the Pharisees place “under the
earth” the rewards and punishments (*Antiquities* XVIII.1.3), and in his
own speech at Jotapata the souls of the righteous “obtain a most holy place
in heaven” (*War* III.8.5).

**[inferred]** The Reformed side with Pirke de R. Eliezer and the Talmud: the
soul goes at once to God and does not sleep, and the body waits for it.
They refuse the choice that the *Jewish Encyclopedia* sets out, between a
soul that waits asleep for the resurrection and a soul whose immediate bliss
makes the resurrection “unnecessary”. Westminster 32.1 holds both halves:
the soul in “the highest heavens … waiting for the full redemption of their
bodies”. Both traditions also warn against curiosity here. Calvin: “to pry
curiously into their intermediate state is neither lawful nor expedient”
(*Institutes* III.25.6, p. 617). Maimonides: “Man ought not to study much
the words of the Agadoth, neither dilate on the exposition regarding the
Messianic advent, as they do not lead to any fear or love of the Lord”
(Kings XII.2).

## The world to come and the final home

Rav's saying (Berakhot 17a, Cohen, p. 112): “In the world to come there is
neither eating nor drinking; no procreation of children or business
transactions; no envy or hatred or rivalry; but the righteous sit enthroned,
their crowns on their heads, and enjoy the lustre of the *Shᵉkīnāh*”. Cohen
compares Matthew 22:30, where the raised are ὡς ἄγγελοι, “as the angels”, in
not marrying. Charles notes that this was not the only rabbinic view: “a
materialistic one prevailed not only popularly, but also in Rabbinic
circles. According to the latter the blessed should beget children and eat
the flesh of the Leviathan” (2 Baruch, p. 82).

Maimonides reads Rav's crowns as “the preservation of the soul in the
intellectual sphere” and the lustre of the Shekhinah as the soul's knowledge
of God (Abelson, p. 39); his world to come “harbors neither body nor aught
of a concrete form” (Repentance VIII.2, p. 431). The Raavad answers that
the crowns may be “literally and naturally, not in similitude” (p. 431,
n. 3).

**[inferred]** Rav's world to come, without eating or marriage, with the
righteous enjoying the presence of God, is close to the Reformed account of
heaven: the Larger Catechism's “immediate vision and fruition of God”
(Q. 90). The difference is whether the righteous have bodies there. Rav
does not say; Maimonides says no; the Raavad and the Reformed say yes, and
the Larger Catechism says it in so many words: “made perfectly holy and
happy both in body and soul”. Maimonides describes that very view, to
dismiss it: “Some hold, in accordance with the literal meaning of the
Midrashim, that the bodies of the pious will also enjoy everlasting
happiness. Their notion is like the well-known belief of certain people,
that there are bodily enjoyments in Paradise” (*Guide* II.27, p. 202).

Where the final home is, the rabbinic answer is the land, and 1 Enoch's is
the earth transformed: “I will transform the earth and make it a blessing:
And I will cause Mine elect ones to dwell upon it” (45:5). 2 Baruch puts the
righteous “in the heights of that world” (51:10). Maimonides' Garden of Eden
is “a fertile spot on the earth's sphere rich in streams and fruits”
(Abelson, p. 41), on this side of the world to come. The Reformed answers,
the highest heaven and the renewed earth, are compared with these in
[final-destination.md](final-destination.md).

## The resurrection of the body

### The same body

The rabbinic images are set out in
[resurrection-of-the-flesh.md](resurrection-of-the-flesh.md#the-jewish-teachers).
Each keeps the identity of the body and changes its condition:

- R. Meir's wheat: “A grain of wheat which is buried naked comes out dressed
  in so many garments” (Sanhedrin 90b, p. 268). Paul: καὶ ὃ σπείρεις, οὐ τὸ
  σῶμα τὸ γενησόμενον σπείρεις, ἀλλὰ γυμνὸν κόκκον (1 Cor. 15:37).
- Resh Lakish: “They will be restored with the blemishes they had in their
  life, and thereafter they will be cured” (Sanhedrin 91b, p. 273).
- 2 Baruch: the earth will restore the dead “making no change in their
  form”, so that they are recognized; afterwards the righteous “will
  respectively be transformed … into the splendour of angels” (50:2; 51:5).
  Charles: “The object with which the dead are raised is for common
  recognition. There is nothing corresponding to this in the N.T.” (pp.
  82–83).
- Pirke de R. Eliezer 34: all the body crumbles “until nothing remains of
  the body except a spoonful of earthy matter”, which, “like the yeast which
  is mixed with the dough, improves and increases, and it raises up all the
  body” (p. 258). Bereshit Rabbah 28:3: the *luz*, “the sacrum, from which
  the Holy One blessed be He will cause man to sprout in the future”, which
  could not be ground, dissolved or broken.
- The Raavad, against Maimonides: the sages taught that the righteous rise
  “in their garments”, that they “will never again return unto dust, but
  remain standing in their concrete form”, and “They will rise in their
  deformities, but will be healed”; and yet “it is possible that the Creator
  will set them in strong, healthy forms as are the forms of angels, and as
  is the form of Elijah” (Glazer, p. 431, n. 3).

**[inferred]** This is the Reformed formula, “the self-same bodies, and none
other, although with different qualities” (Westminster 32.2), and Calvin's
“the substance indeed is the same, but there is a difference as to
quality”. The rabbis also asked the question that divided the Reformed:
what makes the raised body the same? Perkins and Boston answered, the same
particles; Hodge (p. 777) allowed “an identity independent of sameness of
substance”. The *luz* and the spoonful of earth stand between: a material
link with the body that died, from which a body grows that is not made of
all the same matter. That is nearer to Paul's seed than either Reformed
answer is.

### Josephus's “other bodies” and Calvin's “new bodies”

Josephus reports that the Pharisees “say that all souls are incorruptible,
but that the souls of good men only are removed into other bodies” (*War*
II.8.14), and in his speech at Jotapata that the souls of the righteous,
“in the revolutions of ages, they are again sent into pure bodies” (*War*
III.8.5). Calvin describes the same teaching as an error: “others, admitting
that spirits are immortal, hold that they will be clothed with new bodies,
and thus deny the resurrection of the flesh” (*Institutes* III.25.6,
p. 616). Against it he sets 1 Corinthians 15:53, where Paul, “pointing with
the finger to the bodies with which we are clothed, and promising that they
shall be incorruptible, he very plainly affirms that no new bodies are to
be fabricated” (p. 619).

The *Jewish Encyclopedia* doubts that Josephus reports the Pharisees fairly:
“Both the Pharisees and the Essenes believed in the resurrection of the
body, Josephus' philosophical construction of their belief to suit the
taste of his Roman readers notwithstanding”. **[inferred]** The Mishnah and
the Talmud, the Pharisees' heirs, bear the *Encyclopedia* out: their wheat,
their blemishes and their *luz* are the same body, not another. Paul, who
says before the Sanhedrin Φαρισαῖός εἰμι … περὶ ἐλπίδος καὶ ἀναστάσεως
νεκρῶν ἐγὼ κρίνομαι (Acts 23:6), holds the doctrine as the Mishnah holds it,
and as Calvin does.

### All, or the righteous only?

The rabbis differ. Pirke de R. Eliezer 34 reads Isaiah 26:19 of three
classes: “My dead bodies shall arise”, “They are the heathens, who are like
the carcase of the beast; they shall arise for the day of judgment, but they
shall not live” (p. 260). Maimonides holds the resurrection to be “the reward
of the righteous only” (Abelson, p. 42); the *Jewish Encyclopedia* names
Albo, Kimhi, Aboab and Bahya with him, Abravanel for all Israel, and
Manasseh ben Israel for all men. Daniel 12:2 has both: אֵלֶּה לְחַיֵּי עוֹלָם
וְאֵלֶּה לַחֲרָפוֹת לְדִרְאוֹן עוֹלָם. John 5:29 has εἰς ἀνάστασιν ζωῆς and
εἰς ἀνάστασιν κρίσεως, and the Reformed confess the resurrection of “all
the dead” (Westminster 32.2). **[inferred]** Here the Reformed follow Daniel
and Pirke de R. Eliezer against Maimonides.

## The renewal of the world

The texts are treated in [new-heavens-and-new-earth.md](new-heavens-and-new-earth.md#the-jewish-writers).
In brief:

- **Renewal of the same world.** Pirke de R. Eliezer 51: “The sages say: The
  heavens and the earth are destined to pass away and to be renewed”; the
  heavens are rolled together like a scroll of the Torah, which is rolled up
  and opened again (Isa. 34:4), and R. Eliezer concludes that there is
  “passing away (which) does not (really) pass away” (pp. 410–411).
  Calvin: the elements “are to be consumed, only that they may be renovated,
  their substance still remaining the same” (on 2 Pet. 3:10, p. 421).
- **Desolation, then renewal.** R. Kattina: “For six thousand years the world
  will continue, and in the seventh it will be destroyed” (Sanhedrin 97a,
  p. 302). The Raavad draws the conclusion against Maimonides: “Six thousand
  years will this world last, and one thousand years it will be desolate, as
  a consequence the world after that is a new one” (Glazer, p. 437, n. 9).
- **Permanence.** Maimonides: “we do not consider it a principle of our faith
  that the Universe will again be reduced to nothing” (*Guide* II.27,
  p. 201); Isaiah 65:17 is a figure of Israel's restoration (II.29).

**[inferred]** Isaiah 34:4, which Pirke de R. Eliezer reads as the rolling up
and unrolling of a scroll, is one of the texts Quenstedt cites for the
reduction of the world's substance “to nothing”. The Hebrew וְנָגֹלּוּ כַסֵּפֶר
הַשָּׁמָיִם, *and the heavens shall be rolled up as a scroll*, carries the
midrash's sense more easily: a scroll that is rolled up is not destroyed.
And the Hebrew of Isaiah 66:22 calls the new heavens and earth עֹמְדִים
לְפָנַי, *standing before me*, a participle, on which those whom Maimonides
answers built their view that the new heavens “have already been created and
are in existence” (*Guide* II.29, p. 209).

## Maimonides and the Raavad

Maimonides is the one great rabbinic authority whose account of the last
things differs from the Reformed at the root, and the Raavad's objections to
him are, point for point, the Reformed objections.

- **The resurrection affirmed.** “The Resurrection of the Dead is one of the
  cardinal doctrines of the Law of Moses. He who does not believe in this
  has no religion, and no bond with the Jewish faith. But it is the reward of
  the righteous only” (Abelson, p. 42).
- **But not permanent.** “And know that man is bound to die and become
  dissolved into his component parts” (p. 42). **[context]** In the
  *Treatise on the Resurrection of the Dead* (1191), written when he was
  accused of denying the resurrection, Maimonides affirmed it as a miracle,
  “a miracle whose possibility is granted with the assumption of a temporal
  Creation” (Abelson's note, quoting Graetz, p. 57), and held that the
  raised will die again before the world to come. The treatise is not
  reproduced here for want of a public-domain translation.
- **The world to come bodiless.** “The World to Come harbors neither body nor
  aught of a concrete form, save only the souls of the righteous divested of
  body as are the ministering angels” (Repentance VIII.2, p. 431).
- **The Raavad.** “This man's words are in my eyes nigh to such who assert
  that there is no resurrection to body but the souls alone. But, by the
  life of my head, this was not the opinion of our sages” (p. 431, n. 3),
  and against VIII.8, “It appears that he denies that the world will again
  be without form and void, and that the Holy One, blessed is He! will renew
  His world” (p. 437, n. 9).

**[context]** The *Jewish Encyclopedia* goes further than the texts allow:
“Maimonides … took the resurrection figuratively, and substituted for it
immortality of the soul”, citing the very treatise in which he affirmed it.
That is the Reform reading of Maimonides. Saadia, it reports, made the
resurrection fundamental, and Crescas and Albo held it “a specific doctrine
of Judaism, but not one of the fundamental teachings”.

**[inferred]** The Reformed agree with Maimonides that the soul is blessed
without the body before the resurrection, that the heart of that blessedness
is the knowledge of God, and that curiosity about the details is to be
refused. They disagree with him on the one point the Raavad presses: the
raised body is not dissolved again. The Westminster Confession's last word
on the subject is the word Maimonides denies: the bodies “shall be united
again to their souls forever” (32.2).

## The Haskalah and Reform

**Mendelssohn.** **[context]** Moses Mendelssohn (1729–1786), whom the
Haskalah counts its founder, argued in his *Phædon* (1767) for the soul's
immortality from reason alone, and did not discuss the resurrection. Kohler (1918, p. 295): “With Moses Mendelssohn, who in his
*Phædon* tried to translate Plato's proof of immortality into modern terms,
a new attitude toward the nature and destiny of the soul arose in Judaism
among both the philosophers and the educated laity.” Mendelssohn sought to
“prove the immortality of the soul through its indivisibility and
incorporeality”.

**The rabbinical conferences.**

- Philadelphia, 1869, art. 6: “The belief in the bodily resurrection has no
  religious foundation, and the doctrine of immortality refers to the
  after-existence of the soul only.”
- Pittsburgh, 1885, art. 7: “We reassert the doctrine of Judaism that the
  soul is immortal, grounding this belief on the divine nature of the human
  spirit … We reject, as ideas not rooted in Judaism, the beliefs both in
  bodily resurrection and in Gehenna and Eden (Hell and Paradise) as abodes
  for everlasting punishment and reward.” Art. 5: “We consider ourselves no
  longer a nation, but a religious community, and therefore expect neither a
  return to Palestine, nor a sacrificial worship under the sons of Aaron,
  nor the restoration of any of the laws concerning the Jewish state.”

**The prayer book.** The *Jewish Encyclopedia*: the question was raised
“whether the old liturgical formulas expressing the belief in resurrection
should not be so changed as to give clear expression to the hope of
immortality of the soul instead. This was done in all the American Reform
prayer-books.” Kohler (p. 297): he who sees God's will “in the immutable laws
of nature must find it impossible to praise God according to the traditional
formula as the ‘Reviver of the dead,’ but will avail himself instead of the
expression used in the Union Prayer Book after the pattern of Einhorn, ‘He
who has implanted within us immortal life.’” So the close of the
*Amidah*'s second blessing, מְחַיֵּה הַמֵּתִים, “who quickenest the dead”, was
replaced in the Reform liturgy.

**Kohler's account.** The resurrection was “originally purely local and
territorial” (p. 392), was later “modified to include a double resurrection:
the first, national, to usher in the Messianic kingdom, and the other,
universal, to usher in the everlasting life of the future” (p. 395), and is
“in such utter contradiction to our entire attitude toward both science and
religion, that it may be considered obsolete for the modern Jew. Orthodoxy,
which clings to it in formal loyalty to tradition, regards it as a miracle
which God will perform in the future, exactly like the many Biblical
miracles which defy reason” (p. 395).

**The Reformed parallels.** **[inferred]** The same move was made within
Protestantism, and the Reformed had answered it.

- Hodge (p. 771) reports that “many maintain that the only resurrection of
  which the Bible speaks is the resurrection of the soul when the body
  dies”, and sets as his “first position” that “our bodies are the subjects
  of the resurrection spoken of in the Scriptures.” His exposition of 1
  Corinthians 15 is in [resurrection-of-the-flesh.md](resurrection-of-the-flesh.md).
- Calvin had named the ground of the Reformed answer. Kohler grounds his
  rejection on “the immutable laws of nature”; Calvin, on the last things,
  “in regard to all things which lie beyond our sight, and far transcend the
  reach of our intellect, belief must either be founded on the sure oracles
  of God, or altogether renounced” (*Institutes* III.25.5, p. 615). On that
  ground the Reformed stand with what Kohler calls Orthodoxy, and with
  Maimonides' “miracle”.
- Calvin also names the source of the reasoned immortality that Mendelssohn
  revived: of the philosophers on the chief good, “none, however, except
  Plato acknowledged that it consisted in union with God” (III.25.2,
  p. 608). For Calvin that is a partial truth outside the resurrection; for
  Mendelssohn's heirs it replaced the resurrection.

**[inferred]** Pittsburgh gave up the land (art. 5) and the body (art. 7) in
the same declaration, and Kohler's history explains why: if the resurrection
was a hope for Israel on its own soil, it goes with that hope. The rabbinic
sources join body and land as he says. The New Testament keeps both and
widens the second, from the land to the earth (Matt. 5:5) and the world
(Rom. 4:13); the Reformed who look for a renewed earth keep both as the New
Testament does.

## The Lutheran position in the same light

- **Chiliasm.** The Augsburg Confession's “certain Jewish opinions” (XVII)
  describe the rabbinic order correctly, as shown above, and the Reformed
  agree with the condemnation (Calvin, III.25.5). The Lutheran and Reformed
  confessions are one here.
- **The resurrection of the flesh.** The Formula of Concord's “precisely the
  substance of this our flesh, but without sin, will rise again” (Solid
  Declaration I.46) is as near to the rabbis' wheat and the Raavad's
  “concrete form” as the Westminster Confession is. Luther's preference for
  *Leib* over *Fleisch* in the Large Catechism is a point of German idiom.
- **Annihilation.** The Lutheran dogmaticians teach “an absolute
  annihilation of its substance” (Schmid, §66, p. 656). No Jewish source in
  this collection teaches it. The Talmud and the Midrash teach renewal,
  after a time of desolation in the Raavad's reading; Maimonides teaches
  permanence and says outright that the world's reduction to nothing is no
  principle of the faith. Quenstedt's proof texts include Isaiah 34:4, the
  verse Pirke de R. Eliezer reads as the rolling and unrolling of a scroll.
- **The glorified body.** Quenstedt's endowments include “Invisibility”,
  “Impalpability” and “Illocality” (§67, p. 662). The rabbinic raised bodies
  are local and visible: they stand, are recognized, and, in the Talmud,
  “will be supplied with wings similar to the wings of the eagles, and they
  will fly over the world” (Sanhedrin 92b, p. 277). Quenstedt's “Agility”
  has this counterpart; his “Illocality” has none. The Reformed “true body”
  in “a local habitation” (Hodge, p. 855) is the rabbinic body glorified.

**[inferred]** On the Jewish evidence, then, the Lutheran dogmaticians and
the Reformed agree on the resurrection and on chiliasm, and the dogmaticians
stand alone on annihilation and on the illocal body. The illocal body comes
from Lutheran Christology and the doctrine of the Lord's Supper; the
annihilation from a reading of the verses of perishing (Ps. 102:26; Isa.
34:4; 2 Pet. 3:10) that the Jewish readers of the same Hebrew verses did not
share. See [lutheran-and-reformed.md](lutheran-and-reformed.md).

## Assessment

**The resurrection.** **[inferred]** The Reformed doctrine of the
resurrection of the body is the Pharisees' and the Mishnah's doctrine, held
by the New Testament as its own. Paul calls God τοῦ ζῳοποιοῦντος τοὺς
νεκρούς (Rom. 4:17) and τῷ ἐγείροντι τοὺς νεκρούς (2 Cor. 1:9), the
*Amidah*'s מְחַיֵּה מֵתִים in Greek, and says before the Sanhedrin that he is
judged as a Pharisee “of the hope and resurrection of the dead”. Where the
Reformed agree with the rabbis (the same body, changed; the soul with God
meanwhile; the promise to the patriarchs as the proof), the New Testament
agrees with both. Where the Reformed part from them (no Messianic age
before the resurrection; the resurrection of all), the New Testament is the
reason: the Messiah is risen as the firstfruits, and “all that are in the
graves” come forth (John 5:28–29), as Daniel 12:2 says.

**Kohler's two passages.** **[inferred]** Kohler's case that the Hebrew Bible
knows the resurrection only in “two late post-exilic passages” rests on
dating those passages late and on a history of the idea reconstructed from
outside the text. On the text as it stands, Isaiah 26:19 and Daniel 12:2
state the resurrection, and the Torah's promise to the patriarchs implies
it, by an argument that the Talmud tested and kept and that Jesus used. The
Reformed reading has the text on its side.

**The heavens.** **[inferred]** The Reformed three heavens and the
Talmud's seven are counts of the same kind, and neither is in the text.
What both get from the text is a highest heaven where God dwells and where
the souls of the righteous are. The Jewish evidence supports the Reformed
identification of the third heaven with paradise and weakens the claim
that the third is the last (see [third-heaven.md](third-heaven.md)).

**The world.** **[inferred]** The midrash's “passing away (which) does not
(really) pass away” states the Reformed doctrine of renewal, from the same
verses. Maimonides' permanent world agrees with the Reformed that the world
is not annihilated, and differs from them in denying that it will be
changed. The Lutheran annihilation has no Jewish counterpart.

**Maimonides and Reform.** **[inferred]** The two Jewish positions furthest
from the Reformed are Maimonides' bodiless world to come and the Reform
platforms. Both start from the soul's immortality argued from reason, the
first from Aristotle, the second from Plato by way of Mendelssohn. Both
treat the body as the lesser part. The Raavad's answer to Maimonides and the
Reformed answer to Reform are the same: the sages, and the Scriptures, speak
of bodies. That the Reformed should find their nearest allies in this
matter among the rabbis whom Reform left behind is not an accident: both
read the Hebrew Bible as a promise to bodies, in a land, from a God who
“quickenest the dead”.
