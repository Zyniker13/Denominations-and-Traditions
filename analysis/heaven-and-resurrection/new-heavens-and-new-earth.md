# The New Heavens and the New Earth

Part of [Heaven, the New Earth and the Resurrection of the Body](README.md).

When Scripture promises “new heavens and a new earth”, does it mean the
present world purged and renewed, a new world made after the old is
destroyed, or a figure for the restored Church? What did the Reformed teach,
how does the Hebrew and Greek bear on it, and how do the Lutheran and Jewish
answers compare?

The passages are in
[passages.md](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#the-new-heavens-and-the-new-earth).

## Summary

1. **Most Reformed writers teach renewal: the same heavens and earth purged
   by fire and made new, their substance remaining.** Calvin: the elements
   “are to be consumed, only that they may be renovated, their substance
   still remaining the same”. So the Belgic Confession (“to cleanse it”),
   Ames, Boston (“Not that they shall be annihilated”), and Hodge (“The
   destruction here foretold is not annihilation”), who names Turretin and
   says that “Most of the Reformed theologians generally oppose the idea of
   annihilation.”
2. **There are Reformed exceptions.** Wollebius sets out both opinions and
   then says “the understanding reader may subscribe to the latter”, the
   destruction of the world's substance. Edwards has this world “turned into
   a great furnace” for the wicked while the church goes to the highest
   heaven. Poole leaves the question open, leaning to renewal.
3. **Several read the prophetic “new heavens and new earth” first of the
   Church.** Calvin applies Isaiah 65:17 to the Church restored after Babylon
   and under Christ, which “will not be fulfilled till the last resurrection”; Owen's
   sermon of 1649 reads the shaken “heavens and earth” of Hebrews 12 as “the
   Politicall Heights” of the nations; Edwards finds a new heavens and earth
   at each coming of Christ in history.
4. **The Lutheran dogmaticians teach annihilation.** “Not a transformation
   of the world, therefore, but an absolute annihilation of its substance is
   to be expected” (Schmid §66). Quenstedt, Gerhard, Baier and Hollaz agree,
   though Gerhard, as Hodge reports, admitted that Luther and many of the
   fathers held otherwise.
5. **The rabbinic writers expect renewal.** “The heavens and the earth are
   destined to pass away and to be renewed” (Pirke de R. Eliezer 51), a
   “passing away (which) does not (really) pass away”. The Talmud's
   six thousand years and one thousand desolate (Sanhedrin 97a) is read by
   the Raavad as a renewal of the world, and by Maimonides as a figure.
   **Maimonides** holds that the world will never be destroyed and reads
   Isaiah 65:17 as a figure for Israel's restoration.
6. **The Hebrew and Greek hold both kinds of word**, “perish” and “be
   changed” (Ps. 102:26), but the New Testament's own terms lean to renewal:
   παλιγγενεσία (Matt. 19:28), ἀποκατάστασις (Acts 3:21), the passing of the
   σχῆμα (1 Cor. 7:31), “the creation itself” set free (Rom. 8:21), and the
   world that “perished” in the flood and yet remained (2 Pet. 3:6).

## The text

**Psalm 102:25–27; Hebrews 1:10–12.**
([Ps. 102](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#ps-102-25-27),
[Heb. 1](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#heb-1-10-12).) The
Hebrew says of the heavens הֵמָּה יֹאבֵדוּ, “they shall perish”, and then
כַּלְּבוּשׁ תַּחֲלִיפֵם וְיַחֲלֹפוּ, “as a vesture shalt thou change them, and they
shall be changed” (KJV). חָלַף is to pass on, to change, to put on a fresh garment.
The Septuagint has ἀπολοῦνται, “they shall perish”, and ἀλλαγήσονται, “they
shall be changed”; for “thou shalt change them” Brenton's and Swete's texts
read ἑλίξεις αὐτούς, “thou shalt roll them up”, where the Göttingen edition
reads ἀλλάξεις with the Hebrew. Hebrews quotes the Greek with ἑλίξεις. The
verse holds both words: the Lutherans read “perish”, the Reformed “be
changed as a vesture”.

**Isaiah 65:17; 66:22.**
([65:17](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#isa-65-17),
[66:22](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#isa-66-22).) The Hebrew
כִּי־הִנְנִי בוֹרֵא שָׁמַיִם חֲדָשִׁים וָאָרֶץ חֲדָשָׁה is a participle, “I am
creating”, with בָּרָא, the verb of Genesis 1:1. The Greek has no verb of
creating: ἔσται γὰρ ὁ οὐρανὸς καινὸς καὶ ἡ γῆ καινή, “For there shall be a new
heaven and a new earth” (Brenton). In 66:22 the new heavens and earth are said to
“stand” (עֹמְדִים; μένει, “remain”). The adjective is חָדָשׁ, καινός: new in
kind and freshness. νέος, “new in time”, is not used of the new heavens in
either Testament. Neither בָּרָא nor καινός decides between a fresh creation
and a renewal: בָּרָא is also used of what God does in history (Isa. 65:18,
“I create Jerusalem a rejoicing”).

**Isaiah 51:6; 34:4.**
([51:6](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#isa-51-6),
[34:4](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#isa-34-4).) The heavens
“vanish away like smoke” (נִמְלָחוּ, found only here in this sense; the
Greek has ἐστερεώθη, “was made firm”) and the earth will “wax old like a
garment”; the heavens are “rolled together as a scroll”. Rolled up, not
burned.

**2 Peter 3:5–13.**
([passage](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#2pet-3-10-13).)
- The pattern is the flood: δι᾿ ὧν ὁ τότε κόσμος ὕδατι κατακλυσθεὶς
  ἀπώλετο, “the world that then was, being overflowed with water,
  perished” (v. 6). That world perished, and the earth remained.
- Verse 10: οὐρανοὶ ῥοιζηδὸν παρελεύσονται, στοιχεῖα δὲ καυσούμενα
  λυθήσονται. The last words differ by edition: the Textus Receptus and
  the Byzantine text read γῆ καὶ τὰ ἐν αὐτῇ ἔργα κατακαήσεται, “shall be
  burned up”; the SBL text reads εὑρεθήσεται, “shall be found”, laid bare
  before the judge.
- Verse 13: καινοὺς δὲ οὐρανοὺς καὶ γῆν καινὴν κατὰ τὸ ἐπάγγελμα αὐτοῦ
  προσδοκῶμεν, ἐν οἷς δικαιοσύνη κατοικεῖ: the καινοί of Isaiah 65:17 LXX.

**Revelation 21:1, 5.**
([passage](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#rev-21-1-5).)
ὁ γὰρ πρῶτος οὐρανὸς καὶ ἡ πρώτη γῆ παρῆλθε (TR; ἀπῆλθον, Byzantine;
ἀπῆλθαν, SBL), “passed away”. And ἰδοὺ καινὰ ποιῶ πάντα: “behold, I make all
things new”, with πάντα the object and καινά predicative, not “I make all
new things”.

**Romans 8:19–23.**
([passage](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#rom-8-19-23).) καὶ αὐτὴ
ἡ κτίσις ἐλευθερωθήσεται ἀπὸ τῆς δουλείας τῆς φθορᾶς: “the creature itself
also shall be delivered” (KJV), the creation itself. The subject that is set free is the subject that
was subjected to vanity and that groans. Paul joins it to “the redemption
of our body” (v. 23), redemption of the body, not from it.

**Matthew 19:28; Acts 3:21; 1 Corinthians 7:31; Hebrews 12:26–28.**
([Matt.](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#matt-19-28),
[Acts](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#acts-3-21),
[1 Cor.](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#1cor-7-31),
[Heb.](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#heb-12-26-28).)
ἐν τῇ παλιγγενεσίᾳ, “in the regeneration”; ἄχρι χρόνων ἀποκαταστάσεως
πάντων, “until the times of restitution of all things”; παράγει γὰρ τὸ
σχῆμα τοῦ κόσμου τούτου, “the fashion of this world passeth away”, the
σχῆμα being the outward form; and τῶν σαλευομένων τὴν μετάθεσιν … ἵνα μείνῃ τὰ μὴ
σαλευόμενα, “the removing of those things that are shaken … that those
things which cannot be shaken may remain”.

## The Reformed teaching

### Renewal, the substance remaining

- **Calvin**, on 2 Peter 3:10 (p. 421): “Of the elements of the world I shall
  only say this one thing, that they are to be consumed, only that they may
  be renovated, their substance still remaining the same, as it may be
  easily gathered from Rom. viii. 21, and from other passages.” On Romans
  8:21 (p. 305): “God will restore to a perfect state the world, now fallen,
  together with mankind. But what that perfection will be, as to beasts as
  well as plants and metals, it is not meet nor right in us to inquire more
  curiously”. The translator's note on 2 Peter dissents: “All that is said
  here is, that there will be new heavens and a new earth, and not that the
  present heavens and the present earth will be renovated.”
  ([text](../../theologians/reformed/continental/calvin/commentaries-on-the-last-things.md))
- **The Belgic Confession** 37: Christ will come “burning this old world with
  fire and flame, to cleanse it.” **The Scots Confession** 11: “the time of
  refreshing and restitution of all things will come”.
- **Ames**, *Marrow* I.41 (p. 214): “The fire that is appointed to purge and
  renew the World, shall not goe before the judgement, but shall follow.”
  ([text](../../theologians/reformed/continental/ames/marrow-of-sacred-divinity-exaltation-glorification-end-of-world.md))
- **Boston**, *Fourfold State* (p. 286): the visible heavens, earth and sea
  “shall pass away. Not that they shall be annihilated, (•r reduced to
  nothing) that is not the •peration of fire: but they shall be *dissolved,*
  and purged by that 〈…〉, from all the effects of sin, or of the curse,
  upon the•; and then r•n•wed, and made more glorious and stable.”
  ([text](../../theologians/reformed/british/boston/fourfold-state-resurrection-and-heaven.md))
- **Goodwin** (p. 25): “God doth take the same world that was Adams, and
  makes it new and glorious; this same creation groans for this *new
  world*”.
- **Hodge**, *Systematic Theology* III, §3 (pp. 851–855): the passages “are
  not to be understood as predicting great political and moral revolutions”
  (p. 851). “The destruction here foretold is not annihilation” (p. 852),
  for four reasons: combustion “is not a destruction of substance”; the
  flood was not annihilation, “therefore the second is not”; the change is
  called παλιγγενεσία, ἀποκατάστασις, and deliverance from corruption (Matt.
  19:28; Acts 3:21; Rom. 8:21); and no substance is known ever to have been
  annihilated. He adds that the change concerns “our earth and what
  pertains to it”, not the whole universe (p. 853), and that “this renovated
  earth” is “the final seat of Christ’s kingdom” (p. 854).
  ([text](../../theologians/reformed/princeton/hodge/systematic-theology-vol-3-last-things.md))

Hodge reports the division (p. 853): “Many of the old theologians,
especially among the Lutherans, understood the Bible to teach the absolute
annihilation of our world”; but “Most of the Reformed theologians generally
oppose the idea of annihilation. Turrettin certainly does.” Gerhard, he
says, “admits, however, that many of the fathers and Luther himself were on
the other side”, and quotes Luther: “The heavens have their work-day clothes
on; hereafter they will have on their Sunday garments.”

### Undecided: Poole

Poole's *Annotations* on 2 Peter 3 leave both open. On “the heavens shall
pass away” (image 590): “Either wholly, so as to cease *to be*; or rather,
as to their present Being and Condition, so as to cease to be what they now
are, and to give place to the new Heaven”. On verse 13 (image 591): “These
will be new Heavens, and a new Earth, either as to their Substance, or as to
their Qualities refined and purified from all Defilement, and free from all
that Vanity to which the Creature was made subject by the Sin of Man, *Rom.*
8.20, 21.” The heavens that perish are “The Etherial, or starry Heaven, as
well as Aerial”, but not “the Empyreal Heaven, or Place of glorified
Spirits”.
([text](../../theologians/reformed/british/poole/annotations-last-things.md))

### Destruction of the substance: Wollebius and Edwards

**Wollebius** (pp. 301–305) defines the end of the world as “the destruction
of this visible Universe”, and asks whether it will be destroyed “In the
substance, or qualities onely?” He sets out the arguments for renewal (Rom.
8:19–22; 1 Cor. 7:31; 2 Pet. 3:13; the flood; “Because man shall not be
changed in his substance, but in his qualities”) and those for destruction
(Ps. 102:26–27; Heb. 1:11; the language of melting and burning), and
concludes:

> Now although in controversies of this nature, which overthrow not the
> articles of Faith, it be lawful to suspend o•r assent; yet, without
> prejudice to other mens judgments, the understanding reader may subscribe
> to the latter opinion: for it is one thing to be restored, and changed
> unto a better estate; and another thing to wax old like a garment, to
> vanish like smoak, to be dissolved, to melt, to be burned, to passe away;
> to be no more, as the fo• mer testimonies affirm.

His translator, Alexander Ross, adds a note (p. 304) that limits the
fire: “*The Heavens that shall be purged by fire, are the* aerial *onely.*”
and “*The fire shall purge no more, than what the water did purge in the
Flood of* Noah.”
([text](../../theologians/reformed/continental/wollebius/abridgment-of-christian-divinity-last-things.md))

**Edwards**, *History of the Work of Redemption*, uses “new heavens and new
earth” for each great renewal of the Church in history: “there is in each of
these comings of Christ an ending of the old heavens and the old earth, and
a beginning of new heavens and a new earth” (p. 178). At the last, “the very outward
frame of the old world itself shall come to an end; and the church shall
dwell in a world new to it … even heaven” (p. 175). After the judgment
“this world shall be set on fire, and be turned into a great furnace,
wherein all the enemies of Christ and his church shall be tormented for
ever and ever” (p. 279), and the new heavens and new earth are finished
“after every material frame of the old heavens and the old earth are
destroyed” (p. 282).
([text](../../theologians/reformed/american/edwards/history-of-the-work-of-redemption-period-3.md))

### The new heavens as the restored Church

- **Calvin** on Isaiah 65:17 (pp. 397–399): “By these metaphors he promises a
  remarkable change of affairs; as if God had said that he has both the
  inclination and the power not only to restore his Church, but to restore
  it in such a manner that it shall appear to gain new life and to dwell in
  a new world.” The restoration after Babylon “is imperfect, if it be not
  extended as far as to Christ; and even now we are in the progress and
  accomplishment of it, and those things will not be fulfilled till the last
  resurrection”. The prophet “has in his eye the whole reign of Christ, down
  to its final close, which is also called ‘the day of renovation and
  restoration.’ (Acts iii. 21.)” On 66:22 (p. 437): “When he speaks of ‘new
  heavens’ and a ‘new earth,’ he looks to the reign of Christ, by whom all
  things have been renewed”.
- **Owen**, *The Shaking and Translating of Heaven and Earth* (1649), on
  Hebrews 12:26–27 (p. 9): “It is then neither the materiall Heavens and
  Earth, nor yet Mosaicall Ordinances, but the Politicall Heights and
  splendour, the popular multitudes and strength of the Nations of the
  Earth, that are thus to be shaken”. He reads Isaiah 34:4 the same way: the
  “dissolved, rolled *Heavens,* are no other, but the power and heights of
  the opposing Nations” (p. 10).
  ([text](../../theologians/reformed/british/owen/shaking-and-translating-of-heaven-and-earth.md))
- **Poole**, on 2 Peter 3:13, notes that the places of Isaiah “speak of a new
  state of the Church here in the World, yet by way of Allusion to the
  Renovation of the World, which is ultimately there promised”.

**[inferred]** These readings do not deny a renewal of the world at the end;
Calvin and Poole hold both. Owen's sermon reads one text of Hebrews and
one of Isaiah politically, and says nothing here of the world's end.

## The Lutheran dogmaticians: annihilation

Schmid §66
([text](../../theologians/lutheran/heinrich-schmid/doctrinal-theology-last-things.md)):
“After the final judgment, the absolute end of this world will come; angels
and men excepted, everything that belongs to this world will be burnt up by
fire and reduced to nothing. Not a transformation of the world, therefore,
but an absolute annihilation of its substance is to be expected” (pp.
655–656). Baier: heaven and earth “will, with respect to their substance,
perish by means of fire”. Hollaz: God “will destroy with fire and annihilate
the entire fabric of heaven and earth, and all created things, intelligent
creatures alone excepted.” Quenstedt: “The form of this consummation
consists not in the mere change, alteration, or renewing of qualities, but
in the total abolition and reduction of the world's substance itself to
nothing (Ps. 102: 26; 2 Pet. 3:10; Rev. 20: 11; Is. 34: 4; Luke 21:33; Job
14: 12).”

The Lutheran confessions themselves say only that “at the Consummation of
the World Christ will appear for judgment” (Augsburg Confession XVII).
Annihilation is the teaching of the dogmaticians, not of the Book of
Concord. **[inferred]** Luther's “Sunday garments”, as Hodge reports it,
stands with the Reformed.

## The Jewish writers

- **Pirke de Rabbi Eliezer** 51 (Friedlander, pp. 410–411): “The sages say:
  The heavens and the earth are destined to pass away and to be renewed.”
  The heavens are rolled up like a scroll of the Torah and opened again; God
  “will fold up the earth and again will He spread it out and put it in its
  place like a garment”. R. Eliezer: the host of heaven “will again be
  renewed before Him to make known that there is passing away (which) does
  not (really) pass away.” On “There is no new thing under the sun”: “The
  righteous and all their works will be renewed, but the wicked will not be
  renewed” (pp. 415–416).
  ([text](../../theologians/rabbinic/midrash-and-prayer-book-on-the-resurrection.md))
- **The Talmud**, Sanhedrin 97a (Rodkinson, p. 302): “R. Ktina said: For six
  thousand years the world will continue, and in the seventh it will be
  destroyed.” Sanhedrin 92b (p. 277) asks what the righteous will do “at the
  time the Holy One, blessed be He, shall renew his world”: they “will be
  supplied with wings similar to the wings of the eagles, and they will fly
  over the world.”
  ([text](../../theologians/rabbinic/mishnah-and-talmud-on-the-world-to-come.md))
- **1 Enoch** 45:4–5: “I will transform the heaven and make it an eternal
  blessing and light” … “And I will transform the earth and make it a
  blessing”; 91:16: “And the first heaven shall depart and pass away, And a
  new heaven shall appear”. Charles on **2 Baruch** (p. 86): “The world was to be
  renewed”, and the same doctrine of renewal “was naturally applied in due
  course to those destined to live in it.”
  ([text](../../theologians/jewish-antiquity/second-temple-on-heaven-and-resurrection.md))
- **Maimonides**, *Guide* II.27 (p. 202): “reasoning leads to the conclusion
  that the destruction of the Universe is not a certain fact.” II.29: Isaiah
  65:17 is said “figuratively”: “by the phrase, ‘I will create,’ he means
  that God will give them perpetual gladness and joy in place of the
  previous grief and mourning”; and “no prophet or sage has ever announced
  the destruction of the Universe, or a change of its present condition, or
  a permanent change of any of its properties” (p. 209). Of the six thousand
  years: “the phrase ‘one thousand years it will be waste’ distinctly shows
  that time will continue”. In the *Mishneh Torah* (Kings XII.1): “Let none
  suppose that in the time of the Messiah any alteration will take place in
  the system of the present state of the world”.
- **The Raavad**, against Maimonides (Glazer, p. 437, n. 9): “It appears that he
  denies that the world will again be without form and void, and that the
  Holy One, blessed is He! will renew His world, as the sages have said:
  (Sanhedrin 97a.) Six thousand years will this world last, and one thousand
  years it will be desolate, as a consequence the world after that is a new
  one”.
  ([text](../../theologians/jewish-medieval/maimonides/world-to-come-and-resurrection.md))

## Assessment

**The vocabulary.** **[inferred]** The words for the end of the present
heavens are strong: perish (אָבַד, ἀπόλλυμι), vanish like smoke, wax old,
be rolled up, pass away (παρέρχομαι), be dissolved (λύω), be burned up.
Wollebius is right that “it is one thing to be restored … and another
thing to … passe away”, and the Lutheran case rests on this language. But
the same texts set beside these words others that imply continuity: the
heavens are “changed” as a garment is changed (חָלַף, ἀλλάσσω); the world
has a παλιγγενεσία and an ἀποκατάστασις; it is the σχῆμα of the world that
passes; “the creation itself” is set free; God makes “all things new”,
not all new things. And 2 Peter itself gives the measure of its words: the
world of Noah's day ἀπώλετο, “perished”, by water, and the earth remained.
On the Greek, “perish” in this passage does not mean “cease to be”.

**The two readings of verse 10.** **[inferred]** The SBL text's
εὑρεθήσεται, “shall be found”, if it is the reading, fits renewal better
than κατακαήσεται, “shall be burned up”: the earth is laid bare for
judgment, not consumed. None of the writers quoted here discusses it. On
the Textus Receptus too the argument from the flood stands.

**Calvin, Owen and the Church.** **[inferred]** Isaiah 65–66 sets the new
heavens and earth beside the new Jerusalem, long life, and the wolf and the
lamb, in a context of Israel's restoration; Calvin's reading of it as the
restored Church fulfilled at the last day follows the chapter. The New
Testament takes the phrase up for the end itself (2 Pet. 3:13; Rev. 21:1).
Owen's political reading of Hebrews 12:26–27 is harder to square with
verse 28, where what remains is βασιλεία ἀσάλευτος, “a kingdom which cannot
be moved”, received by believers.

**Against the Lutherans.** **[inferred]** The Lutheran doctrine of
annihilation has against it the flood parallel of 2 Peter 3:6, the
liberation of “the creation itself” in Romans 8:21, and the analogy, which
both traditions accept, between the raised body and the renewed world: the
dogmaticians themselves teach that the body is raised the same in substance
with new qualities (see [resurrection-of-the-flesh.md](resurrection-of-the-flesh.md)).
Hodge makes the point: “Our bodies are not to be annihilated, but
changed.”

**The Jewish parallel.** **[inferred]** Pirke de R. Eliezer's “passing
away (which) does not (really) pass away” is as close a statement of the
Reformed doctrine as any Reformed writer gives, and it is drawn from the
same verses (Isa. 34:4; 51:6). The Raavad stands with Calvin, and
Maimonides, who denies any change in nature, stands nearer to Owen's
figurative reading of the prophets than to either side of the
Lutheran–Reformed dispute. The Lutheran annihilation has no counterpart in
the Jewish sources gathered here.
