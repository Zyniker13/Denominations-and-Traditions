# Heaven, the New Earth and the Resurrection of the Body

What did the Reformed, chiefly the historical Reformed of the sixteenth to
nineteenth centuries, teach about:

1. the “third heaven” (2 Corinthians 12:2);
2. the final destination of the Christian;
3. the new heaven(s) and the new earth;
4. the resurrection of the body, or “of the flesh”?

How does that teaching stand against the Hebrew and Greek of the texts it is
argued from? How does it look beside the Jewish reading of the same Hebrew
Bible, from the Second Temple and the Talmud to Maimonides and the
Haskalah? And how does the orthodox Lutheran position differ from it?

The New Testament is read in Greek, as the text itself, and the Old
Testament in Hebrew, with the Septuagint beside it. No argument is drawn
from an Aramaic or Hebrew original reconstructed beneath the Greek.
**[context]** marks a statement resting on history outside the texts;
**[inferred]** marks this study's own reading rather than what a text says
in so many words. Every quotation in these papers has been checked against
the text in the repository from which it is taken.

## Summary of findings

1. **The third heaven.** The Reformed agree that there are three heavens,
   the air, the heaven of the stars, and the heaven of the blessed where
   Christ is in his human nature, and that the third is the highest; they
   identify it with the paradise of 2 Corinthians 12:4. The count is drawn
   from Scripture's use of the word *heaven*; the Greek ἕως τρίτου οὐρανοῦ
   is an ordinal without a total. The Jewish books nearest to Paul put
   paradise in the third of seven heavens, which supports the
   identification and weakens “the highest”. The confessions say only “the
   highest heavens”. ([third-heaven.md](third-heaven.md))
2. **The final destination.** At death the believer's soul is with Christ in
   heaven (Westminster 32.1); here the Reformed, the Lutherans and the
   Greek agree. After the resurrection the older Reformed (Wollebius,
   Watson, Boston, Edwards) put the saints in the highest heaven; Ussher,
   Poole and Hodge on the renewed earth; the confessions do not decide.
   **[inferred]** The texts (Rev. 21:2–3; Matt. 5:5; Rom. 8:21) favour the
   renewed earth. ([final-destination.md](final-destination.md))
3. **The new heavens and earth.** Most of the Reformed (the Belgic
   Confession, Calvin, Ames, Boston, Hodge) teach that the world will be
   purged and renewed, its substance remaining; Wollebius and Edwards lean
   to destruction; the Lutheran dogmaticians teach annihilation, though the
   Book of Concord does not. **[inferred]** The flood of 2 Peter 3:6 and
   “the creation itself” of Romans 8:21 favour renewal.
   ([new-heavens-and-new-earth.md](new-heavens-and-new-earth.md))
4. **The resurrection of the body.** The very body that died is raised, the
   same in substance and new in its qualities; the Reformed keep the word
   “flesh” (Luke 24:39). They differ only on what makes the body the same:
   the same particles (Perkins, Boston) or an identity not tied to the same
   matter (Hodge). The Greek of 1 Corinthians 15 and Philippians 3 supports
   the doctrine. ([resurrection-of-the-flesh.md](resurrection-of-the-flesh.md))
5. **In light of the Jewish sources.** On the resurrection of the body the
   Reformed stand with the Pharisees, the Mishnah, the *Amidah* and the
   Raavad, against the Sadducees, Maimonides' bodiless world to come and the
   Reform platforms of 1869 and 1885. They prove it from the Torah by the
   same argument the Talmud kept: God's promise to the dead patriarchs. The
   rabbis' Messianic age before the resurrection is the one stage the
   Reformed deny, and the Haskalah's immortality without resurrection is a
   position the Reformed had already answered in its Christian forms.
   ([jewish-sources.md](jewish-sources.md))
6. **The Lutheran contrast.** The orthodox Lutherans agree with the Reformed
   on the soul, the resurrection, the judgment and the rejection of
   chiliasm. They differ on whether a glorified body is bound to a place
   (the dogmaticians' “Illocality”, from the doctrine of the Supper) and on
   the end of the world (annihilation). **[inferred]** On both, the
   Reformed have the Greek and the Jewish readers of the Hebrew on their
   side.
   ([lutheran-and-reformed.md](lutheran-and-reformed.md))

## Scope and method

**The Reformed, primarily historical.** The Reformed sources run from Calvin
and Bullinger to Hodge (1872): the continental writers Calvin, Bullinger,
Wollebius and Ames; the British Perkins, Ussher, Goodwin, Owen, Watson,
Vincent, Dickson, Poole's *Annotations* and Boston; the American Edwards
and Hodge; and the Reformed confessions in [confessions/](../../confessions/README.md).
Later writers are not included.

**The Lutheran sources** are the Book of Concord and the seventeenth-century
dogmaticians as Heinrich Schmid gathered them (1843; English 1899).

**The Jewish sources** are those whose texts may be reproduced: the Second
Temple writings and Josephus in public-domain translations; the Mishnah and
Talmud in Rodkinson (1918), Streane (1891) and Cohen (1921); Pirke de Rabbi
Eliezer in Friedlander (1916); Bereshit Rabbah in the *Sefaria Midrash
Rabbah* (CC BY); the prayer book in Singer (1904); Maimonides in Abelson
(1906), Glazer (1927), Soloweyczik (1863) and Friedländer (1904); the Reform
documents in the *Jewish Encyclopedia* (1901–1906) and Kohler (1918). The
Hebrew and Aramaic are from the Vilna Mishnah, the Hebrew Wikisource Talmud
(CC BY-SA) and the Metsudah Siddur (CC BY). Translations still in
copyright, and those licensed only for non-commercial use, are not quoted.

**Quotation.** Every quotation in these papers was checked, word for word,
against the source text in the repository. Translations of Hebrew and Greek
that are this study's own are given in italics, not in quotation marks.

## Gaps

- **Turretin.** No public-domain English translation of Turretin's
  *Institutio* on the last things was found; he is cited only as Hodge
  reports him.
- **Maimonides' *Treatise on the Resurrection of the Dead*** (1191) is not
  reproduced, for want of a public-domain English translation. Its
  position (the bodily resurrection affirmed as a miracle, the raised
  dying again before the world to come) is reported from Abelson's note
  and marked **[context]**.
- **Mendelssohn's *Phædon*** (1767) is described from Kohler and not
  reproduced; it does not discuss the resurrection.
- **Saadia, Crescas, Albo and Abravanel** are known here only through the
  *Jewish Encyclopedia*'s summary.
- **The Göttingen Septuagint** is cited for its readings only; its text is
  in copyright.
- **Luther's own writings on the last things** are not included; his view
  of the world's renewal is known here from Hodge's report and Gerhard's
  admission.
- **Later Reformed writers** (Bavinck, Vos and after) are outside the
  historical scope asked for.
