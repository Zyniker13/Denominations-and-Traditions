# Lutheran and Reformed on Heaven, the New Earth and the Resurrection

Part of [Heaven, the New Earth and the Resurrection of the Body](README.md).

Where do the orthodox Lutheran and the Reformed positions on the last things
agree, where do they differ, and why? The Jewish positions are set beside
them where they bear on the difference. The Lutheran sources are the Book of
Concord and the seventeenth-century dogmaticians (Chemnitz, Gerhard,
Quenstedt, Hollaz, Baier) as Heinrich Schmid gathered them
([text](../../theologians/lutheran/heinrich-schmid/doctrinal-theology-last-things.md));
the Reformed are the confessions and theologians quoted in the other
papers of this study; the Jewish are those in
[jewish-sources.md](jewish-sources.md). **[context]** marks a statement
resting on history outside the texts; **[inferred]** marks this study's own
reading.

## Summary

1. **They agree on most of the doctrine.** The soul lives on after death and
   is at once happy or miserable, without sleep or purgatory. The body that
   died is raised, the same in substance and new in its qualities. Christ's
   coming, the general resurrection and the judgment are one event, with no
   earthly millennium before them. Eternal life is the sight of God.
2. **They differ on whether a glorified body is bound to a place.** The
   Heidelberg Catechism: Christ, “with respect to his human nature, he is no
   more on earth”. The dogmaticians: Christ is in heaven “yet not according
   to local circumscription, but definitively and according to the manner
   of a glorified body” (Quenstedt), and the glorified bodies of the saints
   have “Illocality”. This is the dispute over the Lord's Supper carried
   into the last things.
3. **They differ on the end of the world**, and so on the final home. The
   dogmaticians teach “an absolute annihilation of its substance”, so the
   final home can only be heaven. Most Reformed teach that the world will
   be purged and renewed, and some make the renewed earth the final home.
   Neither side's view is confessional. The Book of Concord does not teach
   annihilation, and Luther spoke of the heavens' “Sunday garments”; on the
   Reformed side Wollebius and Edwards lean to destruction.
4. **Both reject chiliasm and both call it Jewish.** They differ on the
   future of Israel. The Lutheran majority deny a general conversion of the
   Jews before the end (Hollaz, Gerhard); the Reformed are divided (Wollebius
   denies it; Goodwin and Edwards expect “the calling of the Jews”).
5. **Set beside the Jewish sources**, the Reformed are nearer to the rabbis
   on the renewal of the world and on the located body; both traditions
   stand with the rabbis on the resurrection of the same body, and against
   them on the Messianic age. Both found the soul's immortality on
   Scripture rather than on the reasoned proofs that Mendelssohn revived.
6. **[inferred]** On the Greek and Hebrew, the Reformed have the better of
   both differences: the risen Christ's σάρκα καὶ ὀστέα and his being
   received by heaven ἄχρι χρόνων ἀποκαταστάσεως πάντων (Acts 3:21) for the
   located body, and the flood of 2 Peter 3:6 and “the creation itself” of
   Romans 8:21 for renewal.

## The root: the body of Christ

The two confessions part not on the last things as such but on where
Christ's human body now is, and the difference runs into everything said
about heaven and the glorified body.

**The Reformed.** The Heidelberg Catechism (Q. 47): “with respect to his
human nature, he is no more on earth; but with respect to his Godhead,
majesty, grace and spirit, he is at no time absent from us.” Q. 48 answers
the Lutheran objection that this divides the natures: “since the Godhead is
illimitable and omnipresent, it must necessarily follow that the same is
beyond the limits of the human nature he assumed, and yet is nevertheless
in this human nature”. Q. 80: Christ, “according to his human nature is now
not on earth, but in heaven, at the right hand of God his Father”. The
Belgic Confession (19): the human nature “retaining all the properties of a
real body”. Ames: the heaven of the ascension “is not an *ubiquitary*
Heaven, as some doe imagine, so as that ascension should only be a change of
condition, and not of place” (p. 108).

**The Lutherans.** The Formula of Concord: the right hand of God “is no fixed
place in heaven, as the Sacramentarians assert without any ground in the
Holy Scriptures, but nothing else than the almighty power of God, which
fills heaven and earth” (Solid Declaration VIII.28); “God has and knows of
various modes of being in any place” (Epitome VII.14); rejected is the
teaching “That the body of Christ is so enclosed in heaven that it can in no
way be at once and at one time in many or all places upon earth where His
Holy Supper is celebrated” (Epitome VII, negative 11).

The dogmaticians do not deny that the ascension was local. Hollaz: Christ
went up “by a true, real, and local motion” (p. 380). Gerhard: Christ's
taking up “was a τοπικὴ μετάστασις, a local transfer, a visible elevation”
(p. 402). What they deny is that the place holds him. Gerhard rejects the
view that Christ, “residing in a local and bodily manner, He is held
restrained from being present upon earth in an invisible and illocal manner
before the day of judgment” (p. 403). Schmid: “Christ is also in heaven, yet
not according to local circumscription, but definitively and according to
the manner of a glorified body” (p. 402). And Gerhard argues from the last
judgment: “If the seat of majesty on which Christ will sit when He comes to
judgment has been removed so many miles from earth, how will all men, at one
and the same view, be able to see Him?” (p. 405).

**What the difference is not.** The translators of Schmid observe that
“Reformed theologians, when not treating the doctrine of the Lord's Supper,
often reach the same conclusions” on the right hand of God, and quote
Riissen: the session “can be understood not properly and literally, but
figuratively and metaphorically” (p. 406). **[inferred]** So both sides take
the right hand as a figure for Christ's rule. The difference is whether
Christ's human body, which is at the right hand, is in one place. The
Reformed say it is, because a body is in a place; the Lutherans say that a
glorified body is not bound to be.

**The text.** **[inferred]** Each side has its verse. For the Reformed, Acts
3:21: ὃν δεῖ οὐρανὸν μὲν δέξασθαι ἄχρι χρόνων ἀποκαταστάσεως πάντων, *whom
heaven must receive until the times of the restoration of all things*: a
reception for a time, with an end. For the Lutherans, Ephesians 4:10: ὁ
ἀναβὰς ὑπεράνω πάντων τῶν οὐρανῶν, ἵνα πληρώσῃ τὰ πάντα, *that he might fill
all things*, which Gerhard takes as convertible with sitting at the right
hand (p. 404). Neither verse names the Supper. Acts 3:21 speaks of the
human Christ's whereabouts; Ephesians 4:10 of the scope of his rule, as
Ephesians 1:22–23 explains it.

## Where they agree

- **The soul at death.** Schmid §63: the soul's lot, “immediately
  thereafter, is a happy or unhappy one”, and the Roman view “according to
  which not two, but five different places are to be assumed” is rejected
  (pp. 624–625). The Westminster Confession (32.1): “Besides these two
  places for souls separated from their bodies, the Scripture acknowledgeth
  none.” Both reject the sleep of the soul (Schmid §63; Scots Confession 17;
  Calvin's *Psychopannychia*, which the *Institutes* cite at III.25.6).
- **The ground of immortality.** Schmid: “For the immortality of the soul,
  reason has from time immemorial set up an array of proofs, but we become
  incontrovertibly certain of it through the positive declarations of the
  Holy Scriptures” (p. 624). Baier on the resurrection: “this certainly
  cannot be discovered from natural reason, although not opposed to it”
  (p. 640). Calvin likewise credits Plato with seeing that the chief good
  lies in union with God, while knowing “nothing of the sacred bond of that
  union” (*Institutes* III.25.2, p. 608). **[inferred]** Neither tradition
  would have accepted Mendelssohn's *Phædon* as a sufficient ground, or the
  Pittsburgh Platform's “grounding this belief on the divine nature of the
  human spirit”.
- **The identity of the raised body.** The Formula's “precisely the substance
  of this our flesh” and the Scots Confession's “the substance of the self
  same flesh” say the same thing. Hollaz explains the identity by “the
  reproduction or restoration of the same body which had perished by death,
  out of its atoms or particles which had been scattered thence and
  dispersed” (p. 640), as Perkins and Boston do on the Reformed side. See
  [resurrection-of-the-flesh.md](resurrection-of-the-flesh.md).
- **One general resurrection and judgment.** Quenstedt: “Since the second
  advent of Christ, the general resurrection, the final judgment, and the
  end of the world are immediately united, and one follows the other without
  an interval of time” (p. 650). The Reformed confessions have the same
  order without an interval.
- **Eternal life.** Quenstedt names “the beatific and immediate sight of God”
  among its chief blessings (p. 661); the Larger Catechism (90), “the
  immediate vision and fruition of God”. Gerhard: “By the name Heaven, that
  certain place (ποῦ) is to be understood, in which the blessed will see
  God” (p. 662).

## The glorified body

Quenstedt lists the glorified body's endowments: “Spirituality”,
“Invisibility”, “Impalpability”, “Illocality”, “Subtilty”, “Agility”,
“Impassibility”, “Immortality and incorruptibility”, “Strength and
soundness”, “Brilliancy”, “Beauty” (p. 662). For invisibility he cites 1
Corinthians 15:44, and for impalpability and illocality the same verse
(“ib.”). Gerhard, describing the same body, is more restrained: the raised
will be ἰσάγγελοι, “like the angels”, “not angels, not equal to, or the same
as angels”, and their bodies “spiritual, not indeed with respect to essence;
for they will not be spirits, but spiritual bodies” (p. 642).

The Reformed name the opposite qualities. Bullinger: “a true and verie
bodie, of the owne proper kinde, place, disposition, & of the owne proper
shape, and nature” (p. 88). Hodge: “As the resurrection bodies of believers
are to be human bodies they must have a local habitation” (p. 855). Calvin,
on Philippians 3:20, refuses both extremes: “as it were rash and foolish to
mount up beyond the heavens, and assign to Christ a station, or seat, or
place of walking, in this or that region, so it is a foolish and destructive
madness to draw him down from heaven by any carnal consideration” (p. 110).

The Jewish sources have raised bodies that stand, are recognized, and
move: the righteous “will be supplied with wings similar to the wings of
the eagles, and they will fly over the world” (Sanhedrin 92b, p. 277); the
Raavad's raised are “standing in their concrete form”, perhaps “in strong,
healthy forms as are the forms of angels” (Glazer, p. 431, n. 3).

**[inferred]** The qualities that 1 Corinthians 15:42–44 names are
incorruption, glory, power and a σῶμα πνευματικόν; Philippians 3:21 adds
conformity to Christ's glorious body. The risen Christ is seen, touched and
eats (Luke 24:39–43): σάρκα καὶ ὀστέα οὐκ ἔχει, καθὼς ἐμὲ θεωρεῖτε ἔχοντα.
Quenstedt's “Agility” has Christ's appearing and vanishing to argue from,
and the rabbis' eagles' wings agree with it. His “Invisibility”,
“Impalpability” and “Illocality” rest on reading πνευματικόν as removing
the properties of a body, which Paul does not say: the adjective qualifies
σῶμα, and Gerhard himself insists that the spiritual body is not a spirit.
On these three the Reformed have the text.

## The end of the world and the final home

**Annihilation.** Schmid §66: “After the final judgment, the absolute end of
this world will come; angels and men excepted, everything that belongs to
this world will be burnt up by fire and reduced to nothing. Not a
transformation of the world, therefore, but an absolute annihilation of its
substance is to be expected” (pp. 655–656). Quenstedt cites Psalm 102:26, 2
Peter 3:10, Revelation 20:11, Isaiah 34:4, Luke 21:33 and Job 14:12.

**Not the Book of Concord, nor Luther.** The Augsburg Confession says only
that Christ will appear “at the Consummation of the World”. Hodge reports
that Gerhard, holding annihilation, “admits, however, that many of the
fathers and Luther himself were on the other side”, and that “Luther was
wont to say: ‘The heavens have their work-day clothes on; hereafter they
will have on their Sunday garments.’” (p. 853).

**Renewal.** Most of the Reformed: the Belgic Confession (37), Christ
“burning this old world with fire and flame, to cleanse it”; Calvin on 2
Peter 3:10, the elements “are to be consumed, only that they may be
renovated, their substance still remaining the same” (p. 421); Hodge, “Our
bodies are not to be annihilated, but changed” (p. 852), and “Most of the
Reformed theologians generally oppose the idea of annihilation. Turrettin
certainly does” (p. 853). Wollebius and Edwards lean the other way (see
[new-heavens-and-new-earth.md](new-heavens-and-new-earth.md)).

**The order.** **[inferred]** The dogmaticians put the end of the world
after the judgment, so that the world is destroyed when it has no further
use. The Belgic Confession puts the burning at Christ's coming, as a
cleansing before the judgment. Edwards agrees with the Lutherans on the
order: “the destruction of the outward frame of the world itself, at the
conclusion of the day of judgment” (p. 174). The order follows the view of
the world's end, not the confession.

**The final home.** If the world is annihilated, the final home can only
be heaven, and the dogmaticians say so: “The place of their happiness is in
the Scriptures called Heaven” (Schmid §67, p. 656). The older Reformed who
also put it in the highest heaven (Watson, Boston, Edwards) agree in the
result; the Reformed who make the renewed earth the final seat (Ussher,
Poole, Hodge) have no Lutheran counterpart among the dogmaticians. See
[final-destination.md](final-destination.md).

**Against the Jewish sources.** **[inferred]** The rabbis teach renewal
(“passing away (which) does not (really) pass away”) or, with Maimonides,
permanence; none teaches annihilation. Isaiah 34:4, which Quenstedt cites
for it, is the verse Pirke de R. Eliezer reads as the rolling up of a
scroll that is opened again. On this question the Reformed majority stands
with the Jewish readers of the Hebrew, and the dogmaticians stand alone.

## Chiliasm and the Jews

**Chiliasm.** The Augsburg Confession (XVII) condemns “others who are now
spreading certain Jewish opinions, that before the resurrection of the
dead the godly shall take possession of the kingdom of the world, the
ungodly being everywhere suppressed.” The dogmaticians list as Chiliasts
“The Jews, Cerinthus, Papias, Joachim (Abbot of Floris), the Fanatics and
Anabaptists, Casp. Schwenkfeld, and others” (p. 650). Gerhard's survey of
their disagreements names a Reformed theologian among them: “Some hope that
all the godly and saints will first be raised, in order to become partners
in this kingdom; the Jews, that Israelites alone; Piscator, that the
martyrs alone” (p. 651). Calvin calls the Chiliasts' doctrine a “fiction …
too puerile to need or to deserve refutation” (*Institutes* III.25.5,
p. 615).

**[inferred]** Both confessions reject chiliasm, and both are right to call
it Jewish in shape: the rabbinic order puts the days of the Messiah before
the resurrection (see [jewish-sources.md](jewish-sources.md#the-order-of-the-last-things)).
The Reformed exceptions in this collection are Goodwin, whose saints of
the first resurrection reign on earth for a thousand years (“this reigning
is upon their rising from the dead”, p. 32), and Piscator as Gerhard
reports him. Gerhard's own reading of Revelation 20, which begins the
thousand years with Constantine (p. 651), and Calvin's, which refers them
to “the various troubles which await the Church militant in this world”
(p. 615), differ in detail and agree in rejecting an earthly reign.

**The conversion of the Jews.** Schmid: “some, even among Lutheran
theologians, enumerate the general conversion of the Jews. By the great
majority, however, this opinion is rejected” (p. 649). Hollaz: “their
universal, or their certainly manifest and solemn conversion about the time
of the end of the world, is not to be expected” (p. 649). He reads Romans
11:26 with the limitation of the chapter: “All Israel that God foreknew
would believe in Christ will be saved”, and takes ἄχρις οὗ in 11:25 as
“equipollent with always” (pp. 649–650). Wollebius reads the verse the same
way: the words “*until the fulness of the Gentiles come in*” do not mean
“that there shall be such a plausible calling of the Jews, after the
fulness of the Gentiles is come in” (p. 290). Goodwin reads it the other
way: “*Rom.* 11. 26. *All Israel shall be saved.* There he tels us of *a new
world* of the Jewes” (p. 29). Edwards counts “the calling of the Jews” among
the last stages of the world's history (p. 174).

**[inferred]** On Israel's future the line runs through the Reformed, not
between the confessions. The reading of ἄχρις οὗ as “always” is strained:
in Romans 11:25 the hardening lasts ἄχρις οὗ τὸ πλήρωμα τῶν ἐθνῶν
εἰσέλθῃ, and καὶ οὕτω πᾶς Ἰσραὴλ σωθήσεται follows as what happens then.
Goodwin and Edwards keep closer to the Greek here than Hollaz and Wollebius.
None of them, however, joins the conversion of Israel to a return to the
land, which is where the rabbinic hope places it.

## A shared caution

Both traditions warn against curiosity about the details, in nearly the same
words.

- Gerhard: the doctrine of heaven is set forth “not that we may idly dispute
  as theorists, concerning the locality of Heaven, the beatific vision, the
  properties of glorified bodies; but that, as practical men, considering
  the promised joys of eternal life every day … we may keep closely to the
  way leading thither” (p. 663). On the signs of the end: “lest we should
  boldly dare to search into that which God has placed in His own power and
  knowledge” (p. 648).
- Hollaz on the fire of the last day: “the power and nature of this no
  mortal is able to investigate” (p. 656).
- Calvin: “to pry curiously into their intermediate state is neither lawful
  nor expedient” (*Institutes* III.25.6, p. 617); on the place of departed
  souls, “the question is not less futile and inept” (p. 618); on the third
  heaven, “it does not belong to us to satisfy their curiosity” (p. 367).
- Maimonides, from the Jewish side: “Man ought not to study much the words
  of the Agadoth, neither dilate on the exposition regarding the Messianic
  advent, as they do not lead to any fear or love of the Lord” (Kings XII.2).

**[inferred]** The caution is applied unevenly on both sides. Quenstedt's
eleven endowments of the glorified body are the kind of thing Gerhard
warns against disputing, and Perkins's and Poole's taxonomy of the heavens
goes beyond Calvin's reserve.

## Assessment

**What divides them.** **[inferred]** The Lutheran and Reformed doctrines of
the last things are one doctrine with two disputed corollaries. The first,
the located or illocal glorified body, comes from the dispute over Christ's
presence in the Supper and is carried into eschatology by both sides; on
the Greek of Luke 24:39 and Acts 3:21 the Reformed have the stronger case,
and the Lutheran argument from πνευματικόν asks more of the adjective than
Paul gives it. The second, annihilation or renewal, is not a confessional
difference: the dogmaticians' annihilation is not in the Book of Concord,
and Luther, as Gerhard admitted, held the other view. On the text (Rom.
8:21; 2 Pet. 3:6, 13; Acts 3:21's ἀποκατάστασις) renewal has the better
case, and the Jewish readers of the Hebrew read Isaiah 34:4 and 51:6 the
same way.

**What unites them.** **[inferred]** Against the Jewish positions that
differ from both, the two confessions stand together: against the
Messianic age before the resurrection, which they both call Jewish and both
reject; against Maimonides' bodiless world to come; against the Reform
platforms' immortality without resurrection. With the rabbis they hold the
resurrection of the same body, the soul's return to God at death, and a
judgment of body and soul together. The difference between Wittenberg and
Geneva on the last things is narrower than the difference between either
of them and Maimonides, and much narrower than the difference between
either and Pittsburgh.
