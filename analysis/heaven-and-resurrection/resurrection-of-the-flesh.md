# The Resurrection of the Body, or “of the Flesh”

Part of [Heaven, the New Earth and the Resurrection of the Body](README.md).

Is the body that is raised the same body that died, and in what sense the
same? Is it flesh? What changes, and what does not? What did the Reformed
teach, how does it stand against the Hebrew and the Greek, and how do the
Lutheran and Jewish teachers compare?

## Summary

1. **The Reformed teach that the very body that died is raised: the same in
   substance, changed in its qualities.** The confessions say so in
   nearly the same words: “the self-same bodies, and none other, although
   with different qualities” (Westminster Confession 32.2); “their proper
   bodies, in which they formerly lived” (Belgic 37); “in the substance of
   the self same flesh that every man now bears” (Scots 25). Calvin: “the
   substance indeed is the same, but there is a difference as to quality”.
2. **They keep the word “flesh”.** The risen Christ had “flesh, bones, and
   all things appertaining to the perfection of Man's nature” (Thirty-Nine
   Articles 4), and Calvin: “we shall rise again in that same flesh that we
   now carry about with us”. The “spiritual body” of 1 Corinthians 15:44 is
   spiritual “not as to the substance of it, (for in that sense a *spiritual
   Body* is a contradiction;)” (Poole).
3. **The Greek supports this.** The subject of σπείρεται … ἐγείρεται (1 Cor.
   15:42–44) is one and the same; “this corruptible” (τὸ φθαρτὸν τοῦτο, with
   the demonstrative) puts on incorruption (15:53); “the body of our
   humiliation” (τὸ σῶμα τῆς ταπεινώσεως ἡμῶν) is changed in fashion (Phil.
   3:21); “your mortal bodies” are made alive (Rom. 8:11); and the risen
   Christ has σάρκα καὶ ὀστέα (Luke 24:39). ψυχικόν and πνευματικόν both
   qualify σῶμα: the contrast is between two bodies, not between body and
   spirit.
4. **The Reformed differ on what “the same” requires.** Perkins and Boston
   explain it by the same particles of matter; Hodge (1872) holds that
   identity is certain but its ground unknown, and that it may be “an
   identity independent of sameness of substance”.
5. **The Lutherans teach the same identity in nearly the same words**
   (Formula of Concord: “precisely the substance of this our flesh, but
   without sin, will rise again”; Quenstedt: “the same in number and
   essence”). **They differ on the qualities**: Quenstedt counts among the
   glorified body's endowments “Invisibility”, “Impalpability” and
   “Illocality”, while the Reformed insist on a “true and verie bodie, of
   the owne proper kinde, place” (Bullinger) that “must have a local
   habitation” (Hodge).
6. **The rabbinic teachers held the resurrection of the body as an article
   of faith** (“He who says that there is no allusion in the Torah
   concerning resurrection” has no share in the world to come, Mishnah
   Sanhedrin 10:1), raised with its blemishes and then healed, and “dressed”
   like the wheat that is sown bare. Maimonides affirmed it, but made the
   world to come bodiless; the Reform rabbis of 1869 and 1885 gave it up,
   and Kohler called it “obsolete for the modern Jew”.

## The text

**Job 19:25–27.** ([passage](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#job-19-25-27).)
The Reformed proof text for the resurrection of the same flesh. The Hebrew
is hard: וּמִבְּשָׂרִי אֶחֱזֶה אֱלוֹהַּ, *and from my flesh I shall see God*,
where מִן may mean “out of” (seeing from within the flesh: KJV “in my
flesh”) or “apart from”. What is plain is the identity of the one who sees:
אֲשֶׁר אֲנִי אֶחֱזֶה־לִּי וְעֵינַי רָאוּ וְלֹא־זָר, “whom I shall see for myself, and
mine eyes shall behold, and not another”. The Septuagint is a different
text and has the resurrection outright: ἀναστῆσαι τὸ δέρμα μου, *to raise up
my skin*. **[inferred]** The Hebrew allows the Reformed reading and does
not compel it.

**Isaiah 26:19; Daniel 12:2.** ([Isa.](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#isa-26-19),
[Dan.](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#dan-12-2-3).) יִחְיוּ מֵתֶיךָ
נְבֵלָתִי יְקוּמוּן, *thy dead shall live, my corpse shall arise* (נְבֵלָה is a
dead body); the Greek ἀναστήσονται οἱ νεκροὶ καὶ ἐγερθήσονται οἱ ἐν τοῖς
μνημείοις, “the dead shall rise, and they that are in the tombs shall be
raised”, the words John 5:28 takes up. Daniel: וְרַבִּים מִיְּשֵׁנֵי
אַדְמַת־עָפָר יָקִיצוּ, *many of them that sleep in the ground of dust shall
awake*, some לְחַיֵּי עוֹלָם, *to everlasting life*, the only occurrence of the
phrase in the Hebrew Bible. Both texts raise those who are in the dust and
the tombs: bodies.

**1 Corinthians 15:35–54.** ([35–44](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#1cor-15-35-44),
[50–54](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#1cor-15-50-54).)
- The question is πῶς ἐγείρονται οἱ νεκροί; ποίῳ δὲ σώματι ἔρχονται; “with
  what body do they come?” The answer is the seed: οὐ τὸ σῶμα τὸ γενησόμενον
  σπείρεις, ἀλλὰ γυμνὸν κόκκον, “thou sowest not that body that shall be,
  but bare grain” (γυμνός, naked).
- σπείρεται ἐν φθορᾷ, ἐγείρεται ἐν ἀφθαρσίᾳ … σπείρεται σῶμα ψυχικόν,
  ἐγείρεται σῶμα πνευματικόν. The subject of the four pairs of verbs is
  unexpressed and the same: what is sown is what is raised.
- ψυχικός is “of the ψυχή”, animated by the soul, the adjective of Genesis
  2:7 LXX (εἰς ψυχὴν ζῶσαν, quoted in v. 45); πνευματικός is “of the
  πνεῦμα”, governed by the Spirit. Both qualify σῶμα.
- σὰρξ καὶ αἷμα βασιλείαν θεοῦ κληρονομῆσαι οὐ δύναται (v. 50) is explained
  by the parallel clause, οὐδὲ ἡ φθορὰ τὴν ἀφθαρσίαν κληρονομεῖ: man in his
  mortal, corruptible state. The phrase is used of man's frailty in
  Matthew 16:17 and Galatians 1:16.
- δεῖ γὰρ τὸ φθαρτὸν τοῦτο ἐνδύσασθαι ἀφθαρσίαν (v. 53): “this
  corruptible” must put on incorruption, as a garment is put on (the
  ἐνδύω of 2 Cor. 5:2–4).

**Philippians 3:21; Romans 8:11.** ([Phil.](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#phil-3-20-21),
[Rom.](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#rom-8-11).)
μετασχηματίσει τὸ σῶμα τῆς ταπεινώσεως ἡμῶν σύμμορφον τῷ σώματι τῆς δόξης
αὐτοῦ: the body “of our humiliation” is the object of the change; it is
re-fashioned (σχῆμα) and conformed (μορφή), not replaced.
ζῳοποιήσει καὶ τὰ θνητὰ σώματα ὑμῶν: “shall also quicken your mortal
bodies”.

**Luke 24:39.** ([passage](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#luke-24-39).)
ψηλαφήσατέ με καὶ ἴδετε, ὅτι πνεῦμα σάρκα καὶ ὀστέα οὐκ ἔχει καθὼς ἐμὲ
θεωρεῖτε ἔχοντα: “handle me, and see; for a spirit hath not flesh and
bones, as ye see me have.” The risen body is flesh, and can be touched.

**Matthew 22:29–32.** ([passage](../../scripture/last-things/passages.md#matt-22-29-32).)
In the resurrection they neither marry nor are given in marriage, ἀλλ᾿ ὡς
ἄγγελοι, “but are as the angels”: like them in this, not made angels. Jesus
proves the resurrection from the Torah (Exod. 3:6): “I am the God of
Abraham … God is not the God of the dead, but of the living.”

## The Reformed theologians

### Calvin

([Commentaries](../../theologians/reformed/continental/calvin/commentaries-on-the-last-things.md);
[*Institutes* III.25](../../theologians/reformed/continental/calvin/institutes-book-3-chapter-25.md);
[Sermon on Job 19](../../theologians/reformed/continental/calvin/sermon-on-job-19-26-29.md).)

- On 1 Corinthians 15:39 (p. 48): under the name of flesh Paul includes man
  and beast, and “the substance indeed is the same, but there is a
  difference as to quality.” On 15:44 (p. 51): “the substance of the body
  is the same, and that it is the quality only that is here treated of”,
  against “those … who suppose that the substance of the body will be
  spiritual, while there is no mention made here of substance, and no
  change will be made upon it.”
- On 15:53 (p. 61): “This passage, too, distinctly proves, that we shall
  rise again in that same flesh that we now carry about with us, as the
  Apostle assigns a new quality to it which will serve as a garment. If he
  had said, *This corruptible must be renewed*, the error of those
  fanatics, who imagine that mankind will be furnished with new bodies,
  would not have been so plainly or forcibly overthrown.”
- *Institutes* III.25.7–8 (pp. 619–623), against those who “hold that they
  will be clothed with new bodies, and thus deny the resurrection of the
  flesh”: “If God formed new bodies, where would be this change of
  quality?”; “if we are to receive new bodies, where will be the conformity
  of the Head and the members?”; Daniel 12:2 “does not bring new matter from
  the four elements to compose men, but calls forth the dead from their
  graves.” And: “the body in which we shall rise will be the same as at
  present in respect of substance, but that the quality will be different;
  just as the body of Christ which was raised up was the same as that which
  had been offered in sacrifice”.
- Sermon on Job 19:26 (p. 368): Job “trusteth too see God, yea and too see
  him in his fleshe, that is to say, by beeing restored againe.”

### The English, Scottish and Swiss writers

- **Perkins**, *Exposition of the Creed* (1595), p. 526: “The same bodies

- **Poole's *Annotations*** on 1 Corinthians 15 (image 457): the spiritual

- **Boston**, *Fourfold State* (pp. 253–255): “It is sufficient to denominate

  ([text](../../theologians/reformed/british/boston/fourfold-state-resurrection-and-heaven.md))
- **Dickson** (p. 337) asks whether “many of the *Quakers* err, who maintain

  ([text](../../theologians/reformed/british/dickson/truths-victory-over-error-chapters-32-33.md))
- **Vincent** (p. 86): “'tis fit the same Body that was an instrument of

### Hodge: identity without a theory of it

Hodge (*Systematic Theology* III, pp. 774–784) holds the identity as firmly
as any: “identity is involved in the very idea of a resurrection; for
resurrection is a living again of that which was dead; not of something of
the same nature, but of the very thing itself” (p. 774). But he declines
the particle theory. He sets out identity in unorganized matter, in works of
art, and in living things, and concludes of the last: “Here, then, is an
identity independent of sameness of substance” (p. 777). His aim is “to
show that it is perfectly rational for a man to assert the identity between
our present and our future bodies, although he is forced to admit that he
does not know wherein that identity is to consist.”

He adds:

- “our present bodies, that is, our bodies as now organized, consisting as
  they do of flesh and blood, are not adapted to our future state of being”;
- “the glorified people of God will not be cumbered with useless organs”
  (p. 780);
- “our bodies after the resurrection will retain the human form” (p. 780);
- “σῶμα πνευματικόν cannot by possibility mean a body made of spirit” (p.
  784).

([text](../../theologians/reformed/princeton/hodge/systematic-theology-vol-3-last-things.md))

**[inferred]** Hodge's “identity independent of sameness of substance”
moves away from the confessional phrase “in the substance of the self same
flesh” (Scots 25) and from Calvin's “the substance … is the same”. He does
not deny the confessional statement; he refuses to say what the sameness
consists in.

## The Lutheran teaching

- **Large Catechism**, Creed III, §57: “we expect that our flesh will be
  destroyed and buried with all its uncleanness, and will come forth
  gloriously, and arise to entire and perfect holiness in a new eternal
  life.” §60: “the term Auferstehung des Fleisches (resurrection of the
  flesh) here employed is not according to good German idiom”, because
  Germans hearing *Fleisch* think “of the shambles”; “in good German idiom
  we would say Auferstehung des Leibes, or Leichnams (resurrection of the
  body). However, it is not a matter of much moment, if we only understand
  the words aright.”
- **Formula of Concord**, Solid Declaration I.46: “Scripture testifies that
  precisely the substance of this our flesh, but without sin, will rise
  again, and that in eternal life we shall have and retain precisely this
  soul, but without sin.” Epitome I.10 cites Job 19:26: “I shall be
  compassed again with this my skin, and in my flesh shall I see God”.
- **Schmid** §64
  ([text](../../theologians/lutheran/heinrich-schmid/doctrinal-theology-last-things.md)):
  “This will be, in substance, the same body with which the soul was united
  in this life, but endowed with new attributes” (p. 640). Quenstedt: “the
  body, the same in number and essence as we have borne in this life”, and “The
  body which will rise again will be spiritual, not as to substance, but as
  to qualities and endowments.” Hollaz: “the reproduction or restoration of
  the same body which had perished by death, out of its atoms or particles
  which had been scattered thence and dispersed”. Chemnitz: the bodies
  “will have and retain their nature or bodily substance, as Job says”, and
  as the Creed confesses “the resurrection of this flesh”.
- **The glorified body's endowments** (Quenstedt, §67, p. 662):
  “Spirituality”, “Invisibility”, “Impalpability”, “Illocality”, “Subtilty”,
  “Agility”, “Impassibility”, “Immortality and incorruptibility”, “Strength
  and soundness”, “Brilliancy”, “Beauty”.

**[inferred]** On the identity of the body the two confessions are one; the
Formula's “precisely the substance of this our flesh” and the Scots
Confession's “the substance of the self same flesh” could be exchanged. On
the qualities they part. “Invisibility”, “Impalpability” and “Illocality”
are not the qualities of Luke 24:39 (“handle me, and see”), and the
Reformed name the opposite: “a real body” (Belgic 19), “place” (Bullinger),
“a local habitation” (Hodge). The difference follows the Lord's Supper:
the Lutherans argue that the glorified body of Christ is not bound to one
place, and the Reformed that it remains a true body in heaven. See
[lutheran-and-reformed.md](lutheran-and-reformed.md).

## The Jewish teachers

Fuller treatment, with the history, is in [jewish-sources.md](jewish-sources.md).

- **The Mishnah** (Sanhedrin 10:1, Rodkinson, p. 265): “The following have no
  share in the world to come: He who says that there is no allusion in the
  Torah concerning resurrection”. The Gemara gives the proofs, among them R.
  Gamaliel's from the Prophets, Isaiah 26:19, “Thy dead shall live, my dead
  bodies shall arise” (p. 267).
- **The same body, clothed.** Cleopatra asks R. Meir whether the dead, when
  restored, “will they be naked or dressed?” He answers “by an a fortiori conclusion from wheat.
  A grain of wheat which is buried naked comes out dressed in so many
  garments: the upright, who are buried in their dress, so much the more
  shall they come out dressed in many garments” (p. 268). Paul's γυμνὸν
  κόκκον (1 Cor. 15:37) is the same image.
- **Body and soul judged together.** Antoninus and Rabbi: the lame and the
  blind watchmen, whom the owner set one upon the other and “punished them
  together” (Sanhedrin 91a–b).
- **Raised as they were, then healed.** “They will be restored with the
  blemishes they had in their life, and thereafter they will be cured” (p.
  273); “The upright who will be restored in the future by the Holy One,
  blessed be He, will never return to dust” (p. 277).
- **From what remains.** Pirke de R. Eliezer 34 (p. 258): the body crumbles
  “until nothing remains of the body except a spoonful of earthy matter”,
  which, “like the yeast which is mixed with the dough, improves and
  increases, and it raises up all the body.” Bereshit Rabbah 28:3: the
  *luz* of the spine, from which man will be made to sprout.
  ([text](../../theologians/rabbinic/midrash-and-prayer-book-on-the-resurrection.md))
- **The daily prayer** (the second blessing of the *Amidah*, Singer, pp.
  44–45): “Blessed art thou, O Lord, who quickenest the dead.”
- **2 Baruch** 50–51 (pp. 82–84): the earth will restore the dead “making no
  change in their form, but as it has received, so will it restore them”,
  that they may be recognized; afterwards the righteous are transformed
  “into the splendour of angels”.
- **Josephus**: the Pharisees say “that all souls are incorruptible, but that
  the souls of good men only are removed into other bodies”; the
  Sadducees, “That souls die with the bodies” (War II.8.14; Antiquities
  XVIII.1.4).
- **Maimonides** (commentary on Sanhedrin X, Abelson, p. 42): “The
  Resurrection of the Dead is one of the cardinal doctrines of the Law of
  Moses”, but “the reward of the righteous only”. Yet the world to come
  “harbors neither body nor aught of a concrete form” (Repentance VIII.2).
  The Raavad answers from the sages, who said that the righteous rise “in
  their garments” (Ketubot 111b) and “will never again return unto dust,
  but remain standing in their concrete form” (Sanhedrin 92a), and that
  Maimonides' words “are in my eyes nigh to such who assert that there is no
  resurrection to body but the souls alone” (Glazer, p. 431, n. 3).
  ([text](../../theologians/jewish-medieval/maimonides/world-to-come-and-resurrection.md))
- **Reform.** Philadelphia, 1869 (art. 6): “The belief in the bodily
  resurrection has no religious foundation, and the doctrine of immortality
  refers to the after-existence of the soul only.” Pittsburgh, 1885 (art.
  7): “We reject, as ideas not rooted in Judaism, the beliefs both in bodily
  resurrection and in Gehenna and Eden”. Kohler (1918, p. 395): the belief
  “is in such utter contradiction to our entire attitude toward both science
  and religion, that it may be considered obsolete for the modern Jew.”
  ([text](../../theologians/jewish-modern/reform-judaism-on-immortality-and-resurrection.md))

## Assessment

**The identity of the body.** **[inferred]** The Greek of 1 Corinthians 15
and Philippians 3 states the identity that the confessions confess: one
subject sown and raised, “this corruptible” clothed, “the body of our
humiliation” re-fashioned, “your mortal bodies” quickened. The seed of 15:37
shows both continuity (it is this seed that comes up) and change (“not that
body that shall be”). The Reformed formula, the same substance with
different qualities, is a fair summary, and so is the Lutheran one; the
Formula's word “precisely” goes no further than the demonstrative τοῦτο.

**What the text does not decide.** **[inferred]** Whether the same body
requires the same particles (Perkins, Boston, Hollaz) the text does not
say; Hodge's refusal to explain the identity keeps to what is written.

**“Flesh.”** **[inferred]** σὰρξ καὶ αἷμα in 15:50 is answered by φθορά
in the same verse; Luke 24:39 says the risen Christ has σάρξ. The Reformed
reading, that flesh and blood in their mortal state do not inherit, while
the flesh itself is raised, holds both texts together; Luther's preference
for *Leib* over *Fleisch* concerns German idiom, not doctrine, as he says.

**The qualities.** **[inferred]** The qualities that the texts name are
incorruption, glory, power, and spirituality (1 Cor. 15:42–44), and
likeness to Christ's glorious body (Phil. 3:21). The risen Christ is seen,
touched, and eats (Luke 24:39–43). Quenstedt's “Invisibility” and
“Impalpability” have the appearances and disappearances of the risen
Christ to argue from (Luke 24:31), but not his own words in Luke 24:39. The
Reformed “true body” has the better claim to the text.

**The Jewish teachers.** **[inferred]** On the resurrection of the body the
Reformed confessions stand with the Mishnah, the Amidah, the Thirteen
Principles and the Raavad, and against the Sadducees, Maimonides'
bodiless world to come, and the Reform platforms. The rabbinic images, the
wheat that rises dressed, the dead raised with their blemishes and then
healed, the body raised from what remains of it, are images of the same
body changed, which is the Reformed and Lutheran doctrine. Jesus' own proof
in Matthew 22:32 is from the Torah, which is where the Mishnah demands that
the resurrection be found.
