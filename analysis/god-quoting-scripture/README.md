# God Quoting Scripture

How many times in Scripture does God himself, including Jesus, quote his own
written word? Of what kinds are those quotations: word for word, adapted, or
in other words? And does the wording stand closer to the Septuagint, to
another Greek witness, or to the Hebrew?

The New Testament is read here in Greek, as the text itself. No argument is
drawn from an Aramaic or Hebrew original reconstructed beneath it. Where the
text gives words in a Semitic form and renders them into Greek, this study
reports what the text gives and builds nothing on it.

**[context]** marks a statement resting on history outside the texts quoted;
**[inferred]** marks this study's own reading rather than what a text says in
so many words.

## Summary

1. **In the New Testament God quotes earlier Scripture at 114 places.**
   Jesus speaks 105 of them (98 in his ministry and 7 as the risen Christ in
   Revelation). The Father's voice from heaven speaks 7, at the baptism and
   the transfiguration, counted in each Gospel and in 2 Peter 1:17. God on
   the throne in Revelation 21 speaks 2. Leaving out the 26 borderline
   places, where a short phrase or a likeness without a formula could be an
   allusion, there are **88**. Merging the Gospel parallels of one saying,
   there are **70 distinct sayings** (54 without the borderline). The
   Textus Receptus and the Byzantine text have 2 more, because they give
   Mark 9:48 three times (9:44, 9:46).
2. **In the Old Testament the LORD repeats the words of an earlier book at
   52 places** (50 without the borderline). In 42 he speaks through a
   prophet, in 8 in his own person, and in 2 as the Angel of the LORD.
   Nineteen more repetitions are counted apart: 5 within the Pentateuch, 9
   within one prophetic book, and 5 parallel oracles whose direction the
   texts do not show. The two Testaments together give **166** places, or
   138 without the borderline.
3. **By type, the New Testament has 42 direct quotations, 67 adapted and 5
   paraphrases.** Without the borderline the figures are 41, 44 and 3.
   Direct quotations reproduce on average 90% of their words in the
   Septuagint's order, adapted ones 57%, paraphrases 34%. The Old Testament
   has 11 direct, 31 adapted and 10 paraphrases.
4. **Only Jesus in his ministry introduces a quotation as Scripture.** He
   does so 57 times out of 98, most often with γέγραπται, "it is written"
   (20). The risen Christ, the Father and God on the throne never use a
   formula: the words of Scripture are simply theirs.
5. **Where the Septuagint and the Hebrew differ and the quotation takes
   one side, it takes the Septuagint's 17 times and the Hebrew's 5** (15 to
   2 without the borderline). Another 26 reproduce the
   Septuagint's wording where the Hebrew agrees in sense, so 43 of the 114
   stand with the Septuagint as printed.
6. **Fourteen follow a Greek witness other than Brenton's text and Codex
   Vaticanus**: Alexandrinus most often, then Theodotion and the
   Hexaplaric versions. In 6 of these the other witness has the Hebrew's
   sense, in 5 it is another Greek wording of the same Hebrew, and in 3
   Alexandrinus stands against both Vaticanus and the Hebrew.
7. **The rest are mixed (11), independent of both (14), undetermined
   because the Septuagint and the Hebrew agree (22), given in a Semitic form
   (2), or dependent on the edition of the New Testament (3).**
8. **No single Greek manuscript or edition accounts for the quotations.**
   They agree with Vaticanus against Brenton's text in 4 entries and with
   Brenton against Vaticanus in 6 (Mark 10:19 does both). Alexandrinus is with them in 19 entries.
   In Daniel, Matthew 24:30 and 26:64 follow the Old Greek and Matthew
   24:21 and Mark 13:19 follow Theodotion, and Mark 13:26 and Luke 21:27
   follow neither.
9. **The edition of the New Testament changes the affinity in 3 places**
   (Matthew 11:23, Mark 10:19, Mark 14:62), and changes the wording in
   several more. The TR and Byzantine text are nearer the Septuagint in some
   (Matthew 22:44 and Mark 12:36 ὑποπόδιον; Luke 23:46 in the TR; the
   longer forms of Luke 4:4, 4:18 and Matthew 15:8), the SBL in others.
10. **In the Old Testament the Septuagint keeps the verbal link between
    the two passages, wholly or in part, in 46 of the 52.** It lacks the
    quoting passage in 3 (Jeremiah 33:14-16, 33:17 and 48:45-46), and
    renders the two places in different words in 1 (2 Chronicles 24:20).
    Deuteronomy is the book God quotes most, and Jeremiah the book in which
    he quotes most.

## Scope and method

### Who counts as God speaking

Mahler's question names God himself, to include Jesus. This study counts
the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit as speakers:

- **Jesus** in the Gospels, including Luke 4:18-19, where he reads Isaiah
  in the synagogue and says it is fulfilled in their hearing.
- **The risen and exalted Christ** in Revelation (1:17; the letters to the
  churches; 22:12-13).
- **The Father**, as the voice from heaven at the baptism and the
  transfiguration. 2 Peter 1:17 reports the transfiguration voice, and is
  counted as the Father's words in Peter's report.
- **God on the throne** in Revelation 21:5-7.
- **The Holy Spirit.** No place was found where the Spirit, as a speaker
  in the narrative, quotes earlier Scripture. The Spirit speaks in Acts
  8:29, 10:19-20, 13:2 and 21:11 (through Agabus), and in Revelation 14:13
  and 22:17, and none of these words is a quotation. Where an apostle
  introduces Scripture as what the Spirit says (Acts 28:25; Hebrews 3:7,
  10:15), the speaker is the apostle; those places are in the
  [appendix](#appendix-scripture-given-as-gods-words-by-human-speakers).
  In the Old Testament two of the prophets counted here speak when the
  Spirit comes upon them (2 Chronicles 20:14, 24:20).
- **In the Old Testament, the LORD** in his own first person, or by a
  prophet's "thus says the LORD", or as the Angel of the LORD who speaks as
  the LORD (Judges 2:1-3).

Places where an apostle, an evangelist or another human speaker gives Old
Testament words as God's (Matthew 1:22; Acts 2:17; Hebrews 1:5-13 and many
more) are not counted. They are listed in the appendix.

### What counts as a quotation

A place is counted when the speaker's words reproduce the wording of an
earlier text, whether or not they are introduced as Scripture. Each place
has one of three types:

- **Direct**: the words of the source, verbatim or nearly so. They may be
  trimmed at the edges, with no more than a particle, article or pronoun
  changed.
- **Adapted**: recognizably the source's wording, but abridged, reordered,
  changed in person, mood or number, or with words substituted.
- **Paraphrase**: the source's content in largely other words, or a summary
  of a law.

Allusions, where a phrase or image recalls a text without reproducing it,
are listed apart and not counted. So are appeals to Scripture that quote
nothing ("the Son of Man goes as it is written of him").

Each place is also flagged:

- **Formula**: introduced as Scripture or as spoken ("it is written",
  "have you not read", "it was said", "God said").
- **Composite**: two or more texts fused in one citation.
- **Borderline**: no formula, and either a short phrase or a likeness that
  could be an allusion. Borderline places are counted, and left out of the
  strict count.

### Counting

Each place where a quotation is spoken counts once. Two texts fused in one
citation count once (Matthew 11:10 fuses Malachi 3:1 and Exodus 23:20).
Texts quoted one after another count separately (Matthew 15:4 quotes
Exodus 20:12 and then 21:17). The Gospel parallels of one saying count
once each as occurrences, and once together as a saying. The counts are
taken on the text common to the SBL edition, the Textus Receptus and the
Byzantine text, with the differences noted.

### The texts

- **New Testament**: the SBL Greek New Testament, the Textus Receptus and
  the Byzantine text of 1904.
- **Hebrew**: the Masoretic Text in the Westminster Leningrad Codex.
- **Septuagint**: Brenton's Greek text, which follows the Sixtine edition
  of Codex Vaticanus; and Swete's, from lxx.confident.faith. Swete's text
  is Codex Vaticanus (B) itself, except where B is lost: there it is
  Alexandrinus (A) in Genesis 1:1–46:28, and Sinaiticus (ℵ) in Psalms
  105:27–137:6 (Greek numbering).
- **Daniel**: Brenton prints Theodotion's version. Swete prints both the
  Old Greek and Theodotion, and lxx.confident.faith gives the Old Greek.
- **Other Greek witnesses**: the readings of A, ℵ, Q (Marchalianus), F
  (Ambrosianus) and Γ in Swete's apparatus.
- **Aquila, Symmachus and Theodotion**: Field's *Origenis Hexaplorum quae
  supersunt* (1875).
- **Göttingen**: readings noted on lxx.confident.faith (Genesis and the
  Psalms), cited and not copied.

[quotations.md](../../scripture/divine-quotations/quotations.md) prints each
quotation with these texts.

### Affinity

Each New Testament quotation has an affinity, decided by the points where
the witnesses differ and the quotation sides with one of them:

| Affinity | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Septuagint against the Hebrew (`lxx`) | Agrees with the Septuagint where it differs from the Hebrew. |
| Septuagint wording (`lxx-wording`) | Reproduces the Septuagint's wording, and the Hebrew agrees in sense. |
| Another Greek witness (`other-greek`) | Agrees with A, ℵ, Theodotion or the Hexaplaric versions where Brenton and Swete's text differ from it. |
| Hebrew against the Septuagint (`mt`) | Agrees with the Hebrew where the Septuagint differs, in wording no Greek witness shares. |
| Mixed (`mixed`) | Septuagint at one point, the Hebrew or another witness at another. |
| Independent (`independent`) | Agrees with neither against the other. |
| Undetermined (`undetermined`) | The Septuagint and the Hebrew agree, and the Greek is what any literal rendering would give. |
| Semitic (`semitic`) | The text gives the words in a Semitic form and renders them into Greek. |
| Varies (`varies`) | Depends on the edition of the New Testament. |

In Daniel "the Septuagint" means the Old Greek, and Theodotion is another
Greek witness.

## Types

| Type | With a formula | Without | All | Strict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Direct quotation | 31 | 11 | 42 | 41 |
| Adapted quotation | 22 | 45 | 67 | 44 |
| Paraphrase or summary | 4 | 1 | 5 | 3 |
| **All** | **57** | **57** | **114** | **88** |

**Direct quotations are mostly introduced as Scripture.** Of the 42, 31
have a formula. The 11 without one are quoted so exactly that the source is
plain: Matthew 5:5 (Psalm 37:11), Matthew 11:29 (Jeremiah 6:16, borderline),
Matthew 12:40 (Jonah 1:17), Mark 10:6-8
(Genesis 1:27 and 2:24), Matthew 23:39 and Luke 13:35 (Psalm 118:26),
Matthew 27:46 and Mark 15:34 (Psalm 22:1), Mark 13:14 (Daniel), Luke 23:46
(Psalm 31:5).

**Adapted quotations are the largest group, and most have no formula.**
They fit the words of Scripture into Jesus' own sentence. In Matthew
10:35-36 Micah's list of a divided household becomes the purpose of his
coming. In Mark 4:12 Isaiah 6:9-10 becomes the purpose of the parables, in
the third person. In Matthew 24:29-30 Isaiah 13:10, 34:4 and Daniel 7:13
describe his coming. In Revelation the risen Christ speaks Isaiah's "I am
the first and the last" (44:6, 48:12) of himself.

**The five paraphrases** are Matthew 5:31 (the bill of divorce,
Deuteronomy 24:1), Matthew 5:33 (the laws on oaths and vows), Luke 12:53
(Micah 7:6 made into pairs), John 7:38 ("as the Scripture said", the living
water of several passages) and John 8:17 (the law of two witnesses).

**How the quotations are introduced.** Of Jesus' 57 formulas:

- 20 use "it is written" (γέγραπται, γεγραμμένον).
- 8 use "it was said" (ἐρρέθη, εἴρηται, τὸ ῥηθέν): the six antitheses of
  Matthew 5, Luke 4:12 and Matthew 24:15.
- 6 use "have you not read" (ἀνέγνωτε).
- 3 speak of fulfilment or prophecy (Matthew 13:14, 15:7, John 13:18).
- 20 introduce the words in other ways. Matthew 15:4 has "God said";
  Matthew 19:4-5 has "he who created them … said"; Matthew 22:31-32 has "what
  was spoken to you by God". Mark 12:36 has "David himself said in the Holy
  Spirit", and Mark 7:10 "Moses said", which Jesus calls "the word of God"
  three verses later (7:13). Others name the commandments, or answer the
  question which commandment is greatest in the law.

So Jesus names God as the speaker of words written by Moses (Exodus 20:12,
Genesis 2:24, Exodus 3:6) and calls a Psalm "your law" (John 10:34, 15:25).

## Septuagint, another Greek witness, or the Hebrew

### The answer in one table

| Affinity | All | Strict | Sayings |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Septuagint against the Hebrew | 17 | 15 | 8 |
| Septuagint wording, Hebrew agreeing in sense | 26 | 23 | 15 |
| Another Greek witness against Brenton and Swete's text | 14 | 12 | 10 |
| Hebrew against the Septuagint | 5 | 2 | 3 |
| Mixed | 11 | 6 | 9 |
| Independent of both | 14 | 5 | 6 |
| Undetermined (the Septuagint and the Hebrew agree) | 22 | 21 | 11 |
| Given in a Semitic form, with a Greek rendering | 2 | 2 | 1 |
| Depends on the edition of the New Testament | 3 | 2 | 0 |
| The Gospel parallels of one saying differ | | | 7 |
| **All** | **114** | **88** | **70** |

Put more simply:

- **43 stand with the Septuagint as printed** (the first two rows): 38 of
  the strict 88.
- **14 stand with another Greek witness.**
- **5 stand with the Hebrew alone** (2 of the strict 88).
- **11 combine the Septuagint and the Hebrew**, and 14 follow neither.
- **22 cannot be decided**, because the two agree.

Of the 92 that can be decided, then, 57 follow a Greek form of the Old
Testament, Brenton's, Swete's or another. Where the Septuagint and the
Hebrew diverge and the quotation clearly takes one side, it takes
the Septuagint's 17 times and the Hebrew's 5. Taking the other Greek
witnesses into account changes the picture: in 6 places they have the
Hebrew's sense, and the New Testament has it with them.

### Septuagint against the Hebrew (17)

| Where | Source | The Septuagint's reading the New Testament has |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Matthew 4:7; Luke 4:12 | Deuteronomy 6:16 | "you (singular) shall not tempt", for the Hebrew's plural |
| Matthew 13:14-15 | Isaiah 6:9-10 | ἐπαχύνθη, "the heart has grown fat", a statement where the Hebrew commands the prophet to make it fat; and ἰάσομαι, "I will heal them" |
| Matthew 15:8-9; Mark 7:6-7 | Isaiah 29:13 | μάτην δὲ σέβονταί με, "in vain they worship me", where the Hebrew has "their fear of me is a commandment of men learned by rote" |
| Matthew 19:5; Mark 10:7-8 | Genesis 2:24 | οἱ δύο, "the two" shall be one flesh; the Hebrew has no "two" |
| Luke 18:20 | Deuteronomy 5:17-19 | B's order of the commandments, adultery before murder, against the Hebrew and Brenton |
| Matthew 21:16 | Psalm 8:2 | κατηρτίσω αἶνον, "you have prepared praise", where the Hebrew has "you have founded strength" |
| Matthew 21:33; Mark 12:1 (borderline) | Isaiah 5:2 | φραγμὸν περιέθηκεν, "put a fence around", where the Hebrew verb means to dig about |
| Matthew 24:29; Mark 13:24-25 | Isaiah 13:10, 34:4 | the stars fall (πεσοῦνται), where the Hebrew has "all their host shall wither" |
| Matthew 24:30, 26:64 | Daniel 7:13 | ἐπὶ τῶν νεφελῶν, "upon the clouds", with the Old Greek; Theodotion and the Aramaic have "with" |
| Luke 4:18-19 | Isaiah 61:1 | τυφλοῖς ἀνάβλεψιν, "sight to the blind", where the Hebrew has the opening of the prison to those who are bound |
| Revelation 2:26-27 | Psalm 2:9 | ποιμανεῖ, "he will shepherd them", where the Masoretic pointing reads "break them"; the consonants bear either |

These differences are not all of one kind. Isaiah 29:13, Isaiah 6:10,
Psalm 8:2, Isaiah 61:1 and Isaiah 13:10 / 34:4 differ in sense. Genesis
2:24 and Deuteronomy 6:16 differ by a word or a number. Psalm 2:9 differs in
the vowels read with the same consonants. Daniel 7:13 is a choice between
two Greek versions.

### Hebrew against the Septuagint (5)

| Where | Source | The Hebrew's reading the New Testament has |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Matthew 10:35-36 | Micah 7:6 | "a man's enemies are the men of his house", without the Septuagint's "all"; a single word |
| Luke 10:15 (borderline) | Isaiah 14:15 | καταβιβασθήσῃ, "you will be brought down", the Hebrew's passive, in all three editions; Matthew 11:23 [varies](#the-edition-of-the-new-testament) |
| Matthew 11:29 (borderline) | Jeremiah 6:16 | ἀνάπαυσιν, "rest", where the Septuagint has ἁγνισμόν, "purification"; Aquila (ἀνάψυξιν) and Symmachus (ἠρεμίαν) have other words |
| John 13:18 | Psalm 41:9 | τὴν πτέρναν, "the heel", and one loaf of bread (τὸν ἄρτον), where the Septuagint has πτερνισμόν, "tripping up", and loaves |
| Revelation 3:7 (borderline) | Isaiah 22:22 | "he who opens and no one shuts", both halves of the Hebrew's saying, where B has only the second |

Three of the five are borderline, and Micah 7:6 turns on one word. The
strongest case is John 13:18, introduced with "that the Scripture might be
fulfilled". Its words are the Hebrew's sense in John's own Greek: none of
the Greek versions has them.

### Another Greek witness (14)

These follow a Greek witness other than Brenton's text and B. They fall
into three groups.

**The other witness has the Hebrew's sense (6).**

- Matthew 9:13 and 12:7, Hosea 6:6: καὶ οὐ θυσίαν, "and not sacrifice",
  with A and the first hand of Q and with the Hebrew's וְלֹא. B and
  Brenton read ἢ θυσίαν, "rather than sacrifice".
- Matthew 26:31 and Mark 14:27, Zechariah 13:7: τὸν ποιμένα, one shepherd,
  with A, Q, Γ, a corrector of ℵ and the Hebrew, where B has "the
  shepherds". διασκορπισθήσονται τὰ πρόβατα is A's, where B has
  ἐκσπάσατε. Matthew's wording is A's exactly, except that the verb is
  first person (πατάξω), where every witness has an imperative.
- Revelation 1:17 and 22:13, Isaiah 44:6 and 48:12: ὁ ἔσχατος, "the
  last", with the Hebrew's אַחֲרוֹן. Field gives "the others" (Aquila,
  Symmachus, Theodotion) ἐγὼ πρῶτος, καὶ ἐγὼ ἔσχατος at 48:12. The
  Septuagint has "after these things" (44:6) and "for ever" (48:12).

**Another Greek wording of the same Hebrew (5).**

- Matthew 24:21 and Mark 13:19, Daniel 12:1: θλῖψις οἵα οὐ γέγονεν,
  Theodotion's wording, where the Old Greek has οἵα οὐκ ἐγενήθη. Both
  render the Hebrew.
- Matthew 15:4 and Mark 7:10, Exodus 21:17: θανάτῳ τελευτάτω, the
  imperative of A and F, where B has the future τελευτήσει θανάτῳ.
- Mark 9:48, Isaiah 66:24: οὐ τελευτᾷ, the present of A, where B has the
  future τελευτήσει.

**Alexandrinus against both B and the Hebrew (3).**

- Matthew 4:10 and Luke 4:8, Deuteronomy 6:13: προσκυνήσεις, "you shall
  worship", and αὐτῷ μόνῳ, "him only", are A's. B and the Hebrew have
  "you shall fear" and no "only". Brenton's text has μόνῳ, as A does.
  Deuteronomy 10:20 has the same sentence, and there too B reads
  φοβηθήσῃ.
- Luke 23:30, Hosea 10:8: the mountains are told "fall on us" and the hills
  "cover us", A's order (with A's and Q's form πέσετε). B, Brenton and the
  Hebrew have "cover us" first.

[context] Codex Alexandrinus was copied in the fifth century, after the
Gospels were written. Where it agrees with the New Testament against B,
either its text was brought into line with the New Testament by Christian
copyists, or it keeps a Greek form older than itself that the evangelists
also knew. The texts by themselves do not decide which.

### Mixed (11)

These have the Septuagint at one point and the Hebrew or another witness
at another:

- **Matthew 11:10 and Luke 7:27**, Malachi 3:1 with Exodus 23:20. "Behold,
  I send my messenger before your face" is the Septuagint of Exodus 23:20
  word for word. κατασκευάσει τὴν ὁδόν, "he will prepare the way", has the
  Hebrew's sense of Malachi, where the Septuagint has ἐπιβλέψεται, "he
  will look upon". Field gives Aquila σχολάσει and Theodotion ἑτοιμάσει,
  both in the Hebrew's sense in other words. Symmachus survives only in
  Syriac, which modern editors have put back into Greek in different ways;
  no extant Greek witness has κατασκευάσει.
- **Matthew 16:27**, Psalm 62:12 with Proverbs 24:12 (borderline): the
  Septuagint's ἀποδώσει ἑκάστῳ κατά, with the Hebrew's singular "his
  deed" (τὴν πρᾶξιν).
- **Matthew 18:16**, Deuteronomy 19:15: "two witnesses or three" with
  the Hebrew's "or", where the Septuagint has "and"; σταθῇ, the passive of
  A and F; and πᾶν ῥῆμα, "every word", with the Septuagint.
- **Matthew 22:37 and Mark 12:29-30**, Deuteronomy 6:4-5. "With all your
  heart" (καρδίας) is the Hebrew's, and A's and F's, where B has διανοίας,
  "mind". Both Gospels then keep B's διανοία as a further term. Mark has
  four terms where the Hebrew has three, and its ἰσχύος, "strength", is
  not the Septuagint's word (δυνάμεως), nor a reading of Deuteronomy 6:5 in
  Swete's apparatus. Matthew uses the Hebrew's preposition, ἐν, and Mark the
  Septuagint's, ἐξ.
- **Mark 4:29**, Joel 3:13 (borderline): παρέστηκεν, the Septuagint's
  verb, with the Hebrew's "harvest" (θερισμός, where the Septuagint has
  "vintage") and one sickle.
- **Luke 22:37**, Isaiah 53:12: μετὰ ἀνόμων, "with the lawless", with
  the Hebrew's אֶת, where the Septuagint has ἐν τοῖς ἀνόμοις; the noun and
  the verb ἐλογίσθη are the Septuagint's, where Aquila and Symmachus have
  ἠριθμήθη.
- **Revelation 2:23**, Jeremiah 17:10 and 11:20 with Psalm 62:12
  (borderline): ἐραυνῶν, "who searches", with the Hebrew of Jeremiah 17:10,
  where the Septuagint has ἐτάζων; κατὰ τὰ ἔργα in the Septuagint's plural.
- **Revelation 3:19**, Proverbs 3:12 (borderline): ἐλέγχω with B, Brenton
  and the Hebrew; παιδεύω with ℵ and A, the reading Hebrews 12:6 quotes.
- **Revelation 22:12**, Isaiah 40:10 and 62:11 with Psalm 62:12
  (borderline): "my reward is with me", and "his work" in the Hebrew's
  singular.

### Independent of both (14)

- Matthew 5:33: a summary of the laws on oaths and vows, in no source's
  words.
- Mark 4:12, Isaiah 6:9-10: "lest they turn and it be forgiven them"
  (ἀφεθῇ αὐτοῖς), in neither text. [context] The Aramaic Targum of Isaiah
  also has "forgiven"; it is recorded as a parallel only, and nothing here
  rests on it.
- Mark 13:26 (ἐν νεφέλαις, "in clouds") and Luke 21:27 (ἐν νεφέλῃ, "in a
  cloud"), Daniel 7:13: neither preposition is in any witness.
- John 1:51, Genesis 28:12 (borderline): the participles ἀναβαίνοντας καὶ
  καταβαίνοντας are as the Hebrew's, but "upon the Son of Man" is in
  neither text.
- John 7:38 (borderline) and 8:17: paraphrases.
- The Father's 7 at the baptism and the transfiguration (all borderline).
  "My son" is Psalm 2:7's, and "hear him" Deuteronomy 18:15's made an
  imperative. εὐδόκησα shares its verb with Theodotion's Isaiah 42:1, ὃν
  ηὐδόκησεν ἡ ψυχή μου (Field), where the Septuagint has προσεδέξατο.
  Luke 9:35's ὁ ἐκλελεγμένος is Isaiah 42:1's "chosen" in another form. The
  sentences as wholes follow no text.

### Undetermined (22)

The Septuagint and the Hebrew agree, and the Greek is what any literal
rendering would give: the commandments of the Decalogue (Matthew 5:21, 5:27,
15:4, 19:18; Mark 7:10), "love your neighbour as yourself" (Leviticus 19:18;
Matthew 5:43, 19:19, 22:39; Mark 12:31), "my house shall be called a house
of prayer" (Isaiah 56:7; Matthew 21:13, Mark 11:17, Luke 19:46), "I am the
God of Abraham" (Exodus 3:6; Matthew 22:32, Mark 12:26, Luke 20:37), "blessed
is he who comes" (Psalm 118:26; Matthew 23:39, Luke 13:35), "I said, you are
gods" (Psalm 82:6; John 10:34), "they hated me without cause" (John 15:25),
and the shortened forms of Luke 4:4, Luke 8:10 and Luke 22:69.

Even here the wording is the Septuagint's wording, since the two agree.
What cannot be told is whether it was taken from the Septuagint or rendered
afresh [inferred].

### The Septuagint as printed: Brenton and Codex Vaticanus

Brenton's Greek text is not Codex Vaticanus. [context] It follows the
Sixtine edition (Rome, 1587), which printed B's text with corrections and
supplements from other manuscripts. Swete's text is B itself where B
survives. Where the two differ at a point a quotation turns on, the New
Testament goes sometimes with one and sometimes with the other:

| With Swete's text (B) against Brenton | With Brenton against B |
| --- | --- |
| Matthew 5:5: οἱ πραεῖς κληρονομήσουσι **τὴν** γῆν (Psalm 36:11 in B; Brenton and Göttingen lack the article, and so does the Hebrew) | Matthew 4:10 and Luke 4:8: αὐτῷ **μόνῳ** λατρεύσεις (Brenton and A; B lacks "only") |
| Matthew 15:4 (TR): Τίμα τὸν πατέρα σου καὶ τὴν μητέρα, without the second σου (Exodus 20:12 in B) | Mark 10:7-8: προσκολληθήσεται **πρὸς τὴν γυναῖκα** αὐτοῦ (Brenton, D, E and Göttingen; A, Swete's text in Genesis, has the dative) |
| Mark 10:19 (TR and Byz): adultery before murder (Deuteronomy 5 in B) | Matthew 19:18: murder, adultery, theft (Brenton's Deuteronomy, the Hebrew's order) |
| Mark 10:19 and Luke 18:20: Τίμα τὸν πατέρα σου καὶ τὴν μητέρα (Exodus 20:12 in B) | Mark 10:19 (SBL): murder before adultery (Brenton and the Hebrew) |
| Luke 18:20: adultery, murder, theft (Deuteronomy 5 in B) | Revelation 3:7: "the key of David" (Brenton, ℵ, A, the Three and the Hebrew; B has no key) |

Two of the Synoptic parallels part company here. At Genesis 2:24, Matthew
19:5 has the dative τῇ γυναικί, as A (Swete's text in Genesis) does, while
Mark 10:7 has πρὸς τὴν γυναῖκα, as Brenton and D and E do. In Mark 10:19
the SBL edition has the Hebrew's order of the commandments and the TR and
Byzantine text have B's.

Agreement in wording, measured over every word quoted, is nearly the same
for the two editions. Of the 114, 29 agree word for word in order with
Brenton and 33 with Swete; 34 agree with one or the other.

### Alexandrinus and the other uncials

A's readings, from Swete's apparatus, stand with the New Testament in 19
entries. Most are single words:

- the substantive cases above: Deuteronomy 6:13 (Matthew 4:10, Luke 4:8),
  Hosea 6:6 (Matthew 9:13, 12:7), Zechariah 13:7 (Matthew 26:31, Mark
  14:27), Hosea 10:8 (Luke 23:30), Exodus 21:17 (Matthew 15:4, Mark 7:10),
  Isaiah 66:24 (Mark 9:48), Isaiah 22:22 (Revelation 3:7), Proverbs 3:12
  (Revelation 3:19);
- "heart" in Deuteronomy 6:5 (Matthew 22:37, Mark 12:30), and the passive
  σταθήσεται in Deuteronomy 19:15 (Matthew 18:16), with F;
- the article before θεὸς Ἀβραάμ in Exodus 3:6 (Matthew 22:32);
- the omission of τῷ in Deuteronomy 8:3 (Matthew 4:4), with F;
- αὐτῶν after "their eyes" in Isaiah 6:10 (Matthew 13:15), with ℵ and Q;
- the dative of Genesis 2:24 (Matthew 19:5), where A is Swete's text.

ℵ stands with the New Testament in 3 entries (Matthew 13:14-15, Revelation
3:7, 3:19), Q in 6 and F in 6, all of them entries where A does too. In Matthew 13:14-15 the
New Testament follows the first hand of ℵ in the two small points where the

### Aquila, Symmachus and Theodotion

Field's Hexapla was checked wherever the New Testament departs from the
Septuagint toward the Hebrew:

Where the New Testament has the Hebrew's sense, the Three sometimes share
its words (Isaiah 48:12, 42:1) and sometimes give the same sense in other
words (Malachi 3:1, Jeremiah 6:16). [context] The Three are second-century
revisions, later than the New Testament; an agreement shows a shared
rendering of the Hebrew, not that either borrowed from the other.

### The Göttingen edition

The Göttingen readings that bear on these quotations are cited, not
copied:

- Psalm 36(37):11 at Matthew 5:5: Göttingen lacks τὴν, as Brenton does;
  Matthew has B's article.
- Genesis 2:24 at Matthew 19:5 and Mark 10:7: Göttingen reads πρὸς τὴν
  γυναῖκα, as Brenton and D and E do; Mark has it, and Matthew has A's
  dative.

### The edition of the New Testament

In three places the affinity itself depends on the edition:

- **Matthew 11:23**, Isaiah 14:15: the SBL text has καταβήσῃ, "you will go
  down", the Septuagint's verb; the TR and Byzantine text have
  καταβιβασθήσῃ, "you will be brought down", the Hebrew's passive. At Luke
  10:15 all three have the passive.
- **Mark 14:62**, Daniel 7:13: the SBL and TR have μετά, with Theodotion
  and the Aramaic; the Byzantine text has ἐπί, with the Old Greek and with
  Matthew 26:64.
- **Mark 10:19**, the commandments: the SBL text has murder before
  adultery, the Hebrew's order and Brenton's in Deuteronomy; the TR and
  Byzantine text have adultery first, B's order in Deuteronomy 5.

Elsewhere the editions differ in wording without changing the affinity:

- **The TR and Byzantine text nearer the Septuagint**: Matthew 22:44 and
  Mark 12:36, where they read ὑποπόδιον τῶν ποδῶν σου with Psalm 110:1 and
  the SBL text reads ὑποκάτω τῶν ποδῶν σου (a phrase found in the
  Septuagint of Psalm 8:7, ὑποκάτω τῶν ποδῶν αὐτοῦ [inferred]); Luke 23:46,
  where the TR has the Septuagint's future παραθήσομαι; Luke 4:4, where
  they continue with the second half of Deuteronomy 8:3; Luke 4:18, where
  they add the Septuagint's next clause, "to heal the broken-hearted";
  Matthew 15:8, where they add "this people draws near to me with their
  mouth"; Luke 7:27, where they have ἐγώ with Exodus 23:20; Matthew 17:5 and
  Mark 9:7, where they have Deuteronomy 18:15's order, αὐτοῦ ἀκούετε.
- **The SBL text nearer the Septuagint**: Matthew 4:4, ὁ ἄνθρωπος with
  the article; Matthew 9:13 and 12:7, the neuter ἔλεος; Matthew 13:15
  (against the TR), the future ἰάσομαι; John 6:45 (against the TR), θεοῦ
  without the article; John 13:18, ὁ τρώγων μου τὸν ἄρτον, "my bread", with
  the Septuagint's and the Hebrew's "my", where the TR and Byzantine text
  have μετʼ ἐμοῦ; Luke 9:35, ὁ ἐκλελεγμένος, near Isaiah 42:1's "chosen",
  where they have ὁ ἀγαπητός.
- **Only in the TR and Byzantine text**: Mark 9:44 and 9:46, which repeat
  Isaiah 66:24, and the formula at Mark 13:14, "spoken by Daniel the
  prophet".

Neither edition is consistently the nearer to the Septuagint.

### The Semitic cases

Matthew 27:46 and Mark 15:34 give the cry from the cross in Semitic letters
and then render it into Greek ("that is", "which is, being interpreted").
Ἠλὶ ἠλὶ λεμά (Matthew) match the Hebrew's אֵלִי אֵלִי לָמָה; σαβαχθάνι is
not the Hebrew's verb עֲזַבְתָּנִי. Mark has Ἐλωΐ. Each Greek rendering
lacks the Septuagint's πρόσχες μοι, "attend to me", which the Hebrew also
lacks; Mark has εἰς τί where the Septuagint has ἱνατί. This study reports
what the text gives, and builds nothing on the form of the cry.

### What the affinities show [inferred]

- The Greek of the New Testament stands, more often than not, with the
  Greek Old Testament: in the words it uses (43 of 114 with the Septuagint
  as printed, 14 more with another Greek witness), and in the readings
  where the Septuagint departs from the Hebrew (17 to 5). Isaiah
  29:13 ("in vain they worship me"), Isaiah 61:1 ("sight to the blind"),
  Psalm 8:2 ("praise") and Genesis 2:24 ("the two") are the clearest
  cases: Jesus' words have the Septuagint's sense, not only its wording.
- It does not stand with any one Greek text. It goes with B against
  Brenton and with Brenton against B, with A against both, with the Old
  Greek of Daniel and with Theodotion.
- It also has the Hebrew's sense in places, sometimes in the words of a
  Greek witness (Hosea 6:6 and Zechariah 13:7 with A; Isaiah 48:12 with
  the Three), and sometimes in words no Greek witness has (John 13:18;
  Matthew 11:29; Revelation 3:7).
- The quotations most often in the Septuagint's exact words are the
  formal ones, introduced as Scripture. Those woven into Jesus' own
  sentences, without a formula, are freer, and most of the independent and
  mixed cases are among them.

## The Old Testament

### What is counted

A place is counted when the LORD, in his own person or by a prophet's
"thus says the LORD", speaks words that repeat the wording of an earlier
book. Which book is earlier follows the books' own superscriptions and
order. [context] Where critical scholarship dates the books otherwise
(Isaiah 47 and 52 after Zephaniah and Nahum, for instance), the entry says
so, and the direction given here is that of the texts.

### The 52

| Quoting book | Entries |
| --- | --- |
| Jeremiah | 16 |
| Ezekiel | 9 |
| Zechariah | 4 |
| Joshua, Judges, Psalms | 3 each |
| 1 Samuel, 1 Kings, 2 Chronicles, Joel, Malachi | 2 each |
| Isaiah, Hosea, Nahum, Zephaniah | 1 each |

**Deuteronomy is the book God quotes most.** Of the 78 source passages
cited, 50 are in the Pentateuch: 20 in Deuteronomy, 12 in Leviticus, 11 in
Exodus, 6 in Numbers and 1 in Genesis. Isaiah supplies 8 and Jeremiah 6.

Some patterns:

- **"As I said to Moses."** In Joshua 1:3-9 and 20:2-3 the LORD gives
  Moses' words in Deuteronomy as his own, and names "this book of the law"
  (1:8). The Angel of the LORD in Judges 2:1-3 recalls the Sinai warnings
  as "I said".
- **Jeremiah and Deuteronomy.** Seven of Jeremiah's sixteen take up
  Deuteronomy, among them the law of divorce (3:1), the curse of the
  covenant (11:3),
  the Sabbath (17:22), the release of slaves (34:13-14), and the nations'
  question over the ruined land (22:8-9, from Deuteronomy 29:24-25). The
  same question stands in 1 Kings 9:8-9.
- **Jeremiah and Isaiah.** In the oracles on Moab and Babylon (Jeremiah 48
  and 50) the LORD takes up Isaiah's oracles on the same nations, phrase by
  phrase (Isaiah 13, 15-16, 24).
- **Ezekiel and Leviticus.** Ezekiel 20 repeats Leviticus 18:5, "which if
  a man does, he shall live by them", three times (20:11, 13, 21), and
  Ezekiel 34:25-28 and 37:26-27 take up the blessings of Leviticus 26.
- **The prophets quoting the prophets.** Jeremiah 14:10 gives Hosea 8:13
  word for word of Judah; Ezekiel 7:19 takes up Zephaniah 1:18; Joel 3:5
  (English 2:32) marks Obadiah 17 with "as the LORD has said"; Joel 4:10
  turns the ploughshares of Isaiah 2:4 and Micah 4:3 back into swords;
  Zechariah 1:4 quotes "the former prophets" in Jeremiah's words; Malachi
  3:23 (English 4:5) repeats Joel's "the great and terrible day of the
  LORD".
- **The LORD marks his own earlier word.** Jeremiah 33:14 calls the promise
  of the Branch "the good word that I spoke" and gives it again from 23:5-6.

By type: 11 direct, 31 adapted, 10 paraphrase. Twenty-three have a formula
that marks the words as said before ("as I said", "which I commanded", "I
swore", "as the LORD has said"). Two are borderline: 1 Samuel 2:30, where
no passage has the words the LORD says he "said", and Zechariah 13:9,
which recalls Hosea 2:25 in a phrase.

### Does the Greek keep the link?

| The Septuagint | Entries |
| --- | --- |
| keeps the verbal link between the two passages | 15 |
| keeps it in part | 31 |
| renders the two passages in different words | 1 |
| has no link to keep, because the Hebrew shares no wording either | 2 |
| lacks the quoting passage | 3 |

The Septuagint keeps the link, wholly or in part, in 46 of the 52. It
lacks Jeremiah 33:14-26 and 48:45-47, and so the three repetitions in them.
In 2 Chronicles 24:20 Zechariah son of Jehoiada gives Moses' question at
Kadesh (Numbers 14:41), and the Greek renders the two places differently.
1 Samuel 2:30 and Ezekiel 20:23 are paraphrases that share no wording with
their sources in the Hebrew either.

Where the link is partial, the Septuagint usually keeps the substance and
renders the same Hebrew in different Greek words in the two books. Jeremiah
7:23 repeats Leviticus 26:12 word for word in the Hebrew. The Septuagint has
ἔσομαι ὑμῖν εἰς θεόν in Jeremiah and ἔσομαι ὑμῶν θεός in Leviticus. So a
reader of the Greek sees the same promise, but not the same words
[inferred].

### The other repetitions

- **Within the Pentateuch (5)**: God restates his own earlier words, which
  at the time were spoken, not yet a book. Genesis 3:17 recalls 2:17;
  Genesis 26:3-5 recalls the oath of 22:16-18; Exodus 33:1 and Deuteronomy
  34:4 recall the promise of Genesis 12:7; Leviticus 20:24 recalls "a land
  flowing with milk and honey" (Exodus 3:8).
- **Within one book (9)**: an oracle repeated in the same book. Isaiah
  65:25 takes up 11:6-9. Jeremiah repeats 6:12-15 at 8:10-12, 16:14-15 at
  23:7-8, 23:19-20 at 30:23-24, 30:10-11 at 46:27-28, 6:22-24 against
  Babylon at 50:41-43, and the oracle on Edom (49:19-21) against Babylon at
  50:44-46. Ezekiel 33:11 takes up 18:23 and 18:32, and Psalm 108:8-10
  repeats the oracle of Psalm 60:8-10. The Septuagint lacks Jeremiah
  8:11-12 and 30:10-11, so it has those words only once.
- **Parallel oracles (5)**: Micah 4:1-3 and Isaiah 2:2-4; Obadiah 1-5 and
  Jeremiah 49:9, 14-16; Joel 4:16 and Amos 1:2; Joel 4:18 and Amos 9:13;
  Isaiah 41:18 and Psalm 107:35. In each the books do not show which came
  first.

### Appeals to Scripture that quote nothing

Jesus appeals to what is written without quoting it in Matthew 26:24 and
26:54; Mark 9:12-13, 14:21 and 14:49; Luke 18:31, 24:25-27 and 24:44-46;

wilderness) are not quotations.

### Speakers not counted

- **Revelation 21:3-4**, a loud voice from the throne, speaks of God in
  the third person.
- **Revelation 18:4**, "another voice from heaven", gives words of
  Jeremiah 51:45, a verse the Septuagint lacks; the speaker is not
  identified as God.
- **Angels** quote Scripture (Luke 1:17, Malachi 4:6), but the angel is
  not the Angel of the LORD who speaks as the LORD.
- **The Father's voice in John 12:28** ("I have glorified it, and will

- **The LORD's summaries of the prophets' message** within Jeremiah (7:25,

In Hebrews 2:12-13 and 10:5-7 the writer gives the words as the Son's, and
in Hebrews 3:7 and 10:15 as the Spirit's. Had these been counted, the Son
and the Spirit would each have quotations of their own outside the Gospels.
They are listed here because the speaker on the page is the writer of
Hebrews.
