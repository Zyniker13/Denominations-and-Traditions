# The Canon of Scripture in the Reformed Confessions and the Book of Concord

This study asks six questions of the confessional documents of the major
Reformed traditions and of the Lutheran Book of Concord:

1. Does the document define a canon?
2. If so, which books does it include or exclude?
3. How does it treat the Apocrypha?
4. How does it treat the Hebrew (and which Hebrew)?
5. How does it treat the Greek, and in particular the Septuagint (LXX)?
6. Is any of this, or any other reference to the canon, treated as
   foundational or binding?

Every claim below is tied to a chapter, article, section, or paragraph of the
document concerned. Quotations are from the public-domain English texts
listed under [Sources](#sources). Where a point rests on historical context
rather than on the text of a confession, it is marked **[context]**.

The short version:

- **Every Reformed tradition** that defines the canon defines the same one:
  the 39 books of the Hebrew Bible and the 27 books of the New Testament.
  They differ on the *use* of the Apocrypha, not on its exclusion from the
  canon. The scale runs from the Anglican and continental position (read in
  church for example and instruction, never for doctrine) to the Westminster
  position (no authority at all, no other use than "other human writings,"
  and barred from public reading).
- **Only one Reformed confession names the Septuagint**: the Helvetic
  Consensus of 1675, which names it in order to deny that it may be used to
  correct the Hebrew. Westminster, Savoy and the Second London Confession make
  the Hebrew Old Testament and Greek New Testament the "authentical" text but
  do not say *which* Hebrew text. The Helvetic Consensus does: the Hebrew
  "as handed down by the Hebrew Church," vowel points included.
- **The Book of Concord defines no canon at all.** It binds itself to "the
  prophetic and apostolic Scriptures of the Old and of the New Testament
  alone" as the sole rule and norm, but never says which books those are. It
  makes no statement on the Apocrypha, the Hebrew text, or the Septuagint.

## The Reformed confessions

### Continental: the Three Forms of Unity

#### Belgic Confession (1561)

**1. Defines a canon?** Yes. Article 4 ("Canonical Books of the Holy
Scripture") states that "the Holy Scriptures are contained in two books,
namely, the Old and New Testament, which are canonical, against which nothing
can be alleged." Article 5 makes the list closed: "We receive all these
books, and these only, as holy and canonical."

**2. Books included or excluded.** Article 4 lists the 39 books of the
Protestant Old Testament and the 27 books of the New Testament. Some details:

- Chronicles is "two books of the Chronicles, commonly called
  Paralipomenon", the Septuagint and Vulgate title.
- Ezra is "the first of Ezra", then Nehemiah, reflecting the Vulgate's
  1 and 2 Esdras. That is why the apocryphal Esdras books in Article 6 are
  numbered "third" (and, in some translations, "fourth").
- Lamentations is not named separately. It is understood under Jeremiah.
- Paul's epistles are counted as "fourteen," so Hebrews is attributed to
  Paul.

**3. Apocrypha.** Article 6 ("The difference between the canonical and
apocryphal books") names them: "the third book of Esdras, the books of
Tobias, Judith, Wisdom, Jesus Syrach, Baruch, the appendix to the book of
Esther, the Song of the three Children in the Furnace, the history of
Susannah, of Bell and the Dragon, the prayer of Manasses, and the two books of
the Maccabees." Its judgment has three parts:

- they may be read: "the Church may read and take instruction from" them;
- that permission is qualified: "so far as they agree with the canonical
  books";
- they have no doctrinal authority: "they are far from having such power and
  efficacy, as that we may from their testimony confirm any point of faith,
  or of the Christian religion; much less detract from the authority of the
  other sacred books."

*Textual note:* the 19th-century English translation in this repository
names only "the third book of Esdras." Other English translations name "the
third and fourth books of Esdras." The list is otherwise the same.

**4–5. Hebrew and Greek.** Neither is mentioned. Article 3 speaks of the
prophets and apostles committing the Word to writing, but nothing is said of
the languages, of a text form, or of the Septuagint. Indirectly, the Article 4
title "Paralipomenon" shows that the confession works within the
Latin and Greek naming tradition while limiting the canon to the contents of
the Hebrew one.

**6. Foundational or binding.** The doctrine of Scripture comes before the
doctrine of God in this confession: Articles 2–7 precede the Trinity
(Art. 8). Article 5 grounds the canon's authority not in the church's
reception ("not so much because the Church receives and approves them as
such") but in the witness of the Holy Spirit and the books' self-evidence.
Article 7 makes Scripture "this infallible rule" and puts no human writing,
custom, antiquity, or council on a level with it. Because the list in
Article 4 is closed by "these only" in Article 5, the canon list itself is
part of what the confession binds.

#### Heidelberg Catechism (1563)

The Catechism defines no canon and never mentions the Apocrypha, the
Hebrew, or the Greek. Its references to Scripture are general: true faith
holds "for truth all that God has revealed to us in his word" (Q. 21). The
gospel was "first revealed in Paradise," published by "the patriarchs and
prophets," and "fulfilled" in Christ (Q. 19). What a Christian must believe is
"all things promised us in the gospel," which "the articles of our catholic
undoubted christian faith briefly teach us" (Q. 22). Within the Three Forms of
Unity, the canon question is left to the Belgic Confession.

#### Canons of Dort (1619)

The Canons address the five Remonstrant points and say nothing about the
canon, the Apocrypha, or the languages. Their appeals are to "the Scriptures,
both of the Old and New Testament" (Head I, Art. 14) and to what is
"repugnant to Scripture" (e.g. Head II, Rejection of Errors 2).

### Continental: the French (Gallican) Confession (1559)

**1–2. Canon and list.** Article 3 states that "these Holy Scriptures are
comprised in the canonical books of the Old and New Testaments" and lists
them: the 39 Old Testament books (with "the first and second books of the
Chronicles, otherwise called Paralipomenon," "the first book of Ezra," and
Lamentations named separately) and the 27 New Testament books. Hebrews is
listed after "the Epistles of St. Paul," which are thirteen, so the confession
does not attribute Hebrews to Paul as the Belgic does.

**3. Apocrypha.** The Apocrypha are not named. Article 4 instead
distinguishes the canonical books "from other ecclesiastical books upon
which, however useful, we can not found any articles of faith." "Ecclesiastical books"
is the patristic term for books read in church but not canonical (Rufinus,
*Commentary on the Apostles' Creed*).

**4–5. Hebrew and Greek.** Not mentioned.

**6. Foundational or binding.** Article 4 grounds the canon in the testimony
of the Spirit: "We know these books to be canonical, and the sure rule of our
faith, not so much by the common accord and consent of the Church, as by the
testimony and inward illumination of the Holy Spirit." Article 5 makes the
Word in "these books" "the rule of all truth." Nothing may be added to or
taken from it, and all other authorities ("antiquity, or custom, or numbers
… or councils, or visions, or miracles") are to be "examined, regulated, and
reformed according to them." The three creeds are confessed "because they
are in accordance with the Word of God."

### Swiss: the Helvetic confessions and the Helvetic Consensus

#### First Helvetic Confession (1536)

Article 1 speaks of "the canonical or holy Scripture" (Wishart's 1537
English: "The canonycall or holy scrypture whiche is the worde of god")
as given by the Holy Spirit through the prophets and apostles and alone
containing all godliness. Article 5 speaks of "the scripture canonicall."
There is no list, no word on the Apocrypha, and no word on the languages. The
canon is presupposed, not defined.

#### Second Helvetic Confession (1566)

**1. Canon.** Chapter 1 opens by confessing "the canonical Scriptures of the
holy prophets and apostles of both Testaments" to be the true Word of God,
with authority "of themselves, not of men." No list is given.

**3. Apocrypha.** Chapter 1 has a paragraph on the Apocrypha. It reports that
"certain books of the Old Testament were by the ancient authors called
apocryphal, and by the others ecclesiastical," which some would have read in
the churches "but not advanced as an authority from which the faith is to be
established." It cites Augustine (*De Civitate Dei* 18.38). Like the Belgic
and Anglican texts, and unlike Westminster, the Second Helvetic allows that
these books are read in church.

**4–5. Hebrew and Greek.** Neither language is named. Chapter 2 grounds
right interpretation in the Scriptures themselves, including "the nature of
the language in which they were written," but does not specify a text. The
"holy Greek and Latin fathers" are honoured "as far as they agree with the
Scriptures" (ch. 2), a point about patristic authority rather than the text.

**6. Foundational or binding.** The doctrine of Scripture is chapter 1.
Fathers, councils, and traditions are subordinated to it (ch. 2).

*Note on the text:* the English Second Helvetic text in public circulation
online appears to be a modern translation. The phrases above are quoted
briefly for analysis. This repository has not yet acquired a public-domain
English text (see [SOURCES.md](../SOURCES.md)).

#### Helvetic Consensus (Formula Consensus Helvetica, 1675)

This is the only Reformed confession that answers questions 4 and 5
directly.

**4. Hebrew: which Hebrew.** Canon I affirms that God has preserved his Word
since it was written "so that it could not be corrupted by craft of Satan or
fraud of man." Canon II says which text is meant: "The Hebrew original of the
OT which we have received and to this day do retain as handed down by the
Hebrew Church" (citing Rom. 3:2, the "oracles of God") is inspired "not only
in its consonants, but in its vowels — either the vowel points themselves,
or at least the power of the points." Together with the original of the New
Testament it is "the sole and complete rule of our faith and practice," and
"all extant versions, eastern or western," are to be measured against it
"as to a Lydian stone." This is the Masoretic text as received, vowel
pointing included.

**5. Greek / LXX.** Canon III rejects the view of those who "remodel a Hebrew
reading which they consider unsuitable, and amend it from the versions of the
LXX and other Greek versions, the Samaritan Pentateuch, by the Chaldaic
Targums," and those who hold that the ancient versions witness to "other
Hebrew Originals" that differ from the received Hebrew text. The Septuagint is
named only to be denied any authority to correct the Hebrew.
**[context]** The target is Louis Cappel of Saumur, whose *Critica Sacra*
(1650) argued that the vowel points were late and that the ancient versions
could be used to correct the Hebrew.

**1–3, 6.** The Consensus presupposes rather than restates the canon and
says nothing on the Apocrypha. **[context]** It was imposed by subscription
in the Swiss Reformed churches from 1675, but subscription lapsed over the
following decades (Basel in 1686, Geneva in 1706, the remaining cantons by
the 1720s), so its binding force was regional and temporary.

*Copyright note:* the only English translation in this research's source set
(Klauber, 1990) is under copyright, so it is quoted only briefly here. The
Latin original is in the public domain.

### Scottish: the Scots Confession (1560)

**1–3. Canon and Apocrypha.** The Scots Confession gives no list. Its
definition is by reference to the ancient church, in chapter 18 ("Of the
Notes by Which the True Kirk is Discerned from the False"): "the doctrine
taught in our kirks is contained in the written Word of God, to wit, in the
books of the New and Old Testaments. In those books we mean which of the
ancient have been reputed canonical." This excludes the Apocrypha by
implication, without naming them, and makes the ancient church's judgment the
criterion of the canon's extent.

**4–5. Hebrew and Greek.** Not mentioned.

**6. Foundational or binding.** Chapter 19 ("The Authority of the
Scriptures") affirms that Scripture is sufficient and its authority is "of
God and neither to depend on men nor angels." It calls "blasphemous" the claim
that Scripture has "no authority but that which it receives from the kirk."
Chapter 20 subjects general councils to "the plain Word of God." There is a
tension here: the canon's *extent* is identified by what "the ancient" reputed
canonical (ch. 18), while its *authority* is said not to depend on the church
(ch. 19).

### Anglican: the Thirty-Nine Articles and the Irish Articles

#### Thirty-Nine Articles (1571)

**1. Canon.** Article 6 ("Of the Sufficiency of the holy Scriptures for
salvation") defines Scripture by a criterion: "In the name of the holy
Scripture we do understand those Canonical Books of the Old and New
Testament, of whose authority was never any doubt in the Church."

**2. Books.** Under the heading "Of the Names and Number of the Canonical
Books," the Article lists the Old Testament books, using Vulgate names for
Ezra and Nehemiah ("The First Book of Esdras," "The Second Book of Esdras")
and counting "Four Prophets the greater, Twelve Prophets the less." The New
Testament is not listed: "All the Books of the New Testament, as they are
commonly received, we do receive, and account them Canonical." The Old
Testament is thus defined by the "never any doubt" criterion, while the New
Testament (some of whose books were once disputed) is received "as commonly
received."

**3. Apocrypha.** The Article names them and gives them a defined
ecclesiastical use: "And the other Books (as Hierome saith) the Church doth
read for example of life and instruction of manners; but yet doth it not
apply them to establish any doctrine." It lists "The Third Book of Esdras,
The Fourth Book of Esdras, The Book of Tobias, The Book of Judith, The rest of
the Book of Esther, The Book of Wisdom, Jesus the Son of Sirach, Baruch the
Prophet, The Song of the Three Children, The Story of Susanna, Of Bel and the
Dragon, The Prayer of Manasses, The First Book of Maccabees, The Second Book
of Maccabees." The appeal to Jerome ("Hierome") matters: Jerome is the
patristic champion of the Hebrew canon (*hebraica veritas*), and his preface
to the books of Solomon is the classic source of the distinction between
books read "for the edification of the people" and books that confirm
doctrine. **[context]** The Book of Common Prayer
lectionary does appoint lessons from the Apocrypha, and the Homilies endorsed
by Article 35 quote from them, consistent with "the Church doth read" them.

**4–5. Hebrew and Greek.** The Articles say nothing about the original
text of the Old Testament or about the Septuagint. The Greek New Testament is
quoted once, in Article 9 (Original Sin): "the lust of the flesh, called in
the Greek, φρονημα σαρκος." Article 24 requires worship "in a tongue
understanded of the people," a point about the vernacular, not the text.

**6. Foundational or binding.** Article 6 makes Scripture the test of what is
"required of any man, that it should be believed as an article of the
Faith." Article 8 receives the three creeds "for they may be proved by most
certain warrants of holy Scripture." Article 20 makes the Church "a witness
and a keeper of holy Writ" which may not "ordain any thing that is contrary to
God's Word written." Article 21 holds that councils "may err." **[context]**
Clerical subscription to the Articles was required by the Subscription Act of
1571 and Canon 36 of 1604.

#### Irish Articles (1615)

The Irish Articles, drafted by James Ussher, are a link between the
Thirty-Nine Articles and Westminster. Westminster chapter 1 follows their
order closely.

- **Canon (Arts. 1–2).** Article 1: "The ground of our religion and the rule
  of faith and all saving truth is the Word of God, contained in the holy
  Scripture." Article 2 lists "all the Canonical Books of the Old and New
  Testament," with the New Testament listed in full, unlike the Thirty-Nine
  Articles. It says of them: "All which we acknowledge to be given by the
  inspiration of God, and in that regard to be of most certain credit and
  highest authority."
- **Apocrypha (Art. 3).** "The other Books, commonly called Apocryphal, did
  not proceed from such inspiration, and therefore are not of sufficient
  authority to establish any point of doctrine; but the Church doth read them
  as Books containing many worthy things for example of life and instruction
  of manners." This is the first confessional statement that grounds the
  Apocrypha's exclusion explicitly in *lack of inspiration*. Westminster 1.3
  picks up that rationale. The list adds "Baruch, with the Epistle of
  Jeremiah" (Schaff's printing misprints Sirach as "Sarah").
- **Hebrew and Greek (Art. 4).** "The Scriptures ought to be translated out of
  the original tongues into all languages for the common use of all men."
  The languages are not named. The Septuagint is not mentioned.

*Source:* Schaff, *Creeds of Christendom*, vol. 3 (public domain), via CCEL.
The Irish Articles are not yet in this repository.

### British Presbyterian: the Westminster Standards

#### Westminster Confession of Faith (1646)

The fullest Reformed treatment of the canon is in chapter 1, "Of the Holy
Scripture."

**1–2. Canon and list.** WCF 1.2: "Under the name of holy Scripture, or the
Word of God written, are now contained all the books of the Old and New
Testaments, which are these." It lists all 39 Old Testament books
(Lamentations named separately) and all 27 New Testament books. Hebrews is
listed after "Paul's Epistles" as "The Epistle to the Hebrews," without
attributing it to Paul. Then: "All which are given by inspiration of God, to
be the rule of faith and life."

**3. Apocrypha.** WCF 1.3 is the strictest Reformed statement: "The books
commonly called Apocrypha, not being of divine inspiration, are no part of
the Canon of the Scripture; and therefore are of no authority in the Church
of God, nor to be any otherwise approved, or made use of, than other human
writings." Three things set it apart:

- it does not list the Apocrypha;
- it gives no ecclesiastical use beyond that of "other human writings," and
  so withholds the "example of life" reading that the Anglican and continental
  texts allow;
- its proof texts tie the canon to the Jewish Scriptures: Luke 24:27, 44 ("the
  law of Moses, and in the prophets, and in the psalms"), Rom. 3:2 ("unto them
  were committed the oracles of God"), and 2 Pet. 1:21.

The *Directory for the Publick Worship of God* (1645), "Of Publick Reading of
the Holy Scriptures," puts this into practice: "All the canonical books of the
Old and New Testament (but none of those which are commonly called
*Apocrypha*) shall be publickly read in the vulgar tongue, out of the best
allowed translation."

**4–5. Hebrew and Greek.** WCF 1.8 is the central text:

> The Old Testament in Hebrew (which was the native language of the people
> of God of old), and the New Testament in Greek (which at the time of the
> writing of it was most generally known to the nations), being immediately
> inspired by God, and by his singular care and providence kept pure in all
> ages, are therefore authentical; so as in all controversies of religion the
> Church is finally to appeal unto them.

The section then requires translation "into the vulgar language of every
nation."

- *Which Hebrew?* The Confession does not say. It names a language, not a
  manuscript tradition. It says nothing of vowel points, Masoretes, or
  textual variants, and it does not mention the Aramaic portions of Daniel
  and Ezra. Its claim that the text was "kept pure in all ages" was
  understood by the divines of the period to refer to the Hebrew text as
  received **[context]**. The explicit identification of that text with the pointed
  Masoretic text came in the Helvetic Consensus (1675), thirty years later.
- *The Greek.* The Greek in view is the Greek New Testament. The Septuagint
  is not mentioned. By making the Hebrew Old Testament the "authentical"
  text, WCF 1.8 excludes by implication any final appeal to the LXX (or the
  Vulgate) for the Old Testament.
- **[context]** "Authentical" answers the Council of Trent, which in its
  Fourth Session (1546) declared the Vulgate "authentic" for public use, in
  the same session that defined a canon including Tobit, Judith, Wisdom,
  Sirach, Baruch, 1–2 Maccabees, and the Greek additions to Esther and
  Daniel.

The *Form of Presbyterial Church-Government* puts the original languages
into the ordination of ministers: a candidate "shall be examined touching his
skill in the original tongues, and his trial to be made by reading the Hebrew
and Greek Testaments."

**6. Foundational or binding.** The doctrine of Scripture is chapter 1 and
precedes the doctrine of God. WCF 1.4–1.5 grounds the authority of Scripture
"wholly upon God," with "full persuasion" coming "from the inward work of the
Holy Spirit." WCF 1.6 makes Scripture complete ("unto which nothing at any
time is to be added"). WCF 1.10 makes "the Holy Spirit speaking in the
Scripture" the "Supreme Judge" of all controversies, councils, and ancient
writers. Because 1.2 is an explicit list and 1.3 an explicit exclusion, the
extent of the canon is itself confessional doctrine for those who subscribe.
**[context]** The Church of Scotland adopted the Confession in 1647. The
American revision of 1788 changed chapters 20, 23, and 31 and left chapter 1
untouched.

#### Larger and Shorter Catechisms (1647)

Both catechisms assume chapter 1 of the Confession and do not repeat the
list:

- WLC Q. 3: "The holy scriptures of the Old and New Testaments are the word
  of God, the only rule of faith and obedience."
- WLC Q. 4 sets out the internal evidences and the witness of the Spirit.
- WSC Q. 2: "The Word of God, which is contained in the Scriptures of the Old
  and New Testaments, is the only rule to direct us how we may glorify and
  enjoy him."

### Congregational and Baptist: Savoy (1658) and the Second London Confession (1677/1689)

Both documents take over Westminster chapter 1.

- **Savoy Declaration** (Congregational): chapter 1, sections 2, 3 and 8 are
  identical to WCF 1.2, 1.3, and 1.8, covering the list, the Apocrypha as "no
  part of the Canon," and the Hebrew and Greek as "authentical."
- **Second London Baptist Confession** (Particular Baptist): 1.2 and 1.8
  follow Westminster. In 1.3 the Apocrypha are "no part of the canon *or
  rule* of the Scripture," a gloss that defines canon as rule. The
  Confession also adds an opening sentence at 1.1 that puts the doctrine
  first: "The Holy Scripture is the only sufficient, certain, and infallible
  rule of all saving knowledge, faith, and obedience."

## The Lutheran Book of Concord (1580)

The text cited here is the *Concordia Triglotta* English translation (Bente
and Dau, 1921, public domain), from the copy in this repository at
[confessions/lutheran/book-of-concord/](../confessions/lutheran/book-of-concord/README.md).
Paragraph numbers are the Triglotta's, shown there as superscripts. Where the Triglotta numbers the Apology's articles differently from
modern editions, both are given (e.g. "Ap. V (III)").

**1. Does it define a canon?** No. No document in the Book of Concord lists
the books of Scripture, uses "canon" of a list of books, or rules on disputed
books. The Formula of Concord's statement on Scripture is the "Rule and Norm"
section that opens both the Epitome and the Solid Declaration. It names the rule without enumerating it:

- [Epitome, Rule and Norm](../confessions/lutheran/book-of-concord/formula-of-concord-epitome.md#summary-content-rule-and-norm) 1: "the sole rule and standard according to which
  all dogmas together with [all] teachers should be estimated and judged are
  the prophetic and apostolic Scriptures of the Old and of the New Testament
  alone."
- [Solid Declaration, Rule and Norm](../confessions/lutheran/book-of-concord/formula-of-concord-solid-declaration.md#comprehensive-summary-foundation-rule-and-norm) 3: "the Prophetic and Apostolic Scriptures
  of the Old and New Testaments as the pure, clear fountain of Israel, which
  is the only true standard by which all teachers and doctrines are to be
  judged."

The phrase "Canonical Scriptures" occurs once in the whole Book of Concord, in
a quotation of Augustine: bishops are not to be followed if they "hold
anything contrary to the Canonical Scriptures of God"
([Augsburg Confession XXVIII](../confessions/lutheran/book-of-concord/augsburg-confession.md#article-xxviii-of-ecclesiastical-power) 28). The term is used, but the canon
is never set out.

**2. Books included or excluded.** None are specified. In practice the
confessions treat the disputed New Testament books as Scripture. The Apology
deals at length with James 2:24 and concludes that "James does not contradict us" ([Ap. V (III)](../confessions/lutheran/book-of-concord/apology-of-the-augsburg-confession.md#art-v-iii-of-love-and-the-fulfilling-of-the-law) 127) and
"James does not contradict St. Paul" (130), interpreting the epistle rather
than questioning it. **[context]** Luther's critical prefaces on James,
Hebrews, Jude, and Revelation, and Chemnitz's distinction between
*homologoumena* and *antilegomena* (*Examen*, 1565–73), are not part of the
Book of Concord and are not confessionally binding.

**3. Apocrypha.** The Book of Concord makes no statement about the Apocrypha
as a class. Its handling of particular apocryphal texts shows two attitudes:

- **Tobit is quoted and interpreted, not dismissed.** Answering the
  Confutation's appeal to Tob. 4:11 ("Alms free from every sin and from
  death"), the Apology does not deny the book's authority. It interprets the verse so as not to "detract from the
  praise of Christ." Alms free from sin and death "not *ex opere operato*" but
  as "exercises of faith." It argues from Tobit's own context (Tob. 4:5, 4:19)
  that "faith is required before alms" ([Ap. V (III)](../confessions/lutheran/book-of-concord/apology-of-the-augsburg-confession.md#art-v-iii-of-love-and-the-fulfilling-of-the-law) 156–158).
- **2 Maccabees is set apart from "the Scriptures."** On the invocation of
  saints, the Apology concedes that the saints in heaven pray for the Church
  in general, "albeit no testimony concerning the praying of the dead is
  extant in the Scriptures, except the dream taken from the Second Book of
  Maccabees, 15:14" ([Ap. XXI (IX)](../confessions/lutheran/book-of-concord/apology-of-the-augsburg-confession.md#art-xxi-ix-of-the-invocation-of-saints) 9). The sentence can be read two ways:
  2 Maccabees as the single scriptural exception, or (more naturally, given
  "the dream taken from") a non-scriptural exception. Either way the Apology
  bases nothing on it, and goes on to say that for invocation of saints
  "neither a command, nor a promise, nor an example can be produced from the
  Scriptures" (Ap. XXI (IX) 10).
- The Smalcald Articles state the principle that governs both cases: "The
  rule is: The Word of God shall establish articles of faith, and no one
  else, not even an angel" ([SA II.ii](../confessions/lutheran/book-of-concord/smalcald-articles.md#article-ii-of-the-mass) 15).

So the Lutheran confessions take an *operative* position on the Apocrypha
(no article of faith is built on them, and where cited they are read in
harmony with the Gospel) but no *defined* position. They do not deny the
Apocrypha inspiration, as Westminster and the Irish Articles do, and they do
not name them, as the Belgic and the Thirty-Nine Articles do.

**4. Hebrew.** The Book of Concord makes no statement on the Hebrew text, on
which text is authoritative, or on the Vulgate's status. It works with the
Hebrew exegetically:

- On Dan. 4:24 [27], it rejects the Vulgate's "Redeem thy sins by alms" and
  says that "the words of Daniel speak in his own language still more clearly
  of repentance." It also faults Jerome for adding "a particle expressing
  doubt" ([Ap. V (III)](../confessions/lutheran/book-of-concord/apology-of-the-augsburg-confession.md#art-v-iii-of-love-and-the-fulfilling-of-the-law) 140–143). The passage is in fact Aramaic, which the
  Apology does not name.
- It explains *asham* in Isa. 53:10 as "a victim for transgression"
  ([Ap. XXIV (XII)](../confessions/lutheran/book-of-concord/apology-of-the-augsburg-confession.md#art-xxiv-xii-of-the-mass) 23).
- It discusses the claimed Hebrew etymology of *missa* from Deut. 16:10
  ([Ap. XXIV (XII)](../confessions/lutheran/book-of-concord/apology-of-the-augsburg-confession.md#art-xxiv-xii-of-the-mass) 85–87).
- It derives "holy day" (*Feiertag*) "from the Hebrew word sabbath," which
  "properly signifies to rest" ([Large Catechism, Third Commandment](../confessions/lutheran/book-of-concord/large-catechism.md#3rd-commandment) 79).

These are arguments from the original language against the Vulgate, not a
confessional doctrine of the text.

**5. Greek / LXX.** The Septuagint is never mentioned. Greek appears only as
New Testament and patristic philology: *latreia* ([Ap. IV (II)](../confessions/lutheran/book-of-concord/apology-of-the-augsburg-confession.md#art-iv-ii-of-justification) 49), and
*leitourgia*, argued from 2 Cor. 9:12 and Phil. 2:25 to mean "public
ministry" rather than sacrifice ([Ap. XXIV (XII)](../confessions/lutheran/book-of-concord/apology-of-the-augsburg-confession.md#art-xxiv-xii-of-the-mass) 79–83). There is also the
"Greek canon," meaning the Eastern eucharistic liturgy, not a list of books
(Ap. XXIV (XII) 88, 93).

**6. Foundational or binding.** This is where the Lutheran confessions are
most explicit, though the subject is the *authority* of Scripture rather
than its *extent*.

- Scripture alone is the norm: "the Holy Scriptures alone remain the only
  judge, rule, and standard, according to which, as the only test-stone, all
  dogmas shall and must be discerned and judged" ([FC Ep., Rule and Norm](../confessions/lutheran/book-of-concord/formula-of-concord-epitome.md#summary-content-rule-and-norm) 7).
- All other writings are subordinate. They "must not be regarded as equal to
  the Holy Scriptures" and are received only "as witnesses" (Ep. 2). The
  creeds and confessions "are not judges, as are the Holy Scriptures," but
  "only a testimony and declaration of the faith" (Ep. 8). The Solid
  Declaration repeats this ([SD Rule and Norm](../confessions/lutheran/book-of-concord/formula-of-concord-solid-declaration.md#comprehensive-summary-foundation-rule-and-norm) 9–10), and receives the Augsburg
  Confession, Apology, Smalcald Articles, and Catechisms because they are
  "taken from God's Word" (SD Rule and Norm 4–8).
- **[context]** Later Lutheran dogmatics describe this as Scripture the
  *norma normans* and the confessions the *norma normata*. The terms are not
  in the Book of Concord, but the distinction is (Ep. 7–8).

The result is that a Lutheran subscription binds to "the prophetic and
apostolic Scriptures" as sole norm, but the *boundaries* of that corpus are
not confessionally fixed. They are inherited, not defined.

## Comparative observations

**1. Defining the canon is a Reformed act, not a Lutheran one.** The
Reformed confessions written after Trent's Fourth Session (1546) mostly answer
it with a list: Gallican 3 (1559), Belgic 4 (1561), Thirty-Nine Articles 6
(1571), Irish 2 (1615), Westminster 1.2 (1646). The earlier Swiss and Scottish
texts (First Helvetic 1536, Scots 1560) presuppose a canon without listing
it. The Book of Concord, completed in 1580 after Trent, still declines to list
one.

**2. The Reformed agree on the canon and differ on the Apocrypha's use.**
There are three tiers:

| Position | Documents |
| --- | --- |
| Read in church for example and instruction; never to establish doctrine | Thirty-Nine Articles 6; Irish Articles 3; Second Helvetic 1 |
| May be read and learned from *so far as they agree with* the canonical books; cannot confirm any point of faith | Belgic 6; cf. Gallican 4 ("however useful") |
| No authority; no use beyond "other human writings"; not to be read in public worship | Westminster 1.3 and the *Directory*; Savoy 1.3; Second London 1.3 |

The grounds also become stricter over time. The earlier texts rest the
distinction on the church's ancient practice (Jerome, Augustine, "the
ancient"). The Irish Articles (Art. 3) and Westminster (1.3) rest it on a
doctrinal claim: the Apocrypha are "not of divine inspiration."

**3. The Hebrew canon is the governing principle, even where unstated.**
Every Reformed list has exactly the contents of the Hebrew Bible. WCF 1.3's
proof text (Rom. 3:2: "unto them were committed the oracles of God") and
Helvetic Consensus II (citing the same verse for the Hebrew text "as handed
down by the Hebrew Church") make the link explicit: the canon of the Old
Testament is what was entrusted to Israel. Yet the lists follow the
Greek and Latin *order and naming* (Paralipomenon, 1–2 Esdras, the Christian
fourfold order). They do not follow the Hebrew Bible's Law, Prophets, and
Writings. The confessions take the Hebrew *contents* in a
Septuagint-and-Vulgate *arrangement*.

**4. "Which Hebrew" is answered only once.** Westminster, Savoy, and the
Second London Confession say "in Hebrew" and "kept pure in all ages" without
naming a text. Only the Helvetic Consensus (Canons II–III) identifies the
received Masoretic text with its vowel points and rejects correction from the
LXX, the Samaritan Pentateuch, or the Targums. Its binding force was
regional and short-lived.

**5. The LXX is almost entirely absent.** Apart from Helvetic Consensus III,
no Reformed or Lutheran confession names the Septuagint. None addresses the
New Testament's use of the Septuagint in its Old Testament quotations. The
LXX's influence shows only indirectly: in the book names the lists keep, and
in the fact that the books excluded as "apocrypha" are, broadly, the books the
Greek and Latin Bibles contain beyond the Hebrew. Some of them (3–4 Esdras,
the Prayer of Manasseh) were not accepted as canonical even by Trent, but
Belgic 6 and Thirty-Nine Articles 6 list them for completeness.

**6. Binding force.** The Reformed confessions that list the canon bind the
list itself: Belgic 5's "these only," WCF 1.2–1.3, and Thirty-Nine Articles 6.
Subscription to these confessions (Dort's Formula of Subscription for the
Three Forms, Westminster for Presbyterians, the Articles for Anglican clergy)
therefore includes the extent of the canon. The Book of Concord binds just as
firmly to Scripture as the sole "rule and norm," and places that commitment
at the head of the Formula of Concord. Its subscribers, however, are not
committed to any list. In every tradition examined, Scripture's authority is
foundational and comes first: it stands at or near the head of the
Belgic (Arts. 2–7), Gallican (Arts. 2–5), Second Helvetic (ch. 1), Irish
(Arts. 1–7), Westminster, Savoy, and Second London (ch. 1) confessions, and in
the opening "Rule and Norm" of the Formula of Concord. Every document grounds that authority in God and the
witness of the Spirit rather than in the church's reception.
